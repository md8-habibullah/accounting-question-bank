# Chapter 1: Introduction to accounting

## 1.1 Start here: the idea in plain English

Imagine **Rahim** runs a tea stall. Every evening he writes in a notebook what he sold, what he bought, what he still owes the milk supplier and what a regular customer still owes him. That notebook is **accounting**: **recording** what happened, **analysing** it (sorting it into groups) and **summarising** it so that a reader can understand the business at a glance. At the end of the year he can answer two simple questions. *Did I make a profit?* And *what do I own and what do I owe?*

Other people want those answers too. A bank wants to know if Rahim can repay a loan for a second stall. The tax office (NBR in Bangladesh) wants the profit so that it can work out the tax. His milk supplier wants to know that he will pay. A business (an **entity**) therefore prepares **financial statements**, and the people who rely on them are called **users**. In the IFRS **Conceptual Framework** the *primary users* are **existing and potential investors, lenders and other creditors**. They are "primary" because they cannot walk in and ask for a special report. They must rely on the general purpose statements that the entity publishes. Managers are different: they can ask for any internal report, so they are mainly the people who **prepare** the statements.

Not every business is built the same way. Rahim alone is a **sole trader**. Two or more people who share the risks and rewards form a **partnership**. A **limited liability company** is a separate legal "person" owned by shareholders. If Rahim's stall fails with big debts, creditors can claim from his own savings, because in law he and the stall are one. If a company fails, its shareholders lose only what they paid (or agreed to pay) for their shares. That protection is called **limited liability**.

Because many people trust these statements, there are rules. IFRS Accounting Standards and the Conceptual Framework decide what is recorded and how it is shown. This chapter gives you the foundations that the rest of the book builds on: the *qualities* good information must have, the basic *concepts* (going concern, accruals, materiality), the difference between *capital* and *revenue* spending, *ethics*, and *sustainability* reporting. In this book's study plan these topics carry about 15% of the exam marks.

| Word | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Entity** | Any business or organisation that prepares accounts |
| **Financial statements** | The yearly summary reports: financial position, profit or loss, changes in equity, cash flows, and notes |
| **Primary users** | Existing and potential investors, lenders and other creditors |
| **Stakeholder** | Anyone with an interest in the entity (owners, staff, bank, customers, tax office, public) |
| **Stewardship** | How well managers look after the resources the owners have trusted to them |
| **Limited liability** | Owners of a company risk only the amount they paid (or must pay) for their shares |
| **Capital expenditure** | Spending that buys or improves a long-lasting (non-current) asset |
| **Revenue expenditure** | Day-to-day trading costs, or spending that only keeps an asset working |
| **Going concern** | The assumption that the business will carry on for the foreseeable future |
| **Materiality** | Whether leaving out or misstating an item could change users' decisions |
| **ESG** | Environmental, social and governance matters (the heart of sustainability reporting) |

> **KEY FACT** The objective of general purpose financial reporting is to give information about the entity that is **useful to existing and potential investors, lenders and other creditors in making decisions about providing resources to the entity**.

## 1.2 Key facts to remember

- **What accounting is**
    - A way of **recording, analysing and summarising** the transactions of an entity (record: Chapter 3; analyse: Chapter 4; summarise: Chapter 5). An accountant measures revenue, expenditure and, for a business, **profit**.
    - Charities, clubs and government bodies also prepare financial statements.
- **Types of business entity** (table in 1.3)
    - Sole traders (always) and partners (usually) are **personally responsible** for business debts. Shareholders are responsible only for the amount to be paid for their shares (**limited liability**).
    - A sole trader is **not legally separate** from the business. A company is.
    - Limited liability companies must **prepare and publish** financial statements every year under the **Companies Act 1994**. Sole traders and general partnerships need not publish. A **listed** company has shares traded on a stock exchange.
    - Partnerships are not studied further in this exam.
- **Users and their needs** (table in 1.3)
    - **Primary users:** existing and potential **investors, lenders and other creditors**.
    - They need to assess the entity's **economic resources**, the **claims** against it (liabilities) and **changes** in both, and how well **management** has used the resources (**stewardship**). The **timing and certainty of cash** decide whether it can pay staff, suppliers, interest and loans.
    - **Managers/directors** prepare the statements and have internal information, so they are not the typical user. The board of directors is responsible for the statements.
    - The statements cannot meet every need. This "**information gap**" is usually larger in large companies.
    - Internal reports are tailored to managers. The **annual report** is for external users. Special reports exist for one stakeholder, for example tax information for NBR or a cash flow forecast for a bank.
- **Regulation of accounting**
    - Sources of GAAP: the **Companies Act**, local and international **standards**, **stock exchange** requirements, local **custom and practice**.
    - The **IASB** issues IFRS Accounting Standards (older ones are IASs, with the **same status**). **ICAB** adopts them in Bangladesh. The **Conceptual Framework** sets out the concepts behind the standards.
    - **True and fair view / fair presentation:** not defined, decided in courts on the facts. Under IAS 1 it means **faithful representation** of the effects of transactions. Applying IFRS Accounting Standards is **presumed** to give fair presentation.
    - Entities must **select and apply accounting policies** that give relevant, reliable, comparable and understandable information, with extra disclosure where needed. Compliance with IFRS is **stated in a note**.
    - A wrong accounting treatment **cannot be put right by disclosure** or notes. A rare, justified departure from a standard must be disclosed with its reason and effect.
- **Objective and components of financial statements (IAS 1)**
    - To give information on **financial position, performance and cash flows** useful to a wide range of users making economic decisions; to show the results of management's **stewardship**; and to help users **predict future cash flows** (timing and certainty).
    - A complete set: **statement of financial position (SFP)**; **statement of profit or loss and other comprehensive income** (it may be a separate **SPL**); **statement of changes in equity**; **statement of cash flows**; **notes**; and, in certain cases, an extra SFP from the start of the earliest comparative period.
    - **SFP:** a **snapshot at a date** of assets, liabilities and equity. It shows financial structure, liquidity (short-term cash) and solvency (long-term funds).
    - **SPL:** **income and expenses over a period**, giving a profit or a loss. It shows performance and helps predict future returns.
    - The statement of cash flows shows **actual cash** in and out. With the statement of changes in equity, it links the SFP and the SPL.
    - IAS 1 allows other titles ("balance sheet", "cash flow statement").
- **The elements** (full detail in Chapter 2)
    - **Asset:** a present economic resource controlled by the entity as a result of past events.
    - **Liability:** a present obligation to transfer an economic resource as a result of past events.
    - **Equity:** the residual interest in the assets after deducting all liabilities.
    - **Income:** increases in assets or decreases in liabilities that increase equity, other than money put in by owners.
    - **Expenses:** decreases in assets or increases in liabilities that reduce equity, other than money taken out by owners.
    - **Recognition:** an item goes into the statements only if it meets the definition of an element and recognising it gives useful (relevant, faithfully represented) information.
    - So **capital introduced is not income** and **drawings are not an expense**.
- **Capital and revenue items** (table in 1.3)
    - **Capital expenditure:** buys, or improves the earning capacity of, **non-current assets** (kept more than one year). It goes in the **SFP**; a **depreciation** charge goes to the SPL over the asset's life.
    - Costs of bringing an asset to its place and condition (legal fees, duties, carriage, installation) are **capital**. Repairs, maintenance and staff costs for an asset are **revenue**.
    - **Revenue expenditure:** **trade** costs (goods for resale, wages, selling, administration, finance costs) or costs that **maintain** an asset's earning capacity. Charged to the SPL of the period it relates to.
    - **Capital income:** proceeds from selling non-current assets. The **profit or loss on sale** goes in the SPL. **Revenue income:** sales, services, interest and dividends received.
    - **Capital transactions** (owner's money in; loans received or repaid) **never go through the SPL**. **Drawings** are not an expense.
- **Qualitative characteristics** (table in 1.3)
    - **Fundamental:** **relevance** and **faithful representation**. Information needs **both**.
    - **Relevance** = predictive and/or confirmatory value. **Materiality** is an entity-specific aspect of it. Undue delay can reduce it.
    - **Faithful representation** = **complete, neutral, free from error**. "Free from error" does **not** mean perfectly accurate.
    - **Prudence** supports neutrality: assets and income are not overstated, liabilities and expenses not understated. It does **not** allow deliberate understatement.
    - **Enhancing:** **comparability, verifiability, timeliness, understandability**. **Comparability** is the goal; **consistency** is a **means** of reaching it.
- **Concepts and conventions (IAS 1 and the Conceptual Framework)**
    - **Going concern:** the entity will continue for the foreseeable future (at least the next **12 months**), with no intention or need to liquidate or stop trading. If not appropriate, say so, state the basis and the reasons. Disclose any significant **uncertainty**.
    - **Break-up basis** (not a going concern): assets at forced-sale value (net realisable value); no non-current assets or liabilities; all liabilities due within 12 months.
    - **Accruals basis** (matching): record income and expenses when **earned or incurred**, not when cash moves. (The statement of cash flows is the exception.) The **cash basis** records only cash received and paid.
    - **Consistency:** keep presentation and classification the same from period to period, unless operations change significantly, a better presentation exists, or a standard requires a change.
    - **Materiality:** an item is material if omitting, misstating or obscuring it could influence users' decisions. It depends on **size and nature**, with no absolute measure. A rule of thumb is **5% of net profit**. Some items (for example **directors' remuneration**) are material by nature. Immaterial items are combined, and a specific disclosure need not be given if immaterial.
    - **Offsetting:** assets and liabilities, and income and expenses, are shown **separately**, not netted, unless a standard allows it.
    - **Business entity:** the business is treated as separate from its owner, even a sole trader.
    - **Historical cost:** items are recorded at what the business paid. It is **objective and verifiable**. In rising prices it tends to **understate assets and overstate profit**.
    - **Judgement** is needed for many figures (buildings, research and development, brands, reputation), so ethics and standards limit it.
- **Ethics (ICAB Code of Ethics, based on the IESBA Code)**
    - **Five fundamental principles:** **integrity, objectivity, professional competence and due care, confidentiality, professional behaviour** (table in 1.3). Independence and courtesy are **not** on the list.
    - The Code applies to **members, students, affiliates, employees of member firms and member firms**, in **paid and unpaid** activities, and can reach private life (for example as trustee of a charity).
    - It is **principles-based**: follow the **spirit as well as the letter**. Advantages: flexible, no box-ticking or loopholes, fits each case, copes with change. Drawback: some may interpret principles in their own favour.
    - Good dilemma questions: would colleagues think my solution reasonable, have I considered all consequences, could I defend it in public. "Does it help my career?" is **not** one.
    - Background: threats include **self-interest, self-review, advocacy, familiarity and intimidation**. Remove the threat, apply safeguards, or step away. **IESBA** writes the international code; **IFAC** is the global body for the profession.
- **Sustainability**
    - Meeting the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet theirs. It covers **environmental, social and governance (ESG)** issues, not only climate. Accountants help **measure, analyse and report** it.
    - Earlier guidance (for example TCFD, GRI) was mostly voluntary, so disclosures were inconsistent.
    - The **ISSB** was formed by the **IFRS Foundation in 2021**. Its **IFRS Sustainability Disclosure Standards** (first two issued June 2023, for periods from 1 January 2024) **complement** IFRS Accounting Standards, serve **investors' needs**, and began with **climate**.
    - Two aspects: **impacts** (the entity on the environment and society) and **dependencies** (ESG factors on the entity's ability to maintain value).
    - The ISSB has **no authority to make companies apply** its standards. Local law and regulators decide.

## 1.3 Formulas and layouts

> **FORMULA** Assets = Liabilities + Equity

> **FORMULA** Equity = Assets − Liabilities

> **FORMULA** Profit for the period = Income − Expenses

> **FORMULA** Closing equity (sole trader) = Opening equity + Profit − Drawings + Capital introduced

> **FORMULA** Cost of a non-current asset (capital expenditure) = Purchase price + Delivery + Installation + Legal fees and duties paid by the buyer

> **FORMULA** Cost of sales = Units sold × Cost per unit

> **FORMULA** Closing inventory at cost = Units unsold × Cost per unit

> **FORMULA** Closing inventory on a break-up basis = Units unsold × Net realisable value (forced sale price) per unit

> **FORMULA** Rule of thumb for materiality: an item is material if it is more than 5% of net profit (size), or if it is sensitive by nature

## 1.4 Worked examples

### Example 1: Capital or revenue? (and the effect on profit and the SFP)

**Sabina** runs a tailoring shop. During 20X1 she has these items.

| Item | £ | Class | Where it goes |
|---|---:|---|---|
| (a) Industrial sewing machine (price) | 18,000 | Capital expenditure | SFP, non-current asset |
| (b) Delivery of the machine to her shop | 450 | Capital expenditure | Added to the machine's cost |
| (c) Installation of the machine | 950 | Capital expenditure | Added to the machine's cost |
| (d) Repair of her older machine | 380 | Revenue expenditure | SPL |
| (e) Cloth bought and all used in the year | 5,200 | Revenue expenditure | SPL (cost of sales) |
| (f) Cash she took for household bills | 600 | Drawings | Reduces equity, not an expense |
| (g) Old machine sold for £700 (shown in the SFP at £500) | 700 | Capital income | Profit of £200 goes to SPL |

1. Cost of the new machine = 18,000 + 450 + 950 = **£19,400** (delivery and installation are needed to get it ready for use).
2. Revenue expenditure charged to the SPL = 380 + 5,200 = **£5,580**.
3. Depreciation (for illustration, 10% a year on cost) = 19,400 × 10% = £1,940 in the SPL. The machine is in the SFP at 19,400 − 1,940 = £17,460.
4. Profit on selling the old machine = 700 − 500 = **£200**, included in the SPL.

*What if the £1,400 delivery and installation had been wrongly treated as expenses?* Profit would be **understated by £1,400** and non-current assets **understated by £1,400** (before any depreciation effect).

*Easy way to see it:* if you buy a rickshaw, the price plus the cost of getting it ready to drive is the cost of the rickshaw. A puncture repair is only a running cost.

**Capital expenditure £19,400; revenue expenditure £5,580.**

### Example 2: Matching costs to sales, and what changes if the business is not a going concern

**Nilufar** buys 60 school bags at £14 each on credit (£840, not yet paid). She sells 45 bags at £22 each for cash. At the year-end 15 bags are unsold.

| Statement of profit or loss | £ |
|---|---:|
| Revenue (45 × 22) | 990 |
| Cost of sales (45 × 14) | (630) |
| **Profit** | **360** |

| Statement of financial position | £ |
|---|---:|
| Inventory (15 × 14, at cost) | 210 |
| Cash | 990 |
| **Total assets** | **1,200** |
| Equity (the profit) | 360 |
| Trade payables | 840 |
| **Total equity and liabilities** | **1,200** |

*Why only £630 as an expense?* The whole £840 is revenue expenditure, but only the bags **sold** match the sales. The cost of the other 15 bags (£210) waits in the SFP and will be matched with next period's sales. This works because the business is a **going concern**: there is a next period.

*Now suppose Nilufar decides to stop trading* and the 15 bags can only fetch £8 each in a forced sale. On a break-up basis: inventory = 15 × 8 = **£120**. Profit falls by 210 − 120 = 90 to **£270**. Total assets = 120 + 990 = 1,110, equal to equity 270 + payables 840 = 1,110.

**Profit £360 (going concern) or £270 (break-up basis).**

### Example 3: The accounting equation and how the statements link

**Imran's Grocery** is a sole trader. At 31 December 20X1: equipment £12,000, inventory £6,500, trade receivables £1,800, cash £4,200, bank loan £8,000, trade payables £3,300. Equity at the start of the year was £8,900. Profit for the year was £10,500 and Imran took drawings of £6,200.

1. Total assets = 12,000 + 6,500 + 1,800 + 4,200 = **£24,500**.
2. Total liabilities = 8,000 + 3,300 = **£11,300**.
3. Equity = Assets − Liabilities = 24,500 − 11,300 = **£13,200**.
4. Check with the equity roll-forward: 8,900 + 10,500 − 6,200 = **£13,200**. The two methods agree.

| Statement of financial position at 31 December 20X1 | £ |
|---|---:|
| Equipment | 12,000 |
| Inventory | 6,500 |
| Trade receivables | 1,800 |
| Cash | 4,200 |
| **Total assets** | **24,500** |
| Equity | 13,200 |
| Bank loan | 8,000 |
| Trade payables | 3,300 |
| **Total equity and liabilities** | **24,500** |

*Easy way to see it:* the SFP is a photograph on 31 December. The SPL is a film of the whole year. The profit from the film is added to the owner's equity in the photograph.

### Example 4: Which characteristic or concept is being used?

| Situation | Answer | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Two competing garment makers show revenue and expenses under the same headings, so a reader can put them side by side | **Comparability** (enhancing) | Valid comparisons between entities |
| An auditor traces a figure to the supplier's invoice and a physical count | **Verifiability** (enhancing) | Others can check that it is a fair depiction |
| The annual report is published 14 months after the year-end | **Timeliness** (enhancing) | Too late to influence decisions |
| The notes use clear headings, short sentences and tables | **Understandability** (enhancing) | Clear and concise |
| A forecast helps investors estimate next year's profit | **Relevance** (predictive value) | Fundamental characteristic |
| Management leaves out a large loss to please the bank | Not **faithful representation** | Not complete and not neutral |
| A £7 stapler is treated as an expense, not an asset | **Materiality** | Too small to affect decisions |
| The same inventory valuation method is used every year | **Consistency** | A means of achieving comparability |

*A materiality check with numbers.* Net profit is £120,000. Using the 5% rule of thumb, 120,000 × 5% = **£6,000**. A £7,200 error is above the line (probably material). A £900 error in stationery is below it (probably not). A £900 error in **directors' remuneration** might still be material, because that item is sensitive by nature.

### Example 5: Which ethical principle is at stake?

| Situation | Principle |
|---|---|
| A trainee tells a friend, over tea, that a client is about to close a factory (not yet public) | **Confidentiality** |
| An accountant reviews the accounts of a company owned by his brother and goes easy on it because of the family tie | **Objectivity** (a familiarity threat) |
| A finance manager records a sale that has not happened, to hit a bonus target | **Integrity** |
| An accountant is convicted of tax evasion in his private business | **Professional behaviour** (breaking the law, discrediting the profession) |
| A newly qualified accountant takes on IFRS work he has never studied and does not tell the client | **Professional competence and due care** |

*Easy way to see it:* honest, unbiased, skilled, silent about what is private, well-behaved. That is integrity, objectivity, competence and due care, confidentiality, professional behaviour.

## 1.5 Exam traps

1. **"Managers are the main users of the statements."** No. The **primary users** are existing and potential **investors, lenders and other creditors**. Managers mainly **prepare** the statements and have internal information. Other stakeholders (staff, NBR, public) are interested, but they are not the primary users.
2. **Big does not mean capital.** Buying £50,000 of goods for resale is **revenue** expenditure. A £400 delivery charge on a new machine is **capital**. The test is what the spending buys: a lasting asset, or this year's trading.
3. **Same words, different treatment.** Delivery of a new machine **to you** is capital. Delivery of goods **to customers** is a selling cost (revenue). A repair is revenue; an extension is capital.
4. **Sale of a non-current asset.** The **proceeds** are capital income, not sales revenue. Only the **profit or loss on sale** appears in the SPL. And **drawings** and **capital introduced** never touch the SPL.
5. **Mixing up the characteristics.** **Materiality** belongs under **relevance**. **Completeness, neutrality and freedom from error** belong under **faithful representation**. **Prudence** supports neutrality. **Consistency** is a means, not a characteristic. "Reliability" and "consistency" are not the names of the characteristics in the Conceptual Framework.
6. **Prudence and "free from error".** Prudence does **not** mean deliberately understating assets or income. "Free from error" does **not** mean perfectly accurate: it means no material errors in the description or the process.
7. **Going concern is not "profitable" or "can pay all debts".** It means there is no intention or need to liquidate or stop trading for the foreseeable future. When it fails, assets are valued at forced-sale (break-up) amounts and non-current assets and liabilities disappear. Do not confuse **fair presentation** with "accurate". Fair presentation means a faithful representation. A wrong treatment cannot be fixed by a note.
8. **Ethics and sustainability slips.** Independence and courtesy are **not** the five fundamental principles. The Code applies to **unpaid** activities too. For sustainability, the ISSB standards **complement** (not replace) IFRS Accounting Standards, the ISSB **cannot force** companies to use them, and sustainability means **environmental, social and governance** matters, not just climate.

## 1.8 Answers and explanations

### Answers: extra practice questions

**Q1. Answer: B (£22,000)**

- **Why:** The price, delivery and installation are all needed to get the oven ready for use, so they are capital expenditure. The repair of an existing mixer only keeps an old asset working, and flour is a trading cost: both are revenue expenditure.
- **Formula:** 20,000 + 500 + 1,500 = **£22,000**.
- **Easy example:** When you buy a rickshaw, the price plus fitting the seat cover you needed is the rickshaw's cost. Mending a puncture later is just a running cost.
- **Why not the others:** A (£20,000) leaves out delivery and installation. C (£22,700) wrongly adds the repair. D (£26,900) adds everything, including the flour.

**Q2. Answer: A (£490)**

- **Why:** The business is **not** a going concern, so the break-up basis applies: assets are valued at what they would fetch in a forced sale (net realisable value), not at cost.
- **Formula:** 14 unsold × £35 = **£490**.
- **Easy example:** In a closing-down sale you value your leftover stock at the price you will really get, not at what you paid.
- **Why not the others:** B (£840) is 14 × £60, the cost, which is the going concern value. C (£2,160) is 36 × £60, the cost of the tyres already sold. D (£3,000) is the total purchases.

**Q3. Answer: B and E**

- **Why:** Shareholders (owners) look at profit, dividends and risk to their investment. A bank lender wants to know that interest and repayments will be paid when due.
- **Easy example:** Rahim's brother who put money into the stall wants to know about profit. The bank that lent him money wants to know about repayments.
- **Why not the others:** A is wrong because employees care about stability and the ability to pay wages, not the tax bill. C is wrong because suppliers want to know whether they will be paid, not the selling prices. D is wrong because the tax authority wants the profits to assess tax; the dividend decision is the owners' concern.

**Q4. Answer: B and D**

- **Why:** The 5% line is a rule of thumb, and some items are material by nature.
- **Formula:** 84,000 × 5% = **£4,200**. B (£4,300) is above £4,200, so it is material by size. D is directors' remuneration, a sensitive item, so even £300 can be material by nature.
- **Easy example:** Losing a £300 note from the till is minor, but a £300 mistake in the boss's own pay would raise eyebrows.
- **Why not the others:** A (£150) is far below £4,200 and not sensitive. C (£3,900) is below £4,200 and a heading error is not sensitive by nature.

**Q5. Answer: A, C and E**

- **Why:** Faithful representation means the information is **complete, neutral and free from error**.
- **Easy example:** A report that leaves out a big loss (not complete), tilts the picture to please someone (not neutral) or has a counting mistake (not free from error) does not show the real position.
- **Why not the others:** B (materiality) is an entity-specific aspect of **relevance**. D (predictive value) is also part of **relevance**.

**Q6. Answer: D (Confidentiality)**

- **Why:** Confidentiality means not disclosing information learned through work without proper authority, and not using it for the advantage of yourself or others. Delta's private prices and discount terms were passed to a rival.
- **Easy example:** A doctor must not tell a stranger what a patient said in the clinic.
- **Why not the others:** A (integrity) is about being honest and straightforward in general; here the specific duty is confidentiality. B (objectivity) concerns bias and conflicts in judgement. C (competence and due care) concerns knowledge and skill.

**Q7. Answer: C (£570)**

- **Why:** On the accruals basis, revenue is recorded when earned (all 95 scarves, paid or not) and matched with the cost of **only the 95 sold**. The 25 unsold scarves stay in inventory.
- **Formula:** Revenue 95 × 15 = 1,425. Cost of sales 95 × 9 = 855. Profit = 1,425 − 855 = **£570**.
- **Easy example:** Selling on credit is still selling. Stock still on the shelf has not been "used up".
- **Why not the others:** A (£45) is cash received (900) less cost of sales (855), mixing the cash and accruals ideas. B (£345) deducts all 120 scarves (1,080) from the sales and forgets the closing inventory. D (£900) is the cash received, which is the cash basis.

**Q8. Answer: A and E**

- **Why:** The ISSB was formed by the IFRS Foundation in 2021. Sustainability reporting has two sides: **impacts** (the entity on the world) and **dependencies** (the world's ESG factors on the entity's ability to maintain value).
- **Easy example:** A factory's pollution is an impact on the town. A drought that dries up its water supply is a dependency on the environment.
- **Why not the others:** B is wrong because the new standards **complement** IFRS Accounting Standards. C is wrong because sustainability also covers social and governance matters. D is wrong because the ISSB has no authority to mandate use; local law and regulators decide.

| | £ |
|---|---:|
| Net cash from trading | 1,000 |
