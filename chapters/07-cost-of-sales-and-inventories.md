# Chapter 7: Cost of sales and inventories

## 7.1 Start here: the idea in plain English

Imagine you run a small grocery shop. On 1 January you have **100 packets of biscuits** on the shelf. During the year you buy **900 more**. On 31 December you count **150 packets** still on the shelf. How many did you sell? 100 + 900 − 150 = **850 packets**. What you paid for those 850 packets is your **cost of sales**. The 150 packets left on the shelf are not a cost of this year. You have paid for them and you will sell them next year, so on 31 December they are an **asset** in the statement of financial position (balance sheet). The asset is called **inventories** (many people say "stock").

That is why the statement of profit or loss works out *opening inventory + purchases − closing inventory*. It matches each sale with the cost of the goods that were really sold (the matching idea, also called the accruals basis). Revenue minus cost of sales is the **gross profit**.

Two practical problems follow. First, **what are the left-over goods worth?** If the biscuits are stale and you can only sell them at half price, it is not honest to show them at full cost. The rule (IAS 2) is to show inventory at the **lower of cost and net realisable value (NRV)**. Second, **which packets did you sell?** If you bought at £1.00, then £1.10, then £1.20, which price belongs to the packets still on the shelf? Two methods are allowed: **FIFO** (the oldest goods are sold first) and **AVCO** (all the goods are mixed together at an average price).

| Word | Plain meaning | Where it goes |
|---|---|---|
| **Inventories** | Goods held to sell (or materials to make them) | Current asset in the statement of financial position |
| **Cost of sales** | Cost of the goods sold in the year | Statement of profit or loss, just under revenue |
| **Gross profit** | Revenue − cost of sales | Statement of profit or loss |
| **Delivery inwards** (carriage inwards) | You pay to bring goods **to** your business | Added to purchases, so part of cost of sales |
| **Delivery outwards** (carriage outwards) | You pay to send goods **to your customer** | A distribution cost, **below** gross profit |
| **Net realisable value (NRV)** | Selling price minus the costs still to be spent to finish and sell the item | Used to test the value of inventory |
| **FIFO** | First in, first out | A way to put a cost on goods sold and goods left |
| **AVCO** | Average cost (weighted average) | A second way to put a cost on goods sold and goods left |
| **Mark-up** | Profit as a percentage of **cost** | Used to find cost or selling price |
| **Margin** | Profit as a percentage of **selling price** | Used to find cost or selling price |
| **Drawings** | The owner takes cash or goods for personal use | Reduces the owner's capital, not an expense |

> **KEY FACT** Cost of sales is **not** "what we bought". It is the cost of the goods we **sold**. Goods bought but not yet sold wait in inventory and become a cost only in the year they are sold.

## 7.2 Key facts to remember

- **Inventories (IAS 2)** are assets that are: held for sale in the ordinary course of business; in the process of production for sale; or materials and supplies to be used in production or in giving a service. Examples: goods bought for resale, finished goods, work in progress (part-finished goods), raw materials. A van that a car dealer holds **for resale** is inventory (a current asset), not a non-current asset.
- **Cost of sales (trader)** = opening inventory + purchases + delivery inwards − closing inventory. Purchases are the **net** figure: take off returns outwards (goods sent back to suppliers) and trade discounts (and early settlement discounts the business expects to take).
- **Delivery inwards** is part of the cost of getting goods ready to sell, so it goes **into** cost of sales. **Delivery outwards** is a selling and distribution cost, so it is deducted **after** gross profit.
- **Gross profit** = revenue − cost of sales. Opening inventory is last year's closing inventory. Closing inventory is **deducted** in the cost of sales sum (so profit goes up) and shown as a current asset.
- **Double entry at the year-end** (the workbook method, three journals):
    - Opening inventory: Dr Cost of sales, Cr Inventory.
    - Purchases: Dr Cost of sales, Cr Purchases.
    - Closing inventory: Dr Inventory, Cr Cost of sales.
    - What is left on the cost of sales account moves to the statement of profit or loss.
- **Service businesses** (accountants, designers, trainers) have no goods to count. Their cost of sales is the costs that are **directly linked** to the work for clients: direct labour (the hours spent on client work, at the firm's hourly **cost**, not the price charged), sales commission paid for winning a contract, and materials used (for example printing for a client). Office rent, the receptionist's salary, the payroll manager's salary, advertising and other general overheads are **not** cost of sales.
- **Cost of inventory** includes everything needed to bring the item to its present location and condition: purchase price, import duties and other non-recoverable taxes, delivery inwards and handling, and (for made goods) conversion costs (direct labour and a fair share of **production** overheads). It does **not** include selling costs, delivery outwards, administration and general overheads, or storage of finished goods.
- **Lower of cost and NRV (IAS 2).** NRV = expected selling price − costs to complete (repair, modification, repackaging) − costs to make the sale (commission, packing, delivery to the customer). Compare **item by item** (or line by line), never in total. Selling price and replacement cost are **not** used as the value of inventory. Estimating cost as selling price less a normal profit margin is accepted as a shortcut in retail.
- **Writing inventory down.** Goods that are lost, stolen, damaged or obsolete are reduced to their NRV (nil if worthless). You do this simply by putting the **lower value** into closing inventory; cost of sales then automatically includes the loss.
- **Costing methods.** IAS 2 allows **FIFO** and **AVCO**. **LIFO** (last in, first out) is **not** allowed. The workbook uses the **cumulative weighted average**: a new average cost per unit is worked out **after every purchase**; goods sold leave at the current average and do not change it.
- **FIFO v AVCO.** Over the whole life of the goods the total cost is the same. In one year they split it differently between cost of sales and closing inventory. **Rising prices:** FIFO gives a **higher closing inventory, lower cost of sales and higher profit** than AVCO. **Falling prices:** the other way round. **Cash is the same** under both: the method changes the accounting figures, not the money paid or received.
- **Inventory counts.** The purpose of a count is to check the **quantities** really held (and to find loss, theft, damage and errors) before they are valued. A **periodic** count is done at one date (normally the year-end); cost of sales is then found by the formula. A **continuous** (perpetual) system keeps running records of goods in and goods out, and counts a few lines each day or week to check the records.
- **Count on a different date.** If the count is not on the year-end date, adjust the counted value to the year-end (see the formulas in 7.3).
- **Stolen or destroyed inventory (material amount).** Take the cost **out of purchases** (Cr Purchases) and show it as an expense (Dr administrative expenses), so that gross profit is not distorted. An insurance claim is recorded **only if reasonably certain**: Dr Other receivables, Cr **Other income** (not revenue, and not cost of sales).
- **Inventory drawings.** If the owner takes goods for personal use, record them at **cost**: Dr Drawings, Cr Cost of sales (some books credit Purchases; the effect is the same). You do not change opening or closing inventory.
- **Mark-up** is profit ÷ **cost**. **Margin** is profit ÷ **selling price**. Both can be used to find an unknown cost, selling price or closing inventory.
- **Inventory errors.** Closing inventory too **high** makes that year's profit too **high** and next year's profit too **low** (it is next year's opening inventory). The two-year total is unchanged.
- **Sale or return.** Goods sent to a customer who may still send them back are, until the customer accepts them, still the seller's inventory and not yet a sale.

## 7.3 Formulas and layouts

> **FORMULA** Cost of sales = Opening inventory + Purchases − Returns outwards + Delivery inwards − Closing inventory

> **FORMULA** Gross profit = Revenue − Cost of sales

> **FORMULA** Gross profit margin % = Gross profit ÷ Revenue × 100

> **FORMULA** Mark-up % = Gross profit ÷ Cost of sales × 100

> **FORMULA** Goods available for sale = Opening inventory + Purchases − Returns outwards + Delivery inwards = Cost of sales + Closing inventory

> **FORMULA** NRV per unit = Expected selling price − Costs to complete or repair − Selling costs

> **FORMULA** Inventory value = Units × (Lower of cost per unit and NRV per unit), worked out line by line

> **FORMULA** AVCO cost per unit = Total cost of all units in inventory ÷ Number of units in inventory (new figure after every purchase)

> **FORMULA** Cost from selling price: with mark-up, Cost = Selling price × 100 ÷ (100 + mark-up %); with margin, Cost = Selling price × (100 − margin %) ÷ 100

> **FORMULA** Margin % = Mark-up % ÷ (100 + Mark-up %) × 100, and Mark-up % = Margin % ÷ (100 − Margin %) × 100

> **FORMULA** Count AFTER the year-end: Inventory at year-end = Count value − Purchases received after year-end + Cost of goods sold after year-end + Goods returned to suppliers after year-end − Goods returned by customers after year-end

> **FORMULA** Count BEFORE the year-end: Inventory at year-end = Count value + Purchases received up to year-end − Cost of goods sold up to year-end

> **FORMULA** Service business cost of sales = Hours incurred × Hourly cost of each person + Commission for winning the work + Materials used

**The statement of profit or loss layout for a trader.**

| Line | Plus or minus | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | + | Sales for the year |
| Opening inventory | + | Last year's closing inventory |
| Purchases | + | After taking off returns outwards and discounts |

| Closing inventory | − | Lower of cost and NRV |

|---|---:|---|---:|

**Journals you must know** (figures are examples).

| Event | Debit | Credit | £ |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Opening inventory moved to cost of sales | Cost of sales | Inventory | 6,400 |
| Purchases moved to cost of sales | Cost of sales | Purchases | 52,000 |
| Closing inventory recorded (at lower of cost and NRV) | Inventory | Cost of sales | 7,300 |
| Inventory costing £9,000 stolen | Administrative expenses | Purchases | 9,000 |
| Insurer agrees to pay 60% of £9,000 | Other receivables | Other income | 5,400 |
| Owner takes goods costing £300 for own use | Drawings | Cost of sales | 300 |

If the stolen goods were **not** insured, only the first stolen-goods journal is made, and the full £9,000 is a cost. With the claim, the net cost is 9,000 − 5,400 = £3,600.

**Receipts and issues table (for FIFO and AVCO).** Use one row for each movement and carry a running balance.

| Date | Goods in (units, £) | Goods out (units, £) | Balance (units, £) | Unit cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Each movement | cost of the new delivery | cost of the goods sold (FIFO: oldest layer first; AVCO: current average) | what is left | AVCO: new average after each purchase |

**Mark-up and margin side by side** (cost £60).

| £ | Margin 25% (on sales) | Mark-up 25% (on cost) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Sales | 80 | 75 |
| Cost of sales | (60) | (60) |
| Gross profit | 20 | 15 |
| Gross profit ÷ sales | 25.0% | 20.0% |
| Gross profit ÷ cost | 33.3% | 25.0% |

Quick conversions: mark-up 25% = margin 20%; mark-up 50% = margin 33.3%; mark-up 100% = margin 50%; margin 25% = mark-up 33.3%; margin 40% = mark-up 66.7%.

**Cost of sales for a service firm** (example: a client job in progress at the year-end; the hourly cost is what the person costs the firm, not the price charged to the client).

| Person | Hourly cost (£) | Hours incurred | Labour cost (£) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Director | 90 | 2 | 180 |
| Senior consultant | 45 | 10 | 450 |
| Junior consultant | 20 | 16 | 320 |
| **Total** | | | **950** |

Only the hours **actually incurred** are used. The budgeted hours do not matter.

> **REMEMBER** Closing inventory is a **credit** in the cost of sales calculation (it reduces cost of sales) and a **debit** balance in the statement of financial position (an asset).

## 7.4 Worked examples

### Example 1: Cost of sales and gross profit in statement layout

Rubel runs a mobile accessories shop. For the year ended 31 December 20X1: revenue £84,000; opening inventory £6,400; purchases £52,000; returns outwards £1,500; delivery inwards £800; delivery outwards £1,100; rent £9,000; closing inventory £7,300.

| Statement of profit or loss, year ended 31 December 20X1 | £ | £ |
|---|---:|---:|
| Revenue | | 84,000 |
| Opening inventory | 6,400 | |
| Purchases | 52,000 | |
| Less: returns outwards | (1,500) | |
| Add: delivery inwards | 800 | |
| | 57,700 | |
| Less: closing inventory | (7,300) | |
| Cost of sales | | (50,400) |
| **Gross profit** | | **33,600** |
| Delivery outwards | | (1,100) |
| Rent | | (9,000) |
| **Net profit** | | **23,500** |

Gross profit margin = 33,600 ÷ 84,000 = **40.0%**. Mark-up = 33,600 ÷ 50,400 = **66.7%**.

*Easy way to see it:* Rubel had £57,700 of goods available to sell. £7,300 was still on the shelf, so only £50,400 was sold. The £1,100 paid to deliver goods to customers is a selling cost, so it sits **below** gross profit.

### Example 2: FIFO and AVCO with a receipts and issues table

Sabina sells packs of tea at £8 each. In May 20X2 she had these movements.

| Date | Movement | Units | Unit cost (£) |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| 1 May | Opening inventory | 60 | 4.00 |
| 4 May | Purchase | 140 | 4.50 |
| 9 May | Sale | 120 | |
| 16 May | Purchase | 120 | 5.00 |
| 23 May | Sale | 150 | |
| 28 May | Purchase | 50 | 5.40 |

**FIFO** (the oldest layer leaves first).

| Date | Movement | Cost of the movement (£) | Inventory after the movement | Value (£) |
|---|---|---:|---|---:|
| 1 May | Opening | 240 | 60 at 4.00 | 240 |
| 4 May | Buy 140 at 4.50 | 630 | 60 at 4.00; 140 at 4.50 | 870 |
| 9 May | Sell 120 | (510) | 80 at 4.50 | 360 |
| 16 May | Buy 120 at 5.00 | 600 | 80 at 4.50; 120 at 5.00 | 960 |
| 23 May | Sell 150 | (710) | 50 at 5.00 | 250 |
| 28 May | Buy 50 at 5.40 | 270 | 50 at 5.00; 50 at 5.40 | 520 |

The 9 May sale costs 60 × 4.00 + 60 × 4.50 = 510. The 23 May sale costs 80 × 4.50 + 70 × 5.00 = 710.

**AVCO** (a new average after every purchase; sales leave at the current average).

| Date | Movement | Cost of the movement (£) | Balance (units) | Balance (£) | Average cost (£) |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 May | Opening | 240 | 60 | 240 | 4.00 |
| 4 May | Buy 140 | 630 | 200 | 870 | 4.35 |
| 9 May | Sell 120 | (522) | 80 | 348 | 4.35 |
| 16 May | Buy 120 | 600 | 200 | 948 | 4.74 |
| 23 May | Sell 150 | (711) | 50 | 237 | 4.74 |
| 28 May | Buy 50 | 270 | 100 | 507 | 5.07 |

| Result for May | FIFO (£) | AVCO (£) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Revenue (270 units × £8) | 2,160 | 2,160 |
| Cost of goods sold (510 + 710 or 522 + 711) | 1,220 | 1,233 |
| **Gross profit** | **940** | **927** |
| **Closing inventory (100 units)** | **520** | **507** |

Check for both methods: opening 240 + purchases (630 + 600 + 270 = 1,500) = 1,740 = cost of goods sold + closing inventory (1,220 + 520 and 1,233 + 507). Prices rose, so FIFO has the higher closing inventory and the higher profit. The cash paid and received is the **same** under both.

*Easy way to see it:* FIFO is a shop selling the oldest milk first, so the newest (dearest) milk is left in the fridge. AVCO is pouring all the milk into one tank, so every litre costs the average.

### Example 3: Lower of cost and NRV, item by item

Dilruba's shop has three lines at the year-end.

| Line | Units | Cost per unit (£) | Selling price per unit (£) | Repair cost per unit (£) | Selling cost per unit (£) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| P (kettles) | 200 | 18 | 30 | none | 3 |
| Q (hair dryers, need a new part) | 150 | 25 | 32 | 6 | 4 |
| R (irons) | 80 | 40 | 40 | none | 2 |

1. NRV of P = 30 − 3 = **27**. Cost 18 is lower, so value = 200 × 18 = **£3,600**.
2. NRV of Q = 32 − 6 − 4 = **22**. This is **below cost 25**, so value = 150 × 22 = **£3,300** (write-down 150 × 3 = £450).
3. NRV of R = 40 − 2 = **38**. Below cost 40, so value = 80 × 38 = **£3,040** (write-down 80 × 2 = £160).

Inventory in the statement of financial position = 3,600 + 3,300 + 3,040 = **£9,940**. Total write-down = 450 + 160 = £610.

*The wrong way:* total cost 10,550 against total NRV 11,740 would say "cost is lower" and keep £10,550. That hides the losses on Q and R behind the profit on P. IAS 2 says compare each line.

### Example 4: An inventory count on a different date

**Count after the year-end.** Hasan's year-end is 31 December 20X5. He counted inventory on **4 January 20X6**: cost **£61,300**. Between 1 and 4 January: goods costing **£5,200** were received from suppliers; goods were sold for **£9,600** at a gross margin of 25%; goods costing **£400** were sent back to a supplier.

1. Goods received (£5,200) were **not** in the shop on 31 December, but are in the count, so **subtract**.
2. Goods sold: cost = 9,600 × 75% = £7,200. They **were** in the shop on 31 December, but are gone by the count, so **add**.
3. Goods sent back to the supplier (£400) were in the shop on 31 December, but are gone by the count, so **add**.

Inventory at 31 December = 61,300 − 5,200 + 7,200 + 400 = **£63,700**.

**Count before the year-end** (a different case). Count on 28 December: £50,000. Goods received 29 to 31 December: £2,000 (add). Cost of goods sold 29 to 31 December: £3,000 (subtract). Inventory at 31 December = 50,000 + 2,000 − 3,000 = **£49,000**.

*Easy way to see it:* the count is a photograph taken on the wrong day. Wind the film back (or forward) to 31 December, undoing everything that moved in between.

### Example 5: Mark-up and margin

**(a) Cost £60.** At a **25% margin**, selling price = 60 × 100 ÷ 75 = **£80** (profit £20). At a **25% mark-up**, selling price = 60 × 125 ÷ 100 = **£75** (profit £15).

**(b) Working backwards.** Sales are £91,000. With a **30% mark-up**: cost of sales = 91,000 ÷ 1.30 = **£70,000**, gross profit £21,000. With a **30% margin**: gross profit = 91,000 × 30% = £27,300, cost of sales = **£63,700**.

**(c) Estimating closing inventory.** Opening inventory £9,000, purchases £72,000, sales £91,000. Goods available = 9,000 + 72,000 = £81,000.

| £ | 30% mark-up | 30% margin |
|---|---:|---:|
| Goods available for sale | 81,000 | 81,000 |
| Cost of sales (from (b)) | (70,000) | (63,700) |
| **Closing inventory** | **11,000** | **17,300** |

*Easy way to see it:* "mark-up" always talks about **cost** as the 100%; "margin" always talks about **sales** as the 100%. Decide which one is 100% before you do any sum.

## 7.5 Exam traps

1. **Delivery inwards v outwards.** Inwards (goods coming to you) is added to purchases and is in cost of sales. Outwards (goods going to customers) is a distribution cost **below** gross profit. A very common wrong option includes both.
2. **Adding closing inventory instead of deducting it.** Cost of sales = opening + purchases + delivery inwards − closing. Also, do not forget to take **returns outwards** off purchases.
3. **NRV mistakes.** (a) Forgetting the repair or modification cost, not just the selling cost. (b) Comparing the totals instead of **line by line**. (c) Writing inventory **up** to NRV when NRV is higher than cost: never; the value is the **lower** figure. (d) Using selling price or replacement cost.
4. **Count-date direction.** After the year-end: subtract goods received, add goods sold (at **cost**). Before the year-end: the opposite. Sales must be changed from selling price to **cost** first, using the margin or mark-up.
5. **AVCO is a running average.** Recalculate after **every purchase**; a sale does not change the average. A plain average of the prices, or one average for the whole period, is wrong. Also remember: FIFO and AVCO give different profit and inventory, but **the same cash**.
6. **Mark-up v margin.** Mark-up is on cost, margin is on sales. A 25% margin is **not** a 25% mark-up (it equals a 33.3% mark-up). Using the wrong one gives the wrong cost of sales and so the wrong closing inventory.
7. **Drawings, theft and insurance.** Own-use goods are recorded at **cost** (not selling price) by crediting cost of sales, not inventory. Stolen goods come out of purchases and go to expenses. The insurance claim is **other income**, never deducted from cost of sales, and only recorded if reasonably certain.
8. **Two-year effect of an inventory error.** An overstated closing inventory overstates this year's profit and **understates** next year's profit (it is next year's opening inventory). Total profit over the two years is right.

## 7.8 Answers and explanations

### Answers: extra practice questions

**Q1. Answer: C (£94,600)**

- **Why:** Cost of sales uses net purchases. Add delivery inwards (it is a cost of getting goods ready to sell). Do not use delivery outwards (a distribution cost below gross profit).
- **Formula:** 14,600 + 93,000 − 2,400 + 1,300 − 11,900 = **£94,600**.
- **Easy example:** You sold only the goods that left the shop. Goods you sent back to a supplier never got sold, so they come off the cost.
- **Why not the others:** A (£93,300) leaves out delivery inwards. B (£96,700) wrongly adds delivery outwards. D (£97,000) forgets to deduct returns outwards.

**Q2. Answer: B and D**

- **Why:** Cost of inventory is everything spent to bring the goods to their present location and condition: the import duty (B) and transport to the shop (D).
- **Easy example:** If you buy mangoes at the port, the fee to carry them to your stall is part of what the mangoes cost you.
- **Why not the others:** A (delivery to customers) is a distribution cost after the goods are ready for sale. C (advertising) is a selling expense. Neither brings the goods to the shop.

**Q3. Answer: A (£4,950)**

- **Why:** Work out NRV for each line (selling price less repair and selling costs) and use the **lower of cost and NRV** for each line.
- **Formula:** 120 × 14.00 + 80 × 29.00 + 50 × 19.00 = 1,680 + 2,320 + 950 = **£4,950** (working below).
- **Easy example:** Goods you cannot sell for what they cost you are not worth their cost, so you show the lower, truer value.
- **Why not the others:** B (£5,030) ignores the repair cost on Y, so Y stays at cost. C (£5,180) values everything at cost. D (£5,370) uses NRV for every line, even X where NRV is higher than cost.

| Line | NRV per unit (£) | Lower of cost and NRV (£) | Units | Value (£) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| X | 20.00 − 2.50 = 17.50 | 14.00 (cost) | 120 | 1,680 |
| Y | 42.00 − 9.00 − 4.00 = 29.00 | 29.00 (NRV) | 80 | 2,320 |
| Z | 20.00 − 1.00 = 19.00 | 19.00 (NRV) | 50 | 950 |
| **Total** | | | | **4,950** |

**Q4. Answer: D (£715)**

- **Why:** Closing units = 40 + 100 − 70 + 60 − 90 + 30 = 70. Under FIFO the oldest goods are sold first, so the 70 units left are the **newest** ones.
- **Formula:** After the 8 June sale: 70 at £9. The 25 June sale takes 70 at £9 and 20 at £10, leaving 40 at £10. Add the 29 June purchase. Closing = 40 × 10.00 + 30 × 10.50 = 400 + 315 = **£715**.
- **Easy example:** The shopkeeper sells the oldest tyres first, so the tyres still on the rack are the latest, dearest ones.
- **Why not the others:** A (£1,420) is the FIFO **cost of sales** (590 + 830), not the closing inventory. B (£590) uses the oldest prices (40 at £8 and 30 at £9), which is LIFO, and is not allowed. C (£735) values all 70 units at the latest price.

**Q5. Answer: B (£884)**

- **Why:** AVCO needs a new average cost after each purchase. Sales leave at the current average and do not change it.
- **Formula:** Average after 9 July = 1,800 ÷ 300 = 6.00. Average after 22 July = (900 + 800) ÷ 250 = 6.80. Closing inventory = 130 × 6.80 = **£884** (working below).
- **Easy example:** Each time a new sack of rice is tipped into the drum, work out the new average price per kilo. Then every scoop taken out costs that average.
- **Why not the others:** A (£995) is the FIFO answer (100 at £8 + 30 at £6.50). C (£845) is one average for the whole month (all goods available, 2,600 ÷ 400 = £6.50) times 130 units, which ignores the sales between the purchases. D (£1,716) is the cost of sales, not the closing inventory.

| Date | Movement | Units | £ | Average (£) |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 July | Opening | 100 | 500 | 5.00 |
| 9 July | Buy 200 at 6.50 | 300 | 1,800 | 6.00 |
| 14 July | Sell 150 at 6.00 | 150 | 900 | 6.00 |
| 22 July | Buy 100 at 8.00 | 250 | 1,700 | 6.80 |
| 28 July | Sell 120 at 6.80 | 130 | 884 | 6.80 |

Check: cost of sales 900 + 816 = 1,716; goods available 500 + 1,300 + 800 = 2,600 = 1,716 + 884.

**Q6. Answer: A and D**

- **Why:** A service firm's cost of sales is the cost directly linked to the client work: the freelancer's fee (A) and the commission for winning the contract (D).
- **Easy example:** A tailor's cost of sales includes the cloth and the sewing helper for **your** suit. It does not include the rent of the whole workshop.
- **Why not the others:** B (head office rent) and C (accountant's salary) are general overheads, which are not directly linked to a client job. They go to other expense headings.

**Q7. Answer: B and C**

- **Why:** Closing inventory 20X1 was too high, so cost of sales was too low and 20X1 profit was **too high**. The same wrong figure is 20X2's opening inventory, so 20X2 cost of sales was too high and 20X2 profit was **too low**.
- **Formula:** 20X1: 30,000 − 5,000 = **£25,000**. 20X2: 34,000 + 5,000 = **£39,000**. Total for both years = 64,000 either way.
- **Easy example:** It is like borrowing £5,000 of profit from next year to make this year look good; next year has to pay it back.
- **Why not the others:** A (£35,000) adds the error to 20X1 instead of taking it away. D (£29,000) takes the error away in 20X2 as well, but in 20X2 the error **reverses**, so profit goes up.

**Q8. Answer: C (£10,000)**

- **Why:** A 25% mark-up means cost is 100 and selling price is 125. So cost of sales = sales × 100 ÷ 125. Closing inventory is what is left from the goods available.
- **Formula:** Cost of sales = 150,000 × 100 ÷ 125 = 120,000. Closing inventory = 12,000 + 118,000 − 120,000 = **£10,000**.
- **Easy example:** The shop had £130,000 of shoes to sell. It sold shoes that cost £120,000. So £10,000 of shoes were left to be destroyed.
- **Why not the others:** A (£17,500) treats 25% as a **margin** on sales (cost = 75% of 150,000 = 112,500). B (£37,500) is 25% of sales, a profit figure, not an inventory figure. D (£130,000) is the goods available, forgetting to deduct the cost of sales.

**Q9. Answer (Rahman Electrical Supplies)**

**Q10. Answer (Anwar Gadgets)**

**Requirement 1: FIFO**

| Date | Movement | Cost of the movement (£) | Inventory after | Value (£) |
|---|---|---:|---|---:|
| 1 Jan | Opening | 2,000 | 200 at 10.00 | 2,000 |
| 8 Jan | Buy 300 at 11.00 | 3,300 | 200 at 10.00; 300 at 11.00 | 5,300 |
| 15 Jan | Sell 350 | (3,650) | 150 at 11.00 | 1,650 |
| 22 Jan | Buy 250 at 13.00 | 3,250 | 150 at 11.00; 250 at 13.00 | 4,900 |
| 29 Jan | Sell 300 | (3,600) | 100 at 13.00 | 1,300 |

- 15 Jan sale: 200 × 10.00 + 150 × 11.00 = 2,000 + 1,650 = **£3,650**.
- 29 Jan sale: 150 × 11.00 + 150 × 13.00 = 1,650 + 1,950 = **£3,600**.
- Cost of goods sold = 3,650 + 3,600 = **£7,250**. Closing inventory = 100 × 13.00 = **£1,300**.

**Requirement 2: AVCO**

| Date | Movement | Cost of the movement (£) | Balance (units) | Balance (£) | Average cost (£) |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 Jan | Opening | 2,000 | 200 | 2,000 | 10.00 |
| 8 Jan | Buy 300 at 11.00 | 3,300 | 500 | 5,300 | 10.60 |
| 15 Jan | Sell 350 | (3,710) | 150 | 1,590 | 10.60 |
| 22 Jan | Buy 250 at 13.00 | 3,250 | 400 | 4,840 | 12.10 |
| 29 Jan | Sell 300 | (3,630) | 100 | 1,210 | 12.10 |

- Cost of goods sold = 3,710 + 3,630 = **£7,340**. Closing inventory = 100 × 12.10 = **£1,210**.
- *Check (both methods):* opening 2,000 + purchases 6,550 (3,300 + 3,250) = 8,550 = 7,250 + 1,300 = 7,340 + 1,210.

**Requirement 3: profit for January**

| £ | FIFO | AVCO |
|---|---:|---:|
| Revenue (350 × 20 + 300 × 22 = 7,000 + 6,600) | 13,600 | 13,600 |
| Opening inventory | 2,000 | 2,000 |
| Purchases | 6,550 | 6,550 |
| Less: closing inventory | (1,300) | (1,210) |
| Cost of sales | (7,250) | (7,340) |
| **Gross profit** | **6,350** | **6,260** |
| Operating expenses | (2,100) | (2,100) |
| **Profit for the month** | **4,250** | **4,160** |

FIFO profit is **£90 higher**, exactly the difference in closing inventory (1,300 − 1,210).

**Requirement 4: statement of financial position at 31 January**

Cash is the same under both methods: opening bank 3,000 + receipts 13,600 − purchases 6,550 − expenses 2,100 = **£7,950**.

| £ | FIFO | AVCO |
|---|---:|---:|
| Inventory | 1,300 | 1,210 |
| Cash at bank | 7,950 | 7,950 |
| **Total assets** | **9,250** | **9,160** |
| Equity: opening capital | 5,000 | 5,000 |
| Equity: profit for the month | 4,250 | 4,160 |
| **Total equity** | **9,250** | **9,160** |

Why cash is the same: the choice of FIFO or AVCO only changes how the purchase cost is shared between cost of sales and closing inventory in the accounts; the actual money paid to suppliers and received from customers has not changed.

*Cash check:* profit + fall in inventory = cash movement. FIFO: 4,250 + (2,000 − 1,300) = 4,950. AVCO: 4,160 + (2,000 − 1,210) = 4,950. Both equal 7,950 − 3,000 = 4,950.

*Marking tips:* the traps are (1) AVCO's new average after **each purchase** (10.60, then 12.10), (2) the sale not changing the average, (3) saying cash is different because profit is different.

### Answers: true or false

| # | Answer | Reason and easy example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **False** | Delivery outwards is a distribution cost, deducted **below** gross profit. Only delivery **inwards** is in cost of sales. Paying a rickshaw to take a parcel to your customer is a selling cost. |
| 2 | **True** | Closing inventory reduces cost of sales (it was not sold) and is carried forward as a current asset. Goods still on the shelf are an asset, not a cost. |
| 3 | **False** | IAS 2 allows FIFO and AVCO. LIFO is **not** allowed. |
| 4 | **True** | With rising prices, FIFO leaves the newest (dearest) goods in closing inventory, so cost of sales is lower and profit is higher. |
| 5 | **True** | Cost 100, selling price 125, profit 25. Profit is 25% of cost (mark-up) and 25 ÷ 125 = 20% of selling price (margin). |
| 6 | **False** | The overstated closing inventory is **next** year's opening inventory, which makes next year's cost of sales too high and next year's profit **understated**. The error reverses. |
| 7 | **True** | Goods taken by the owner are drawings, valued at cost. The credit to cost of sales takes the cost out of the trading figures. Taking a tin of biscuits home from your own shop is not a business expense. |
| 8 | **False** | The insurance claim is **other income** (with an other receivable if unpaid). It is never netted against cost of sales. |
