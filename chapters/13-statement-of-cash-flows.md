# Chapter 13: Statement of cash flows

## 13.1 Start here: the idea in plain English

Meet Rina, who runs a small tailoring shop. At the end of the year her accountant says: "You made a **profit of £20,000**." Rina opens her cash box and finds she has **less cash than last year**. How can that be?

- Customers took clothes on credit and still owe her £5,000. The sale is in profit, but **no cash has arrived**.
- She bought £4,000 of cloth that is still on the shelf. The cash has **left**, but it is not yet a cost.
- She bought a new sewing machine for £14,000. The cash has left, but the machine is **not an expense**. Only its depreciation is.
- She repaid £6,000 of a bank loan. The cash has left, but **repaying a loan is not an expense**.

The lesson: **profit is not cash**. A business can show a profit and still run out of money to pay wages and suppliers. So the law requires a company to publish a second statement that follows only the **cash**: the **statement of cash flows** (some people say "cash flow statement"). The rules are in an accounting standard called **IAS 7**.

The statement sorts every cash movement into three groups, so a reader can see *where the cash came from and where it went*:

| Group | Plain meaning | Everyday examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Operating activities** | The day-to-day trading of the business | Cash from customers, cash to suppliers and staff, interest paid, tax paid |
| **Investing activities** | Buying and selling long-term assets and investments | Buying a machine, selling a van, interest received |
| **Financing activities** | Money from and to the owners and lenders | Issuing shares, taking or repaying a loan, dividends paid |

The statement ends by showing the **net change in cash and cash equivalents** and checks it against the cash in the statement of financial position (balance sheet). **Cash equivalents** are very safe, short-term deposits that can be turned into cash at once (see 13.2).

> **KEY FACT** Operating + investing + financing = the **net increase or decrease** in cash and cash equivalents. Opening cash and cash equivalents plus that change must equal closing cash and cash equivalents in the statement of financial position.

## 13.2 Key facts to remember

- **Purpose (IAS 7):** to give historical information about the changes in cash and cash equivalents, split into operating, investing and financing activities. It shows how well the business can **generate cash** and what it needs cash for. It helps users (shareholders, banks, staff) judge **liquidity** (can it pay its bills?) and **solvency**. Profit alone does not show this.
- Both listed and unlisted companies must produce the statement.
- **Cash** = cash on hand and demand deposits (money you can take out at any time).
- **Cash equivalents** = short-term, highly liquid investments that are readily convertible into known amounts of cash and have an insignificant risk of changes in value. The workbook's guide is a maturity of **three months or less from the date of acquisition**. Shares in other companies and 12-month deposits are **not** cash equivalents.
- **Bank overdraft:** in the ICAEW exam-standard questions that this question bank follows, a bank overdraft is **deducted** when working out cash and cash equivalents, so opening or closing cash can be a negative figure. It is not part of "borrowings". If a question tells you to treat it differently, follow the question.
- **Operating activities** = the main revenue-producing activities and anything that is not investing or financing. **Tax paid** and **interest paid** are shown here (the workbook's rule for exam questions).
- **Investing activities** = cash paid to buy, and cash received from selling, property, plant and equipment (PPE), intangible assets and other non-current investments. **Interest received** (and dividends received) are shown here (the workbook's rule).
- **Financing activities** = cash from issuing shares or loans, cash repaid on loans, and **dividends paid** (the workbook's rule).
- **Two methods** for "cash generated from operations":
    - **Direct method:** cash received from customers, less cash paid to suppliers and employees. IAS 7 prefers it, but it is not compulsory.
    - **Indirect method:** start from **profit before tax** and adjust. It is easier to prepare, so it is the one the exam is more likely to use: **use it unless the question says otherwise**.
- **Indirect method steps:** (1) start with **profit before tax**; (2) add back **finance costs**, deduct **finance income**; (3) add back **non-cash expenses** (depreciation, amortisation, impairment); (4) **add a loss** or **deduct a profit** on disposal of non-current assets; (5) adjust for changes in **inventories, receivables, prepayments, payables, accruals and provisions**.
- **Why add back finance costs and deduct finance income?** The *actual cash* for interest is shown on its own lines later (interest paid in operating, interest received in investing). Leaving the accounting figures in the profit would count them twice.
- **Why the profit or loss on disposal is removed:** it is not a cash flow. The real cash (the **proceeds**) is shown in investing activities.
- **Working capital rule (think of cash):** an **increase in an asset** (inventories, receivables, prepayments) takes cash **away**, so deduct it. A **decrease in an asset** adds cash. An **increase in a liability** (payables, accruals, provisions) keeps cash in the business, so add it. A **decrease in a liability** takes cash away, so deduct it.
- **Non-cash items are never cash flows:** depreciation, amortisation, impairment, profit or loss on disposal, **bonus issues**, **revaluations**, and assets bought on credit until the cash is paid. Depreciation, amortisation, impairment and profit or loss on disposal appear only as **adjustments** in the operating reconciliation (to cancel them out of profit). Bonus issues, revaluations and unpaid assets do not appear at all.
- **Gross presentation:** if shares are issued for cash and the cash is used to buy a building, show **both** an inflow (financing) and an outflow (investing). Do not net them to nil.
- Only dividends **paid** are cash flows, not dividends declared.
- **Exam context:** the exam may ask you to complete the full statement (a pro forma is given), to complete extracts, or to work out one cash flow with a T-account. Multiple choice questions often ask for one figure such as tax paid, dividends paid, purchases of PPE or cash generated from operations.

## 13.3 Formulas and layouts

> **FORMULA** Net cash flow = Net cash from operating activities + Net cash from investing activities + Net cash from financing activities

> **FORMULA** Closing cash and cash equivalents = Opening cash and cash equivalents + Net increase in cash (or − Net decrease)

> **FORMULA** Cash generated from operations (indirect) = Profit before tax + Finance costs − Finance income + Depreciation + Amortisation + Impairment + Loss on disposal − Profit on disposal ± Changes in working capital

> **FORMULA** Working capital changes = (Opening inventories − Closing inventories) + (Opening receivables − Closing receivables) + (Closing payables − Opening payables)

> **FORMULA** Cash received from customers (direct method) = Revenue + Opening trade receivables − Closing trade receivables

> **FORMULA** Tax paid = Opening tax payable + Tax charge in profit or loss − Closing tax payable

> **FORMULA** Interest paid = Opening interest payable + Finance cost − Closing interest payable

> **FORMULA** Interest received = Opening interest receivable + Finance income − Closing interest receivable

> **FORMULA** Dividends paid = Opening retained earnings + Profit for the year − Closing retained earnings

> **FORMULA** Purchases of PPE (cash) = Closing carrying amount − Opening carrying amount + Depreciation charge + Carrying amount of assets disposed of

> **FORMULA** Proceeds from sale of PPE = Carrying amount at disposal + Profit on disposal (or − Loss on disposal)

> **FORMULA** Cash from share issues = (Closing share capital + Closing share premium) − (Opening share capital + Opening share premium) − Bonus issue paid from retained earnings

> **FORMULA** Loans: Closing loans − Opening loans = cash raised (if positive) or cash repaid (if negative). Add current and non-current loans together.

**The statement of cash flows (indirect method).** Brackets mean cash going out or a deduction.

## 13.4 Worked examples

### Example 1: Why profit and cash are different (Rina's tailoring shop)

Rina's profit before tax is **£20,000**. Interest and tax are ignored here to keep the idea clear. During the year:

- depreciation of **£3,000** was charged;
- trade receivables **rose** by £5,000;
- inventories **rose** by £4,000;
- trade payables **rose** by £2,000;
- she bought a sewing machine for **£14,000** cash and repaid a loan of **£6,000**;
- cash at the start of the year was **£11,000**.

| | £ |
|---|---:|
| Profit before tax | 20,000 |
| Depreciation (non-cash, add back) | 3,000 |
| Increase in receivables (customers owe more) | (5,000) |
| Increase in inventories (cash spent on stock) | (4,000) |
| Increase in payables (we have not paid yet) | 2,000 |
| **Cash generated from operations** | **16,000** |
| Investing: purchase of sewing machine | (14,000) |
| Financing: repayment of loan | (6,000) |
| **Net decrease in cash** | **(4,000)** |
| Opening cash | 11,000 |
| **Closing cash** | **7,000** |

A profit of £20,000, but cash went **down** by £4,000.

*Easy way to see it:* profit counts the sale when you make it. Cash counts only when the money moves. The machine and the loan repayment never touch profit at all.

### Example 2: Cash generated from operations (indirect method) and the direct method

Profit before tax is **£120,000**. The statement of profit or loss includes: depreciation £35,000, a **profit** on disposal of a machine £6,000, finance costs £9,000 and finance income £2,000. Balances:

| | Opening (£) | Closing (£) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Inventories | 60,000 | 48,000 |
| Trade receivables | 41,000 | 52,000 |
| Trade payables | 33,000 | 39,000 |

| | £ | Reason |
|---|---:|---|
| Profit before tax | 120,000 | Start here |
| Finance costs | 9,000 | Add back (shown later as interest paid) |
| Finance income | (2,000) | Deduct (shown later as interest received) |
| Depreciation | 35,000 | Non-cash, add back |
| Profit on disposal | (6,000) | Not cash; the proceeds go in investing |
| Decrease in inventories (60,000 − 48,000) | 12,000 | Stock was sold or used; cash released |
| Increase in receivables (41,000 − 52,000) | (11,000) | Customers owe more; less cash |
| Increase in payables (39,000 − 33,000) | 6,000 | We owe suppliers more; cash kept |
| **Cash generated from operations** | **163,000** | |

**The direct method** reaches cash generated from operations from the other direction: it adds up the actual cash received and paid. It does not use the figures above. A separate small trader has revenue of £92,000 (receivables rose from £8,000 to £11,000), purchases of £51,000 (payables fell from £7,000 to £5,000) and wages of £24,000 (accrued wages rose from £1,000 to £2,500).

- Cash received from customers = 92,000 − (11,000 − 8,000) = **£89,000**.
- Cash paid to suppliers = 51,000 + (7,000 − 5,000) = **£53,000**.
- Cash paid to employees = 24,000 − (2,500 − 1,000) = **£22,500**.
- Cash generated from operations = 89,000 − 53,000 − 22,500 = **£13,500**.

### Example 4: Non-current assets, shares and loans

**(a) Purchases of PPE.** Carrying amount of PPE: opening £250,000, closing £310,000. Depreciation was £40,000. A machine with a carrying amount of £25,000 was sold at a **profit of £6,000**.

- Additions = 310,000 − 250,000 + 40,000 + 25,000 = **£125,000** (cash paid, if all paid for in cash).
- Sale proceeds = 25,000 + 6,000 = **£31,000**.
- In operating activities the £6,000 profit is **deducted**. In investing activities, show **(125,000)** and **+31,000**.
- If £15,000 of the additions was still unpaid at the year-end (included in trade payables), the cash paid is 125,000 − 15,000 = **£110,000**. The £15,000 must also be taken out of the trade payables movement in the operating section.

**(b) Shares with a bonus issue.** Opening share capital £100,000 (£1 shares) and share premium £30,000. Closing share capital £140,000 and share premium £53,000. During the year there was a **1 for 10 bonus issue** (10,000 shares) paid out of the share premium account. Further shares were then issued for cash.

- Total closing 140,000 + 53,000 = 193,000. Total opening 100,000 + 30,000 = 130,000.
- The bonus issue only moves £10,000 from share premium to share capital, so it nets to nil.
- Cash from shares = 193,000 − 130,000 = **£63,000**. (Check: 30,000 new shares at £2.10 = £63,000.)
- *If the bonus had been paid from retained earnings,* the £10,000 of new share capital would arise without any cash, so it must be subtracted. For example, with closing share capital £140,000 and closing share premium £63,000 (opening figures unchanged): cash = (140,000 + 63,000) − (100,000 + 30,000) − 10,000 = **£63,000**.

**(c) Loans.** Opening loans £120,000, closing loans £90,000. No new loan was taken. The reduction of **£30,000** is cash **repaid** (financing outflow).

## 13.5 Exam traps

1. **Starting from the wrong profit.** The indirect method starts from **profit before tax**, not profit for the year. Tax is dealt with later as "tax paid".
2. **Wrong sign for disposals.** A **loss** on disposal is **added** back; a **profit** is **deducted**. Then the **sale proceeds** (not the profit or loss) go in investing. If a question says "carrying amount £21,000, sold for £30,000", the £9,000 profit is deducted and £30,000 is the investing inflow.
3. **Working capital the wrong way round.** More receivables or inventories = cash **out** (deduct). More payables = cash **kept** (add). Work out each movement as "opening − closing" for assets and "closing − opening" for liabilities.
4. **Mishandling interest.** Finance costs are added back in the reconciliation, then the **interest actually paid** is a separate line (use the accrued interest balances). Finance income is deducted, then **interest received** is shown in investing. Do not forget accrued interest sitting inside trade payables or receivables.
5. **Using the charge instead of the cash.** Tax paid is not the tax charge. Use opening payable + charge − closing payable. If a question does not give the tax charge, the workbook's approach is to assume that last year's closing tax payable was the amount paid in the year, and to find the charge as the balancing figure. If a tax **recoverable** (an asset) is shown, treat it as a negative payable.
6. **Non-cash items treated as cash.** A **bonus issue**, a revaluation, or an asset bought on credit is not a cash flow. For a bonus issue adjust the share capital and premium working. For PPE bought on credit, only the cash actually paid is shown, and the unpaid part must be removed from the payables movement.
7. **Mixing loans, overdrafts and dividends.** Add current and non-current loans together; a loan that only moved from non-current to current has **not** been repaid. An overdraft is part of cash and cash equivalents, **not** a loan. Dividends: use **paid**, found from the retained earnings working, not "declared".
8. **Cash equivalents and gross flows.** A 12-month deposit or shares in another company is **not** a cash equivalent. Count the maturity from the date the deposit was **placed**, not from the year-end. When shares are issued for cash to buy a building, show an inflow and an outflow, not nil.

### Q10. Scenario: working back from cash flows to the statement of financial position (10 marks)

**Zahir Tools Ltd** has a year-end of **30 June 20X7**. Its draft statement of cash flows is complete, but the closing statement of financial position was lost.

## 13.8 Answers and explanations

### Answers: extra practice questions

**Q1. Answer: A and B**

- **Why:** Investing activities are buying and selling long-term assets such as vans and machines.
- **Easy example:** Buying or selling a rickshaw that earns your living is an investing decision. Paying for petrol is not.
- **Why not the others:** C (repaying a bank loan) is **financing**. D (paying suppliers for goods) is **operating**.

**Q2. Answer: C (£110,500)**

- **Why:** Start with profit before tax, add back non-cash and finance items, remove the profit on disposal, then adjust for working capital.
- **Formula:** 84,500 + 22,000 − 3,000 + 6,000 − 1,500 − 9,000 + 4,500 + 7,000 = **£110,500**.
- **Easy example:** Stock you have bought but not sold has used up cash, so it is deducted. Customers who finally pay bring cash in, so it is added.
- **Why not the others:** A (£101,500) deducts finance costs and adds finance income, the wrong way round. B (£105,500) has the working capital movements reversed. D (£116,500) adds the profit on disposal instead of deducting it.

**Q3. Answer: B (£85,000)**

- **Why:** Tax paid = what was owed at the start + this year's charge − what is still owed at the end.
- **Formula:** 41,000 + 96,000 − 52,000 = **£85,000**.
- **Easy example:** You owed £41,000, got a new bill of £96,000, and still owe £52,000. So you paid £85,000.
- **Why not the others:** A (£11,000) is only the change in the liability. C (£96,000) is the charge, not the cash. D (£107,000) uses 96,000 + 52,000 − 41,000, swapping the opening and closing balances.

**Q4. Answer: C (£68,000)**

- **Why:** Retained earnings grow by the profit and shrink by the dividends. Profit should have added £134,000, but the balance only rose by £66,000. The difference is the dividends.
- **Formula:** 210,000 + 134,000 − 276,000 = **£68,000**.
- **Easy example:** You put £134,000 of profit into the family savings jar, but the jar only grew by £66,000, so £68,000 was taken out.
- **Why not the others:** A (£66,000) is the increase in retained earnings. B (£142,000) ignores the opening balance (276,000 − 134,000). D (£200,000) adds the increase to the profit.

**Q5. Answer: D (£185,000)**

- **Why:** Closing carrying amount = opening + purchases − depreciation − carrying amount of disposals. Rearrange to find purchases. Use the **carrying amount** of the machine, not the sale proceeds.
- **Formula:** 560,000 − 480,000 + 70,000 + 35,000 = **£185,000**.
- **Easy example:** Start with a shop worth £480,000. It lost £70,000 of value and £35,000 was sold, so without new buying it would be worth £375,000. To reach £560,000 you must have bought £185,000.
- **Why not the others:** A (£80,000) is only the increase in carrying amount. B (£150,000) forgets the disposal. C (£179,000) uses the sale proceeds (£29,000) instead of the carrying amount (£35,000).

**Q6. Answer: B, C and D**

- **Why:** A loss on disposal is not a cash payment, so it is added back. More payables means less cash paid out, so it is added. Finance costs are added back because the interest actually paid is shown on its own line.
- **Easy example:** If you delay paying your supplier, you keep cash in your pocket for longer.
- **Why not the others:** A is wrong because an increase in trade receivables means customers have not yet paid, so it is **deducted**.

**Q7. Answer: A (£16,700)**

- **Why:** Cash in hand, the bank balance and the deposit with an original term of 3 months or less count. The overdraft is deducted. The six-month deposit and the shares do not count.
- **Formula:** 1,200 + 14,300 − 6,800 + 8,000 = **£16,700**.
- **Easy example:** Cash equivalents are like money in a drawer you can use tomorrow without losing any value. A six-month fixed deposit or shares are not.
- **Why not the others:** B (£21,700) includes the six-month deposit. The deposit has 3 months left at the year-end, but its original term was 6 months. C (£23,500) ignores the overdraft. D (£30,300) adds the overdraft instead of deducting it.

**Q8. Answer: C (£115,000)**

- **Why:** The bonus issue (1 for 5 on 200,000 = 40,000 shares) only moves £40,000 from share premium to share capital. It brings in no cash. The cash is the increase in total share capital and premium.
- **Formula:** (290,000 + 105,000) − (200,000 + 80,000) = **£115,000**. Check: 50,000 new shares at £2.30 = £115,000.
- **Easy example:** Moving money from one pocket to another of your own trousers does not make you richer.
- **Why not the others:** A (£25,000) is only the movement in share premium. B (£90,000) is only the movement in share capital. D (£155,000) adds the bonus issue instead of ignoring it.

**Requirement 3: why cash from operations (£149,000) is higher than profit (£68,000)**

Profit for the year (£68,000) is struck **after depreciation of £60,000**, which uses no cash, so depreciation is the main reason. Profit is also after tax (£24,000), which is added back to reach profit before tax, and after finance costs (£15,000), which are added back next; the real cash for them is shown on the "tax paid" and "interest paid" lines further down. The £7,000 profit on disposal is not cash and is removed. Working capital changes are a small net outflow of £11,000 (inventories and receivables up, payables up, prepayments down, accruals down).

*Check:* 68,000 + 24,000 = 92,000 profit before tax; + 15,000 + 60,000 − 7,000 − 11,000 = **149,000**.

*Marking tips:* the trap is the disposal. The carrying amount is the proceeds **less the profit** (£11,000), not the proceeds (£18,000). A second trap is the interest balance: finance cost £15,000 is not the cash £14,000. The balance sheet check is the proof that every figure is right.

### Answers: true or false

| # | Answer | Reason and easy example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **False** | Profit is not cash. Credit sales, buying stock, buying machines and repaying loans can all make cash fall even in a profitable year (like Rina's tailoring shop). |
| 2 | **True** | Depreciation reduces profit but no cash leaves the bank for it. The cash left when the asset was bought. |
| 3 | **True** | A new loan is money from lenders, so it is a financing inflow. |
| 4 | **True** | The profit is not a cash flow, so it is removed from profit before tax. The cash received (proceeds) is shown in investing. |
| 5 | **False** | A bonus issue moves amounts within equity. No cash comes in, so it is **not** shown at all. |
| 6 | **False** | More receivables means customers have not paid yet, so cash generated is **lower**: the increase is deducted. |
| 7 | **False** | Tax paid = opening payable + charge − closing payable. Part of the tax is usually paid in the next year. |
| 8 | **True** | Cash flows are shown gross: the share issue is a financing inflow, the building is an investing outflow. They are not netted to nil. |
