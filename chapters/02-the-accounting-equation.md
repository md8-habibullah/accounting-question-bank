# Chapter 2: The accounting equation

## 2.1 Start here: the idea in plain English

Imagine Nasreen starts a small tea stall. She takes **£5,000 from her savings** and pays it into a new bank account for the stall. Where did the stall's money come from? From Nasreen. So the stall **owes** Nasreen £5,000. If she closed the stall tomorrow, she would expect her £5,000 back.

This is the first big idea. In accounting the business is treated as a **separate person** from its owner. The business has its own list of "what I have" and "what I owe". The owner is someone the business can owe money to. This is called the **business entity concept**. It is true even for a sole trader (one person running a business), where the law sees the person and the business as the same. Without it, Nasreen's house rent and the stall's rent would be mixed up, and nobody could tell whether the stall is doing well.

Now the second big idea. Everything the business **has** (its **assets**) was paid for by someone. Either the owner provided it (**capital**) or outsiders provided it (**liabilities**, what the business owes). So the two sides must always agree:

> **KEY FACT** Assets = Capital + Liabilities. This is the accounting equation. It is true at every moment, and every transaction keeps it balanced.

Move the pieces around and you get **Capital = Assets − Liabilities**. This is "what would be left for the owner if the business paid everyone else". It is also called **net assets**.

What about profit? When the stall earns a profit, the business ends up with more than before, and that extra belongs to the owner. So **profit is added to capital**. (This answers "why are retained profits added to capital?": the business owes the profit to its owner.) When Nasreen takes money out for her own use (**drawings**), the business owes her less, so **drawings are deducted from capital**. Putting it together:

> **REMEMBER** Closing capital = Opening capital + Capital introduced + Profit − Drawings.

Two statements use these ideas. The **statement of financial position** (balance sheet) is a **photograph** of the business on one date: assets on one side, capital and liabilities on the other. The **statement of profit or loss** is a **film of the year**: income minus expenses over a period gives the profit. The profit then moves into capital in the photograph.

| Word | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Asset** | Something the business has or is owed that will bring future benefit (cash, inventory, equipment, money owed by customers) |
| **Liability** | Something the business owes to outsiders (loan, overdraft, money owed to suppliers) |
| **Capital** (sole trader) / **equity** | What the business owes its owner: money put in plus profits kept, less money taken out |
| **Net assets** | Assets − liabilities. Always equal to capital |
| **Drawings** | Money or goods taken out by the owner for private use |
| **Income / expenses** | Income increases capital (not from the owner). Expenses decrease capital (not drawings) |
| **Trade receivable** (debtor) | A customer who has bought on credit and not yet paid (an asset) |
| **Trade payable** (creditor) | A supplier we have bought from on credit and not yet paid (a liability) |
| **Current / non-current** | Current: used up, sold or paid within about a year. Non-current: kept or due for longer than a year |

## 2.2 Key facts to remember

- **The equation:** Assets = Capital + Liabilities. Also Capital = Assets − Liabilities, and Liabilities = Assets − Capital. It is also called the **balance sheet equation**.
- **Every transaction has two effects** (a "dual effect"), so the equation always stays in balance.
- **Elements of the financial statements** (Conceptual Framework): **assets, liabilities, equity, income and expenses**. These five only.
    - **Profit** is *not* an element. It is the difference between income and expenses.
    - **Drawings** are not an element either. They are a withdrawal of equity.
- **Definitions (short):**
    - An **asset** is a present economic resource controlled by the entity as a result of past events. An economic resource is a right that has the potential to produce economic benefits.
    - A **liability** is a present obligation of the entity to transfer an economic resource as a result of past events.
    - **Equity** (capital) is the residual interest in the assets of the entity after deducting all its liabilities.
    - **Income** is an increase in assets or decrease in liabilities that increases equity (other than contributions from owners). **Expenses** are the opposite (other than distributions to owners).
- **Business entity concept:** the business is a separate entity from its owner for accounting, whether or not the law agrees. A **company** is also separate in law. A **sole trader** is not separate in law, so business debts can be met from private resources, but the accounts still keep them apart.
- **Capital** is what the business owes its owner. **Loans from banks or other people are liabilities, not capital**, even if the lender is a relative.
- **Capital introduced** can be **cash or another asset** (for example the owner's own car). Assets and capital both rise. It is **not income** and **not profit**.
- **Drawings:** money **and goods** taken out by the owner.
    - Examples: cash for living costs, a "salary" the owner pays to himself or herself, goods taken home, a private bill paid from the business bank account.
    - Drawings are **not an expense**. They never appear in the statement of profit or loss. They are deducted from capital in the statement of financial position.
- **Profit** (income − expenses) increases capital. A **loss** decreases it.
- **Credit transactions:**
    - Buy goods on credit: inventory (asset) up, **trade payable** (liability) up. No cash moves.
    - Sell goods on credit: **trade receivable** (asset) up, inventory down. Profit is recognised **now**, not when the customer pays.
    - Customer pays later: cash up, receivable down. **No effect on profit** (an exchange of one asset for another).
    - Pay a supplier later: cash down, payable down. **No effect on profit.**
- **Accruals (matching) concept:** income is matched with the expenses of earning it, in the period it is earned or incurred, not when the cash moves. This is why credit sales and credit purchases are recorded straight away.
- **Profit is not cash.** A business can make a profit and still see its bank balance fall (customers slow to pay, more inventory bought, money spent on equipment, owner's drawings).
- **Non-current assets** are used in the business for **more than one period** (more than a year) and are **not bought to be sold** to customers. They can be **tangible** (building, machine, van, fixtures and fittings) or **intangible** (patent, licence).
- **Current assets** are cash or items expected to turn into cash within the normal operating cycle or a year: **inventories, trade and other receivables, prepayments, cash and cash equivalents** (cash in hand and at the bank).
- **Purpose decides the class, not the item.** A car is inventory (current) for a car dealer but a non-current asset for a business that uses it. An investment is current if held short term and non-current if held for the long term.
- **Current liabilities** are due within **12 months**: trade payables, other payables, accruals (accrued costs), tax payable, a **bank overdraft**, and the part of a loan that is repayable within 12 months. **Non-current liabilities** are due **after more than 12 months** (a loan or mortgage repayable in five years).
- **Depreciation:** the cost of a non-current asset is spread over its useful life. It is an **expense** (reduces profit) and reduces the asset's **carrying amount** (cost less accumulated depreciation). **No cash leaves** when depreciation is charged.
- **Statement of financial position (SFP):** the financial position at **one point in time**. **Statement of profit or loss (SPL):** financial **performance over a period** of time.
- **Statement of profit or loss structure:** revenue − cost of sales = **gross profit**. Add other income, deduct expenses (distribution costs, administrative expenses, finance costs) = **profit for the period**.
- **Cost of sales** is the cost of the goods actually **sold**. Inventory bought but not sold is an asset, not an expense.
- **Link between the statements:** the profit for the period from the SPL is added to capital in the SFP. Drawings are deducted in the SFP only.
- **Gross profit margin** shows how much of each £1 of revenue is left after the cost of the goods sold.

## 2.3 Formulas and layouts

> **FORMULA** Assets = Capital + Liabilities

> **FORMULA** Capital = Assets − Liabilities (this is also called net assets)

> **FORMULA** Closing capital = Opening capital + Capital introduced + Profit − Drawings

> **FORMULA** Profit = Closing capital − Opening capital − Capital introduced + Drawings

> **FORMULA** Profit = Income − Expenses

> **FORMULA** Gross profit = Revenue − Cost of sales

> **FORMULA** Profit for the period = Gross profit + Other income − Expenses

> **FORMULA** Gross profit margin = (Gross profit ÷ Revenue) × 100%

> **FORMULA** Annual depreciation (straight-line) = (Cost − Residual value) ÷ Useful life in years

> **FORMULA** Carrying amount = Cost − Accumulated depreciation

> **FORMULA** Non-current assets + Current assets = Capital + Non-current liabilities + Current liabilities

## 2.4 Worked examples

### Example 1: Starting a business and keeping the equation balanced

Nasreen starts a tea stall. Record each step and check the equation.

1. She pays **£5,000** of her own money into the business bank account.
2. She buys a tea cart for **£1,200**, paid from the bank.
3. She buys tea and sugar (inventory) costing **£800** on credit.
4. She sells goods that cost **£500** for **£900**, paid into the bank.
5. She pays the supplier **£300**.
6. She takes **£100** from the bank for her own use.

The table shows the balance **after each step**.

| Step | Bank | Cart | Inventory | Total assets | Capital | Payables | Total capital + liabilities |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 5,000 | 0 | 0 | **5,000** | 5,000 | 0 | **5,000** |
| 2 | 3,800 | 1,200 | 0 | **5,000** | 5,000 | 0 | **5,000** |
| 3 | 3,800 | 1,200 | 800 | **5,800** | 5,000 | 800 | **5,800** |
| 4 | 4,700 | 1,200 | 300 | **6,200** | 5,400 | 800 | **6,200** |
| 5 | 4,400 | 1,200 | 300 | **5,900** | 5,400 | 500 | **5,900** |
| 6 | 4,300 | 1,200 | 300 | **5,800** | 5,300 | 500 | **5,800** |

- Step 2 is a swap: cash down, cart up. Total assets do not change.
- Step 3 is a credit purchase: assets and liabilities both rise by £800.
- Step 4 adds profit of 900 − 500 = **£400** to capital. Cash rises by the full £900, but inventory falls by £500.
- Step 6 is drawings. Capital falls by £100. It is not an expense.

*Easy way to see it:* the stall's total capital is her £5,000 + £400 profit − £100 she took = **£5,300**, and the closing statement agrees: 5,800 = 5,300 + 500. **Closing capital £5,300; total assets £5,800.**

### Example 2: Credit transactions and profit

Neelam sells phone covers. Look at three events and see **when profit is recorded**.

| Event | Inventory | Receivables | Payables | Bank | Capital |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| A. Buys goods costing £900 on credit (10 March) | +900 | | +900 | | |
| B. Sells goods costing £600 for £1,000 on credit (15 March) | −600 | +1,000 | | | +400 |
| C. Customer pays £1,000 (30 March) | | −1,000 | | +1,000 | 0 |
| D. Pays the supplier £900 (28 April) | | | −900 | −900 | 0 |

- At A, no cash moves, yet inventory and payables both rise. This is the **accruals concept**: the debt exists from the day of purchase.
- At B, the **profit of £400** is recognised on 15 March, when the sale is made. The customer has not yet paid.
- At C, nothing happens to profit. It is only a swap of one asset (receivable) for another (cash).

*Easy way to see it:* in the shop you hand over the phone cover and write the amount in your notebook. You have earned the money the moment the customer takes the goods, even though the cash is not in your hand. **Profit £400 recognised at event B, not at C.**

### Example 3: Finding the profit from opening and closing capital

Rashid keeps no full records. At the start of the year his business had assets of **£45,000** and liabilities of **£15,000**. At the end of the year it had assets of **£62,000** and liabilities of **£18,000**. During the year he paid **£5,000** of his own money into the business and took **£9,000** in drawings. What was the profit?

1. Opening capital = 45,000 − 15,000 = **£30,000**.
2. Closing capital = 62,000 − 18,000 = **£44,000**.
3. Rearrange: Profit = Closing capital − Opening capital − Capital introduced + Drawings = 44,000 − 30,000 − 5,000 + 9,000 = **£18,000**.
4. Check: 30,000 + 5,000 + 18,000 − 9,000 = 44,000. This agrees.

*Easy way to see it:* capital rose by £14,000. But £5,000 of that rise came from Rashid's own pocket, so it is not profit (only £9,000 of the rise comes from the business itself, after drawings). On the other side he took £9,000 out, so the business must have earned that £9,000 as well. £9,000 + £9,000 = £18,000. **Profit = £18,000** (not the plain rise of £14,000).

### Example 5: The statement of profit or loss and its link to capital

Imran sells cycle spares. His year ends on **30 June 20X3**. Revenue was £120,000 and the cost of the goods sold was £78,000. He received £1,500 of commission from a supplier. Expenses: van running costs £3,200 and driver's wages £9,800 (selling and delivery); rent £6,000, insurance £1,400 and office salaries £8,600 (administration); loan interest £900. He took **£10,000** in drawings. His capital at 1 July 20X2 was £30,000.

| Imran: statement of profit or loss for the year ended 30 June 20X3 | £ |
|---|---:|
| Revenue | 120,000 |
| Cost of sales | (78,000) |
| **Gross profit** | **42,000** |
| Other income: commission received | 1,500 |
| Distribution costs (3,200 + 9,800) | (13,000) |
| Administrative expenses (6,000 + 1,400 + 8,600) | (16,000) |
| Finance costs: loan interest | (900) |
| **Profit for the year** | **13,600** |

Gross profit margin = 42,000 ÷ 120,000 × 100% = **35%**.

| Capital in the statement of financial position | £ |
|---|---:|
| Capital at 1 July 20X2 | 30,000 |
| Profit for the year | 13,600 |
| Less drawings | (10,000) |
| **Capital at 30 June 20X3** | **33,600** |

The £10,000 drawings are **not** in the statement of profit or loss. They are deducted from capital. If Imran had called them "wages" and deducted them as an expense, profit would wrongly show as £3,600. **Profit £13,600; closing capital £33,600.**

## 2.5 Exam traps

1. **Treating drawings as an expense.** A "salary", private bill or goods taken home by a sole trader are **drawings**. They reduce capital but do **not** reduce profit and never appear in the statement of profit or loss.
2. **Treating a loan as capital.** Capital is the owner's stake. A bank loan (or a loan from an uncle) must be repaid, so it is a **liability**. Its interest is an expense, but the loan itself is not.
3. **Confusing the rise in capital with the profit.** Profit = increase in capital **− capital introduced + drawings**. Forgetting either item gives the wrong profit. Check the signs: introduced is subtracted, drawings are added back.
4. **Counting the whole selling price as profit.** On a credit sale of goods costing £600 for £1,000, receivables go up £1,000 but inventory goes down £600. Profit and capital go up only **£400**. Buying inventory is not an expense until it is sold.
5. **Profit is not cash.** A credit sale raises profit but not cash. Buying equipment, repaying a loan and drawings reduce cash but not profit. Depreciation reduces profit but not cash.
6. **Wrong side for receivables and payables.** A trade **receivable** is an **asset** (customers owe us). A trade **payable** is a **liability** (we owe suppliers). Customers paying us is only a swap: cash up, receivable down.
7. **Classifying by what the item is, not how it is used.** A car is inventory for a car dealer and a non-current asset for a business that drives it. A loan is split: the part due within 12 months is **current**, the rest is **non-current**. A bank overdraft is a **current liability**, not a negative asset.
8. **Mixing up the two statements, and the elements.** The SFP is a snapshot at **one date**. The SPL covers a **period**. The elements of the financial statements are assets, liabilities, equity, income and expenses. **Profit** and **drawings** are **not** elements.

## 2.8 Answers and explanations

### Answers: extra practice questions

**Q1. Answer: C (£73,000)**

- **Why:** First find the closing capital. Then add the liabilities, because assets = capital + liabilities.
- **Formula:** Closing capital = 40,000 + 18,000 − 7,000 = 51,000. Assets = 51,000 + 22,000 = **£73,000**.
- **Easy example:** A shop's stock, cash and fittings were paid for by the owner's share (£51,000) and by outsiders (£22,000). Together that is the value of everything the shop has.
- **Why not the others:** A (£29,000) takes the liabilities away instead of adding them. B (£51,000) is only the closing capital; liabilities were forgotten. D (£87,000) adds the drawings instead of deducting them.

**Q2. Answer: C and D**

- **Why:** In C, one asset (cash) goes down and another (inventory) goes up by £400. In D, an asset (bank) and a liability (loan) both go down by £2,000. Neither involves income, an expense or the owner, so capital does not change.
- **Easy example:** C is like swapping a £400 note for £400 of goods on the shelf. D is like paying back a friend from your wallet: you have less cash and owe less.
- **Why not the others:** A is an expense, so profit and capital fall by £600. B is capital introduced, so capital rises by £500 (even though it is not profit).

**Q3. Answer: D (assets up £900; capital up £900)**

- **Why:** Receivables go up by £2,400 but inventory goes down by £1,500. Net, assets rise by £900. That £900 is the profit, so capital rises by £900. No liability is created by a sale.
- **Formula:** Profit = 2,400 − 1,500 = **£900**.
- **Easy example:** You sell a bag that cost you £1,500 for £2,400 and are promised the money next week. You are £900 better off today, even though you hold no extra cash yet.
- **Why not the others:** A ignores the inventory that left. B wrongly creates a liability. C balances (2,400 = 1,500 + 900) but treats the £1,500 cost as new debt, which is not what happened.

**Q4. Answer: B (£11,500)**

- **Why:** Net assets are the same as capital. The rise in capital includes £2,500 that the owner paid in (not profit). Drawings of £8,000 reduced capital, so the business had to earn that amount as well.
- **Formula:** Profit = 31,000 − 25,000 − 2,500 + 8,000 = **£11,500**. Check: 25,000 + 2,500 + 11,500 − 8,000 = 31,000.
- **Easy example:** Your jar held £25 and now holds £31. You dropped in £2.50 of your own and took out £8 for sweets. So you earned £11.50.
- **Why not the others:** A (£6,000) is the plain rise in net assets. C (£16,500) adds the capital introduced instead of subtracting it. D (£500) reverses the signs of both items.

**Q5. Answer: A, C and D (equity, liabilities, income)**

- **Why:** The Conceptual Framework names five elements: assets, liabilities, equity, income and expenses.
- **Easy example:** Think of the five elements as the five ingredients of the accounts. Profit is the dish you cook from two of them (income minus expenses).
- **Why not the others:** B (profit) is the result of income minus expenses, not an element itself. E (drawings) is a withdrawal of equity by the owner, not a separate element.

**Q6. Answer: C (current £6,000; non-current £24,000)**

- **Why:** The part repayable within 12 months is a current liability. The rest, due after more than 12 months, is non-current.
- **Formula:** 30,000 − 6,000 = 24,000 non-current.
- **Easy example:** A car loan with 12 instalments due this year and 36 more later: the next 12 are "short term".
- **Why not the others:** A shows it all as non-current and ignores the amount due soon. B shows it all as current, which is wrong for the later repayments. D swaps the two figures.

**Q7. Answer: A (36%)**

- **Why:** The gross profit margin uses gross profit and revenue only. The other expenses of £30,000 are not part of it.
- **Formula:** Gross profit = 150,000 − 96,000 = 54,000. Margin = 54,000 ÷ 150,000 × 100% = **36%**.
- **Easy example:** You sell a £100 item that cost you £64. You keep £36 of every £100 before other costs: 36%.
- **Why not the others:** B (16%) deducts the other expenses too: that is the net profit margin (24,000 ÷ 150,000). C (56.25%) divides gross profit by cost of sales (54,000 ÷ 96,000). D (64%) is cost of sales as a percentage of revenue.

**Q8. Answer: B (drawings of £650)**

- **Why:** Private costs of the owner, whether paid in cash or taken as goods, are drawings. They reduce capital but are not business expenses, so they do not reduce profit.
- **Formula:** 450 + 200 = **£650** (goods taken are recorded at their cost).
- **Easy example:** If a shop owner buys herself lunch with shop money, the lunch is not a shop cost. She has taken money out for herself.
- **Why not the others:** A treats private costs as expenses, so profit would be understated. C ignores the goods: goods taken out of the business are drawings too, even though no cash moves. D splits the entry wrongly: goods taken home are drawings, not an expense.

| Capital reconciliation, year ended 31 December 20X4 | £ |
|---|---:|

**Requirement 3:** The rise in capital is 46,300 − 44,000 = £2,300. It is **not** the profit because capital is also changed by two things that are not profit. Hasina **paid in £6,000** (capital up, no profit earned) and **took out £12,500** (capital down, but the profit had already been earned). Profit = 2,300 − 6,000 + 12,500 = **£8,800**, much more than the rise.

*The cash rule:* an **increase in an asset** or a **decrease in a liability** uses cash. A **decrease in an asset** or an **increase in a liability** keeps or brings cash. Profit of £8,800 turned into a fall in cash because Hasina took out £12,500 and repaid £4,000 of the loan, and because more cash was tied up in inventory and unpaid customers.

*Marking tips:* the traps are (a) opening and closing **capital** come from assets less liabilities, not from the lists of assets alone; (b) capital introduced is subtracted and drawings are added back; (c) the brother's figure of £2,300 is the plain rise in capital.

### Answers: true or false

| # | Answer | Reason and easy example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **True** | Capital = Assets − Liabilities (net assets). If a shop has £10,000 of goods and owes £4,000, the owner's share is £6,000. |
| 2 | **False** | A loan must be repaid, so it is a **liability**. Cash (an asset) and liabilities both go up. Capital is unchanged. |
| 3 | **False** | Drawings are the owner taking money or goods for private use. They are deducted from **capital** in the statement of financial position, not from profit. |
| 4 | **True** | This is the business entity concept. A sole trader is the same person as the business in law, but the accounts keep the business's records apart from the owner's private life. |
| 5 | **False** | The statement of financial position is a snapshot at **one date**. The statement of profit or loss shows performance over a period. |
| 6 | **False** | The profit was recognised when the credit sale was made. Receipt of the cash only swaps one asset (receivable) for another (cash). |
| 7 | **True** | Inventory (asset) and trade payable (liability) both rise by the cost, so the equation stays balanced. Example: £500 of goods on credit means +£500 inventory and +£500 payable. |
| 8 | **True** | Depreciation is an expense that spreads the cost of an asset over its life. It lowers profit and the carrying amount, but no cash is paid when it is charged. |
