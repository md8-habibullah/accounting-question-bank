# Chapter 12: Company financial statements under IFRS Accounting Standards

## 12.1 Start here: the idea in plain English

Picture a school report card. Every school uses the same boxes: subject, marks, grade, comment. Because the layout is fixed, a parent can compare two children, or two schools, in a minute. Company accounts work the same way. A company must show its results in a fixed layout set by a standard called **IAS 1** *Presentation of Financial Statements* (IAS means International Accounting Standard; the full family of rules is called IFRS Accounting Standards). A bank, an investor or a tax officer can open the accounts of any company and know exactly where to look.

A company produces these main statements:

- **Statement of profit or loss (SPL):** the *film of the year*. Revenue minus costs gives profit.
- **Statement of financial position (SFP)** (the old name is *balance sheet*): the *photo on the last day*. It lists what the company owns, what it owes, and what is left for the owners.
- **Statement of changes in equity (SOCE):** the *bridge between two photos of the owners' share*. It shows profit added, dividends paid out and new shares sold.
- The **statement of cash flows** is the next chapter (Chapter 13).

Where do the numbers come from? From the **trial balance**: the list of every ledger balance at the year-end, before the year-end corrections. Your job has three steps. First, **correct** the trial balance with the adjustments (closing inventory, depreciation, irrecoverable debts, accruals, prepayments, loan interest, tax, provisions, bonus issue). Second, **sort** every figure into the right line. Third, **write** the lines in the fixed layout. The theory is small. Care and neat workings win the marks.

The ICAB workbook says that 40% of the exam needs the preparation of single-company financial statements. In the exam you are asked for **either** an SPL and an SFP **or** a statement of cash flows. The SOCE is tested only with multiple choice and multiple response questions.

| Word | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Functional format** | Costs are grouped by *what the money was used for* (making, selling, running the business), not by *what it was* (wages, rent) |
| **Cost of sales** | The direct cost of the goods sold or the service given |
| **Distribution costs** | The cost of selling and getting goods to customers |
| **Administrative expenses** | The cost of running the business: everything else |
| **Finance costs** | Interest and other costs of borrowing |
| **Operating profit** | Profit from running the business, before finance income, finance costs and tax |
| **Equity** | The owners' stake: share capital, share premium, retained earnings and other reserves |
| **Retained earnings** | Profits kept in the company over the years, after dividends |
| **Bonus issue** | Free extra shares given to existing owners, paid for from reserves |
| **Proforma** | The empty layout the exam gives you to fill in |

> **KEY FACT** Two built-in checks: (1) the trial balance has equal debits and credits; (2) after the adjustments, **total assets must equal total equity plus liabilities**. If the SFP does not balance, you have missed one side of an adjustment.

## 12.2 Key facts to remember

**The statements**

- In IAS 1 the first statement is called the *statement of profit or loss and other comprehensive income*. For the Accounting exam, **total comprehensive income = profit for the period**.
- The company name and the date (or period covered) must be shown clearly. IAS 1 also needs figures for the previous year; the workbook's examples and exam proformas show only the current year.
- IAS 1 allows expenses to be shown by *nature* or by *function*. The workbook uses the **functional** format: cost of sales, distribution costs, administrative expenses.
- In the computer-based exam, expenses are entered as **negative numbers** (a minus sign or brackets). Follow the order of the lines in the proforma you are given.

**Statement of profit or loss: which cost goes where**

- **Cost of sales:** purchases (net of discounts received) plus carriage inwards (the workbook calls it *delivery inwards*), adjusted for opening and closing inventory. For a manufacturer also wages of production staff and the depreciation and maintenance of production assets. For a service company it is mainly the cost of staff time.
- **Distribution costs:** wages of marketing and distribution staff, sales commission, vehicle running costs and carriage outwards, depreciation of delivery vehicles, advertising and promotion.
- **Administrative expenses:** wages of administrative staff, depreciation of non-current assets used by the office, amortisation of intangible assets, impairment losses, irrecoverable debts expense, and everything else.
- **Other income** (shown after gross profit): rent received, insurance claim proceeds, and gains on disposal of non-current assets (unless the question tells you to put the gain inside an expense line).
- **Finance income** (the workbook says it may also be called *investment income*): bank interest received, dividends received on investments.
- **Finance costs:** interest on loans, overdrafts and debt securities, and the dividend on **redeemable** preference shares.
- If the question tells you where a cost goes, **follow the question**.
- **Dividends paid to shareholders are not an expense.** They are a share-out of profit and go through retained earnings.
- **Income tax expense** = tax on this year's profit **plus** any under-provision (or **minus** any over-provision) from last year.

**Statement of financial position: which balance goes where**

- Assets and liabilities are split into **current** (settled or used within 12 months) and **non-current**.
- **Non-current assets:** property, plant and equipment (land, buildings, equipment, vehicles), right-of-use assets, goodwill and other intangible assets (for example development costs, net of amortisation).
- **Current assets:** inventories; trade and other receivables (shown **net of** the allowance for receivables); prepayments (shown as *other current assets*, or on their own line as in many exam proformas); cash and cash equivalents.
- **Equity:** share capital (at nominal value, including **irredeemable** preference shares), share premium, retained earnings, other reserves (for example a revaluation surplus).
- **Non-current liabilities:** long-term borrowings (bank loans, loan notes, debentures, **redeemable** preference shares), lease liabilities.
- **Current liabilities:** trade and other payables (including accruals and interest payable), deferred income, short-term borrowings (a **bank overdraft** and any loan instalment due within 12 months), provisions, income tax payable.
- A bank overdraft is **not** netted off against cash unless there is a legal right of set-off.
- **Deferred income** is money received before it is earned. It is a current liability, because the company still owes the goods or service.

**Statement of changes in equity**

- It shows, for **each component of equity**, the movement from the opening balance to the closing balance.
- It includes: profit for the period (total comprehensive income); issues of shares (share capital and share premium); **dividends** paid to owners; **bonus issues**.
- It does **not** include: loans received, purchases of non-current assets, or dividends on redeemable preference shares (those are finance costs in profit or loss).
- The **retained earnings column is just the retained earnings T-account** written across the page.
- Dividend paid = **number of shares in issue at the payment date** × dividend per share. Number of shares = share capital ÷ nominal value.
- **Bonus issue:** Dr Share premium (use all of it first, unless told otherwise), then Dr Retained earnings; Cr Share capital. No cash moves and total equity does not change.
- **Irredeemable** preference shares are equity; their dividend is deducted from retained earnings. **Redeemable** preference shares are liabilities; their dividend is a finance cost.
- Share premium cannot be used to pay a cash dividend.

**Year-end adjustments you must be able to make**

- **Closing inventory** is valued at the **lower of cost and net realisable value (NRV)**, item by item. If given at selling price, convert it back to cost.
- **Depreciation:** land is not depreciated. Charge it to the line the question names (production assets to cost of sales, delivery vehicles to distribution, office assets to administrative expenses).
- **Irrecoverable debt:** write it off first, then work out the allowance on the **remaining** receivables. Only the **change** in the allowance goes to profit or loss.
- **Accruals and prepayments:** apportion by months (Chapter 9).
- **Loan interest:** the finance cost covers the whole year. Any unpaid part is an accrual. A loan instalment due within 12 months is a **current** liability.
- **Tax:** Dr Income tax expense, Cr Income tax payable.
- **Provision** (simplified rule used in the workbook): create it only if there is a **present obligation** and payment is **probable** (more than 50% likely). Provide the **likely amount in full**. Dr expense, Cr Provisions (current liabilities, if due within 12 months). If it is not probable, no provision is made.

**The exam method (from the workbook)**

1. Read the question quickly. Count the adjustments and note where each expense belongs.
2. Move the trial balance figures into the proforma or into your workings.
3. Deal with **one adjustment at a time**, easiest first.
4. Put your workings into the proforma.
5. Add up the SPL, move the profit to the retained earnings working, then add up the SFP.
6. If the SFP does not balance, look for a missed figure or a missed second side.

- The workbook also shows a second route: write a **journal** for each adjustment, add the journals to the trial balance to make an **extended (final) trial balance**, then copy the lines into the statements. It gives the same answer. The workings-table method above is faster when the exam gives you a proforma.

## 12.3 Formulas and layouts

> **FORMULA** Gross profit = Revenue − Cost of sales

> **FORMULA** Cost of sales = Opening inventory + Purchases + Carriage inwards (+ production wages and depreciation of production assets) − Closing inventory

> **FORMULA** Closing inventory = lower of cost and NRV, for each item. NRV = Expected selling price − costs still to be paid to complete and sell it

> **FORMULA** Cost of goods given at selling price = Selling price × 100 ÷ (100 + mark-up %)

> **FORMULA** Operating profit = Gross profit + Other income − Distribution costs − Administrative expenses

> **FORMULA** Profit before tax = Operating profit + Finance income − Finance costs

> **FORMULA** Profit for the period = Profit before tax − Income tax expense

> **FORMULA** Income tax expense = Estimate for this year + Under-provision for last year (or − Over-provision)

> **FORMULA** Straight-line depreciation = Cost (less any residual value) × rate (or ÷ useful life). Reducing balance = Carrying amount at start of year × rate. Land: nil

> **FORMULA** Allowance at year-end = % × (Trade receivables − Irrecoverable debts written off). Charge to profit or loss = Irrecoverable debts written off + Closing allowance − Opening allowance

> **FORMULA** Finance cost for the year = Loan × interest rate × fraction of the year. Accrual = Finance cost − Interest already paid

> **FORMULA** Dividend paid = (Share capital ÷ Nominal value) × Dividend per share, using the shares in issue **at the payment date**

> **FORMULA** Bonus issue = Shares in issue × (new shares ÷ existing shares) × Nominal value

> **FORMULA** Closing retained earnings = Opening retained earnings + Profit for the year − Dividends (equity and irredeemable preference) − Bonus issue taken from retained earnings

> **FORMULA** Total equity = Share capital + Share premium + Retained earnings + Other reserves. Net assets = Total assets − Total liabilities = Total equity

## 12.4 Worked examples

### Example 1: Which line does each cost go to?

| Item | Line in the statement | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Purchases of goods for resale | Cost of sales | Direct cost of what was sold |
| Carriage inwards (cost of delivery *to* us) | Cost of sales | Part of the cost of getting the goods |
| Wages of factory workers | Cost of sales | Production staff |
| Sales commission paid to sales staff | Distribution costs | Cost of selling |
| Fuel and repairs for delivery vans | Distribution costs | Getting goods to customers |
| Depreciation of delivery vans | Distribution costs | Asset used for distribution |
| Newspaper advertising | Distribution costs | Marketing and promotion |
| Salaries of office staff | Administrative expenses | Running the business |
| Depreciation of the head-office building | Administrative expenses | Office asset |
| Irrecoverable debts expense | Administrative expenses | Workbook rule |
| Amortisation of development costs | Administrative expenses | Intangible asset |
| Interest on a bank loan | Finance costs | Cost of borrowing |
| Rent received from a tenant | Other income | Not revenue, not finance |
| Bank interest received | Finance income | Return on savings |

*Easy way to see it:* ask "what was this money *for*?" Making it, selling it, running the office, or borrowing money.

### Example 2: Closing inventory, depreciation and the expenses workings

Lotus Furniture Ltd has a year-end of 31 December 20X4. Trial balance figures: opening inventory £20,000; purchases £150,000; carriage inwards £4,000; sales commission £9,000; van running costs £11,000; office salaries £62,000; insurance £6,000.

- Closing inventory: tables cost £12,000 (can be sold for £15,000); chairs cost £9,000 (can now be sold for only £7,200, with no further costs).
- Depreciation: machinery used in production cost £100,000 at 10% straight line; delivery vans cost £60,000 at 20% straight line; the office building cost £300,000 including land of £100,000, depreciated at 2% straight line on the building only.

Step 1: closing inventory at the lower of cost and NRV. Tables: lower of 12,000 and 15,000 = **12,000**. Chairs: lower of 9,000 and 7,200 = **7,200**. Total = **£19,200**.

Step 2: depreciation. Machinery 100,000 × 10% = 10,000 (production). Vans 60,000 × 20% = 12,000 (distribution). Building (300,000 − 100,000) × 2% = 4,000 (administration). The land is not depreciated.

### Example 3: Receivables, loan interest, loan classification and tax

**(a) Allowance.** Trade receivables are £90,000. An irrecoverable debt of £4,000 is written off. The allowance is 5% of the remaining receivables. The opening allowance was £3,000.

- Remaining receivables = 90,000 − 4,000 = 86,000. Closing allowance = 86,000 × 5% = 4,300.
- Charge to administrative expenses = 4,000 + 4,300 − 3,000 = **£5,300**.
- Trade and other receivables in the SFP = 86,000 − 4,300 = **£81,700**.

**(b) Loan interest.** A company has 6% loan notes of £200,000 in issue all year. Interest is paid half-yearly and only the first half (£6,000) has been paid. Finance cost = 200,000 × 6% = **£12,000**. Accrual = 12,000 − 6,000 = **£6,000** (Dr Finance costs, Cr Accruals).

**(c) Loan classification.** A bank loan of £300,000 is repaid in 5 equal yearly instalments, the first due within 12 months of the year-end. Instalment = 300,000 ÷ 5 = 60,000. **Current** liability £60,000; **non-current** liability 300,000 − 60,000 = **£240,000**.

**(d) Tax.** The tax account has a **debit** balance of £2,000, because last year's tax was settled for £2,000 more than was provided (an *under-provision*). This year's tax is estimated at £30,000.

- Income tax expense = 30,000 + 2,000 = **£32,000**. Income tax payable in the SFP = **£30,000**.
- If instead last year's tax had been over-provided by £2,000 (a credit balance), the expense would be 30,000 − 2,000 = £28,000.

*Easy way to see it:* last year you guessed the tax bill too low and had to top it up. The top-up is a cost of this year's accounts, because we do not go back and change last year's accounts.

### Example 4: Statement of changes in equity

Rahim Traders Ltd has a year-end of 30 June 20X5. At 1 July 20X4: share capital (£1 shares) £100,000; share premium £20,000; retained earnings £55,000. During the year:

- On 30 November 20X4 it paid a dividend of 10p per share.
- On 1 March 20X5 it issued 20,000 £1 shares at £1.40 each for cash.
- On 30 June 20X5 it made a 1 for 6 bonus issue, using share premium.
- Profit for the year was £38,000.

Workings. Dividend = 100,000 shares × 10p = **£10,000** (the new shares did not yet exist on 30 November). Issue: cash 20,000 × 1.40 = 28,000, split into share capital 20,000 and share premium 8,000. Bonus issue: shares in issue = 120,000, so 120,000 ÷ 6 = **20,000** new shares, taken from share premium (now 28,000 before the bonus).

| £ | Share capital | Share premium | Retained earnings | Total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Balance at 1 July 20X4 | 100,000 | 20,000 | 55,000 | 175,000 |
| Dividend paid (10p × 100,000) | | | (10,000) | (10,000) |
| Issue of shares (20,000 × £1.40) | 20,000 | 8,000 | | 28,000 |
| Bonus issue 1 for 6 | 20,000 | (20,000) | | 0 |

## 12.5 Exam traps

1. **Wrong cost line.** Depreciation of delivery vans is a **distribution** cost, not cost of sales. Depreciation of the office is **administrative**. Irrecoverable debts expense is **administrative**. Read where the question tells you to put each item.
2. **Depreciation errors.** Land has an unlimited life, so it is **never depreciated**: take the land cost out before you multiply by the rate. Reducing balance uses the **carrying amount** (cost less accumulated depreciation), not the cost.
3. **Inventory at the wrong value.** Use **lower of cost and NRV**, line by line (do not net a gain on one line against a loss on another). If inventory is given at selling price, convert it to cost first. A mark-up of 25% means cost = selling price ÷ 1.25, **not** selling price × 75%.
4. **Treating dividends as an expense.** Equity dividends and irredeemable preference dividends reduce **retained earnings**; they never reduce profit. The one exception is the dividend on **redeemable** preference shares: it is a **finance cost** (those shares are a liability).
5. **Bonus issue mistakes.** A bonus issue brings in **no cash**, has **no effect on profit**, and does not change total equity. Use share premium first. Count the shares **in issue at that date**, including shares issued earlier in the year. For a dividend, count the shares in issue **on the payment date**.
6. **Allowance double counting.** Only the **change** in the allowance goes to profit or loss. Write off the irrecoverable debt **first**, then work out the allowance on what remains. In the SFP, show receivables **net** of the allowance, not the allowance as a liability.
7. **Interest and loan classification.** The finance cost is for the **whole year**; only the unpaid part is an accrual. A loan instalment due within 12 months is a **current** liability. A bank overdraft is a **current** liability too, not "negative cash".
8. **Provisions, tax and signs.** If a provision is needed (present obligation and more than 50% likely), provide the **whole likely amount**. Do not multiply by the probability. If the chance is 50% or less, make **no** provision. A tax **under-provision** from last year **adds** to this year's tax expense. In the exam, enter expenses as **negative** numbers.

## 12.6 Extra practice questions

Do these without looking at the answers. Questions 1 to 8 are in exam style (multiple choice or multiple response). Questions 9 and 10 are longer scenario questions: write your answers in the blank space.

**Q1.** *(Select TWO.)* Which of the following would be classified as **distribution costs** in a company's statement of profit or loss?

- A) Sales commission paid to the sales team
- B) Depreciation of the head-office building

## 12.8 Answers and explanations

### Answers: extra practice questions

**Q1. Answer: A and C**

- **Why:** Distribution costs are the costs of selling goods and getting them to customers. Sales commission and the running costs of delivery vans both fit.
- **Easy example:** In a tea stall, the boy who carries tea to the nearby offices is a distribution cost. The person who keeps the accounts is an administrative cost.
- **Why not the others:** B (depreciation of the head-office building) is an **administrative** expense. D (irrecoverable debts expense) is an **administrative** expense in the workbook's classification.

**Q2. Answer: C (£159,500)**

- **Why:** Inventory is valued at the lower of cost and NRV, item by item. The damaged item is valued at 4,500, not 6,000. Closing inventory = 18,000 − 6,000 + 4,500 = 16,500.
- **Formula:** 25,000 + 148,000 + 3,000 − 16,500 = **£159,500**.
- **Easy example:** You paid £6 for a cake that you can now sell for only £4.50. You cannot count it as £6 of wealth.
- **Why not the others:** A (£156,500) leaves out carriage inwards. B (£158,000) values the closing inventory at cost, so inventory is too high and cost of sales too low. D (£164,000) leaves the damaged item out of closing inventory completely (value nil instead of 4,500).

**Q3. Answer: D (£55,500)**

- **Why:** The new shares were issued on 1 June, so on 30 September there were 240,000 shares. The bonus issue on 31 December is also based on 240,000 shares, and it reduces retained earnings because the question says it is taken from retained earnings.
- **Formula:** Dividend = 240,000 × 5p = 12,000. Bonus issue = 240,000 ÷ 5 = 48,000. Closing retained earnings = 84,000 + 31,500 − 12,000 − 48,000 = **£55,500**.
- **Easy example:** A bonus issue is like a club relabelling part of its reserve fund as extra membership cards for the existing members. No money comes in or goes out. Only the labels change, and the reserve fund is smaller afterwards.
- **Why not the others:** A (£57,500) pays the dividend on only 200,000 shares (10,000). B (£63,500) works out the bonus on only 200,000 shares (40,000). C (£103,500) ignores the bonus issue.

**Q4. Answer: B and D**

- **Why:** The SOCE shows changes in each part of equity. An issue of shares raises share capital and share premium. A dividend reduces retained earnings.
- **Easy example:** The SOCE is the story of the owners' share only. A machine bought on credit and a loan repayment change assets and liabilities, not the owners' share.
- **Why not the others:** A (machine on credit) adds an asset and a liability. C (loan repayment) reduces cash and a liability. Neither touches equity.

**Q5. Answer: A (charge £3,650; net receivables £73,150)**

- **Why:** The irrecoverable debt is written off first. The allowance is then 5% of what remains. Only the **change** in the allowance is charged.
- **Formula:** Remaining receivables = 80,000 − 3,000 = 77,000. Closing allowance = 77,000 × 5% = 3,850. Charge = 3,000 + 3,850 − 3,200 = **£3,650**. Net receivables = 77,000 − 3,850 = **£73,150**.
- **Easy example:** You write off a bad loan to a neighbour, then top up your "bad luck" jar from £3,200 to £3,850. Only the top-up of £650 is a new cost.
- **Why not the others:** B (£650) forgets the £3,000 write-off. C (£3,800) works out the allowance on £80,000 instead of £77,000. D (£6,850) charges the whole new allowance and ignores the opening allowance.

**Q6. Answer: B (£66,000)**

- **Why:** Only the instalment due within 12 months (one fifth) is current. The three months of unpaid interest is also a current liability (an accrual).
- **Formula:** Instalment = 300,000 ÷ 5 = 60,000. Interest = 300,000 × 8% × 3/12 = 6,000. Total = **£66,000**.
- **Easy example:** It is the cash you must find in the next 12 months for this loan: the next instalment plus the interest you already owe.
- **Why not the others:** A (£6,000) leaves out the instalment. C (£60,000) leaves out the unpaid interest. D (£306,000) treats the whole loan as current, although only one instalment is due within 12 months.

**Q7. Answer: C (£36,000)**

- **Why:** Selling price = cost + 25% of cost = 125% of cost. So cost = selling price ÷ 1.25. Inventory must be shown at cost (or NRV if lower), never at selling price.
- **Formula:** 45,000 × 100 ÷ 125 = **£36,000**.
- **Easy example:** A shirt costs you £100 and you sell it at £125. The £125 includes your £25 profit. You are holding £100 of cost.
- **Why not the others:** A (£33,750) treats 25% as a share of the selling price (45,000 × 75%). B (£45,000) keeps the selling price. D (£56,250) adds 25% instead of taking it away.

**Q8. Answer: B (£70,800)**

- **Why:** Only the dividend on the **redeemable** preference shares is a finance cost, so it reduces profit. Equity dividends and irredeemable preference dividends are taken from retained earnings, after profit.
- **Formula:** 75,000 − 4,200 = **£70,800**. (Retained earnings then rise by 70,800 − 10,000 − 4,000 = 56,800.)
- **Easy example:** Redeemable preference shares are like a loan, so the "dividend" is interest. Irredeemable preference shares are like ownership, so their dividend is a share-out of profit.
- **Why not the others:** A (£75,000) ignores the finance cost. C (£66,800) treats both preference dividends as finance costs. D (£56,800) takes all three dividends off profit.

### Answers: true or false

| # | Answer | Reason and easy example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **False** | Equity dividends are a share-out of profit and are deducted from retained earnings. They are not an expense. Like a family sharing the shop's profit: it is not a cost of running the shop. |
| 2 | **True** | A bonus issue moves an amount from share premium (or retained earnings) to share capital. Both are part of equity, so the total is the same and no cash moves. |
| 3 | **True** | Redeemable preference shares are a liability, so their dividend is treated like loan interest: a finance cost in profit or loss. |
| 4 | **False** | The allowance reduces the receivables (an asset). It is deducted from trade receivables, not shown as a liability. |
| 5 | **True** | Delivery vans are used to get goods to customers, so their depreciation is a distribution cost. |
| 6 | **False** | An overdraft is shown as a current liability (short-term borrowing). It is netted against cash only if there is a legal right of set-off. |
| 7 | **True** | Last year's accounts are not reopened. The top-up is charged this year: tax expense = this year's estimate + under-provision. |
| 8 | **False** | Land has an unlimited life and is not depreciated. Only the buildings are depreciated, so take the land cost out first. |
