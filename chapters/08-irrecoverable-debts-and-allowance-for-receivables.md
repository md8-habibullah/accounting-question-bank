# Chapter 8: Irrecoverable debts and the allowance for receivables

## 8.1 Start here: the idea in plain English

Rahim runs a small grocery near a college. His regular customers buy now and pay at the end of the month. This is selling **on credit**. The money these customers owe him is called **trade receivables** (many people say "debtors"). It is an asset, because Rahim expects to collect it. It appears in the **statement of financial position** (the "balance sheet").

One day a customer, Mr Salam, closes his stall and disappears. He owes Rahim **£400**. Rahim knows this money will never arrive. It would be wrong to keep showing £400 as "money owed to me". So Rahim **writes the debt off**. He takes the £400 out of receivables and records a £400 cost in the **statement of profit or loss** (the "income statement"). In exam language this is an **irrecoverable debt** (the old name is *bad debt*).

Now think about the other customers who still owe money. From past years Rahim knows that about 5 pounds in every 100 pounds are never collected. He does not know **which** customers will fail. So he does not write anyone off yet. But he does not want to show the full receivables figure either. If customers owe £10,000, he thinks he will only collect about £9,500. The £500 "cushion" is the **allowance for receivables**. It is an **estimate**. It follows the idea of **prudence**: do not show assets or profit higher than you can reasonably expect.

Finally, some months later Mr Salam sends £150 after all. The £400 was already removed from receivables, so Rahim does not put it back. He simply records the cash and a **£150 reduction in the expense**.

| Word | Plain meaning | In the accounts |
|---|---|---|
| **Trade receivables** | Money customers owe us for goods or services sold on credit | Current asset |
| **Irrecoverable debt** (bad debt) | A debt that is **not expected to be paid** | Expense |
| **Write off** | Remove the debt from receivables and charge it as an expense | Dr Irrecoverable debts expense, Cr Trade receivables |
| **Allowance for receivables** (old name: provision for doubtful debts) | Our estimate of how much of the **remaining** receivables will not be collected | Separate account, deducted from receivables |
| **Expected credit loss** | The amount of receivables we expect not to collect, because of the risk that customers default | The idea behind the allowance |
| **Carrying amount** | Receivables after deducting the allowance (the "net" figure) | Shown in the statement of financial position |

> **REMEMBER** A **write-off** is about **one named customer** who will not pay: it removes the debt from the trade receivables account. An **allowance** is an **estimate for the rest**: it sits in its own account and is only **deducted** in the statement of financial position. The allowance never goes through the trade receivables account.

> **KEY FACT** Both ideas use the same expense account, **irrecoverable debts expense**. Write-offs and increases in the allowance are debits. Recoveries and decreases in the allowance are credits. That is why exam questions mix them together.

## 8.2 Key facts to remember

- **Irrecoverable debt:** a debt that is **not expected to be paid**. The customer might be bankrupt, in liquidation, dishonest or untraceable.
- **Writing off a debt:** Debit **Irrecoverable debts expense**, credit **Trade receivables**. The **whole** amount owed by that customer is written off. The customer's personal account in the receivables ledger is cleared too.
- **Where a write-off appears:**
    - It is an **administrative expense** in the statement of profit or loss.
    - **Revenue is not reduced.** The sale did happen. Failing to collect the money is a separate matter. So **gross profit does not change**; only **net profit** falls.
    - The receivable is no longer an asset, because it is no longer expected to bring in any money.
- **Debt recovered after it was written off:** Debit **Cash at bank**, credit **Irrecoverable debts expense**. The entry is the same whether the cash arrives in the same year or a later year. Do **not** credit trade receivables (the debt has already been removed).
- A computerised system cannot match a receipt from a customer who has already been written off, so the receipt is often posted to **suspense**. The correction is: Dr Suspense, Cr Irrecoverable debts expense.
- **Do not assume a debt is irrecoverable** just because the customer paid only part, disputes the invoice, or the bank **dishonoured** a cheque. These may be administrative problems. For a dishonoured payment, **reinstate the debt**: Dr Trade receivables, Cr Cash at bank. A customer who has gone bankrupt, with nothing left to pay creditors, **is** a clear case of an irrecoverable debt.
- **Allowance for receivables:** an estimate of the receivables that will not be collected (the expected credit losses). IFRS 9 requires it. It needs judgement, so it is less certain than a write-off. In the exam you are **given the probability of non-payment**; you only need to apply it.
- **Calculation:** Allowance = percentage × receivables **remaining after** the write-offs. If a question gives different percentages for different groups of customers, work out each group separately and add them up.
- **Only the change is charged each year:**
    - **Increase** in the allowance: Dr Irrecoverable debts expense, Cr Allowance for receivables.
    - **Decrease** in the allowance: Dr Allowance for receivables, Cr Irrecoverable debts expense.
    - An allowance **cannot be a debit balance**, so the most it can fall by is to nil.
- **The allowance is a separate ledger account** with a credit balance. In the statement of financial position it is **deducted from trade receivables**, and the net figure (the **carrying amount**) is shown. It is **not** a liability.
- **The irrecoverable debts expense account** collects: debits (debts written off, increases in the allowance) and credits (debts recovered, decreases in the allowance). The balance is transferred to the statement of profit or loss. If the credits are bigger, the net figure is a **credit** (it adds to profit).
- **The trade receivables account is only changed by:** credit sales (debit), cash received, discounts and credit notes, debts written off (all credits), and dishonoured payments (debit). It is **not** changed by the allowance or by cash recovered on a debt written off earlier.
- **Effect on the statements:**
    - Write-off: receivables fall, net profit falls, gross profit unchanged.
    - Increase in allowance: net receivables fall, net profit falls, gross profit unchanged.
    - Decrease in allowance: net receivables rise, net profit rises, gross profit unchanged.
    - Recovery of a written-off debt: cash rises, net profit rises, gross profit unchanged.
- **Cash:** writing off a debt and changing the allowance involve **no cash**. Only a recovery brings cash in, and a dishonoured payment takes cash back out.
- **Adjusting the trial balance:** add the adjustments in the adjustment columns, open new lines for irrecoverable debts expense and the allowance if they are missing, then cross-cast to the final trial balance. Watch for a balance already sitting in the trial balance (for example write-offs already recorded during the year).
- Do not mix up three look-alikes:
    - **Bad debt:** a customer cannot pay what was agreed.
    - **Allowance:** an estimate for debts that may fail in future.
    - **Discount:** a price reduction the customer is entitled to. It reduces revenue, not expenses.

## 8.3 Formulas and layouts

> **FORMULA** Irrecoverable debts expense in the statement of profit or loss = Debts written off − Debts recovered + Increase in allowance (or − Decrease in allowance)

> **FORMULA** Allowance required at the year-end = Receivables remaining after write-offs × probability of non-payment (%)

> **FORMULA** Change in allowance = Closing allowance − Opening allowance (positive = increase = debit the expense; negative = decrease = credit the expense)

> **FORMULA** Net trade receivables (carrying amount) = Receivables after write-offs − Closing allowance

> **FORMULA** Closing receivables = Opening receivables + Credit sales + Dishonoured payments reinstated − Cash received − Discounts and credit notes − Debts written off

> **FORMULA** Cash received from credit customers (through the receivables account) = Opening receivables + Credit sales − Debts written off − Closing receivables (gross, after write-offs, before the allowance)

> **FORMULA** Cross-check: Cash from customers = Revenue − Irrecoverable debts expense − Increase in net receivables (this works when the only items are sales, cash, write-offs, recoveries and the allowance)

**The three ledger accounts together.** Suppose in a year debts of £700 were written off, £200 was recovered on an old debt, and the allowance rose from £1,500 to £1,620.

| Irrecoverable debts expense: debit side | £ | Credit side | £ |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Trade receivables (debts written off) | 700 | Cash at bank (debt recovered) | 200 |
| Allowance for receivables (increase) | 120 | **Statement of profit or loss (balancing figure)** | **620** |
| | 820 | | 820 |

| Allowance for receivables: debit side | £ | Credit side | £ |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Balance c/d (new allowance) | 1,620 | Balance b/d (old allowance) | 1,500 |
| | | Irrecoverable debts expense (**increase**, balancing figure) | 120 |
| | 1,620 | | 1,620 |

When the allowance **falls**, the balancing figure appears on the **debit** side and is credited to the expense account. For example, opening allowance £1,800 and closing allowance £1,500:

| Allowance for receivables: debit side | £ | Credit side | £ |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Irrecoverable debts expense (**decrease**, balancing figure) | 300 | Balance b/d (old allowance) | 1,800 |
| Balance c/d (new allowance) | 1,500 | | |
| | 1,800 | | 1,800 |

**Where each item goes (use this as a checklist).**

| Event | Journal | Trade receivables account | Net profit | Cash |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Debt written off | Dr Irrecoverable debts expense, Cr Trade receivables | Credit side (falls) | Down | No cash |
| Cash recovered on a debt written off earlier | Dr Cash at bank, Cr Irrecoverable debts expense | Not touched | Up | Cash **in** |
| Allowance increased | Dr Irrecoverable debts expense, Cr Allowance | Not touched | Down | No cash |
| Allowance decreased | Dr Allowance, Cr Irrecoverable debts expense | Not touched | Up | No cash |
| Dishonoured cheque | Dr Trade receivables, Cr Cash at bank | Debit side (rises) | No effect | Cash **out** again |
| Discount taken unexpectedly by a customer | Dr Revenue, Cr Trade receivables | Credit side (falls) | Down (revenue falls) | Customer pays less |

**How it looks in the statements.** Use the same business as above: receivables after write-offs £32,400 and an allowance of 5% (£1,620):

| Extract: statement of financial position (current assets) | £ |
|---|---:|
| Trade receivables | 32,400 |
| Less: allowance for receivables | (1,620) |
| **Trade receivables, net (carrying amount)** | **30,780** |

| Extract: statement of profit or loss | £ |
|---|---:|
| Administrative expenses include: irrecoverable debts expense | 620 |

Gross profit is **not** affected: the irrecoverable debts expense sits below gross profit, in administrative expenses.

## 8.4 Worked examples

### Example 1: Writing off a debt, and a later recovery

Mehnaz runs a stationery shop. At 31 December 20X1 the customer Sultana Print owes **£800**. The liquidator writes that unsecured creditors will receive nothing.

1. **31 December 20X1.** The debt will not be paid, so write it off. Dr Irrecoverable debts expense £800; Cr Trade receivables £800.
2. The original sale of £800 stays in revenue. Only net profit for 20X1 falls, by £800.
3. **In 20X2** the liquidator unexpectedly pays **£150**. Dr Cash at bank £150; Cr Irrecoverable debts expense £150. Trade receivables is **not** touched.
4. Over the two years the net cost of this customer is 800 − 150 = **£650**. It is £800 in 20X1, then a £150 gain in 20X2.

*Easy way to see it:* a cheque you tore up as worthless is later honoured. You do not put the old cheque back in your drawer. You just bank the money and call it a lucky recovery.

### Example 2: Setting up and changing the allowance (5% each year)

A business estimates that 5% of its remaining receivables will not be paid. There was no allowance before 20X1.

| | 20X1 | 20X2 | 20X3 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Receivables after write-offs (£) | 36,000 | 42,000 | 28,000 |
| Allowance required at 5% (£) | 1,800 | 2,100 | 1,400 |
| Opening allowance (£) | 0 | 1,800 | 2,100 |
| Charge / (credit) to irrecoverable debts expense (£) | **1,800** | **300** | **(700)** |
| Net receivables in the statement of financial position (£) | 34,200 | 39,900 | 26,600 |

The 20X3 allowance account shows the fall: Dr Allowance £700 (balancing figure), Cr Irrecoverable debts expense £700. The closing balance carried down is £1,400. Only £700 is credited to profit or loss, **not** the whole £2,100.

**Part (b): different percentages for different groups.** If a question says £30,000 of receivables are not overdue (probability of non-payment 1%) and £10,000 are overdue (10%), the allowance is 30,000 × 1% + 10,000 × 10% = 300 + 1,000 = **£1,300**.

*Easy way to see it:* the allowance is like a "weather forecast" for your receivables. Each year you update the forecast, and you only record the **change** from last year's forecast.

### Example 3: A whole year in the ledger and the statements

Nadia's Print Shop has a year-end of **31 December 20X5**. Opening receivables £25,000 and an opening allowance of £1,000. During the year: credit sales £180,000; cash received from credit customers £170,000; debts written off £2,000; £300 recovered on a debt written off in 20X4. The allowance at the year-end is to be **4%** of the remaining receivables.

1. Closing receivables = 25,000 + 180,000 − 170,000 − 2,000 = **£33,000**.
2. Allowance required = 33,000 × 4% = **£1,320**. Increase = 1,320 − 1,000 = **£320**.
3. Irrecoverable debts expense = 2,000 − 300 + 320 = **£2,020**.
4. Net receivables = 33,000 − 1,320 = **£31,680**.

| Trade receivables account: debit side | £ | Credit side | £ |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Balance b/d | 25,000 | Cash received | 170,000 |
| Credit sales | 180,000 | Irrecoverable debts expense (written off) | 2,000 |
| | | Balance c/d | 33,000 |
| | 205,000 | | 205,000 |

| Irrecoverable debts expense: debit side | £ | Credit side | £ |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Trade receivables (written off) | 2,000 | Cash at bank (recovered) | 300 |
| Allowance for receivables (increase) | 320 | **Statement of profit or loss** | **2,020** |
| | 2,320 | | 2,320 |

| Extract: statement of profit or loss (year ended 31 December 20X5) | £ |
|---|---:|
| Administrative expenses: irrecoverable debts expense | 2,020 |

| Extract: statement of financial position (31 December 20X5) | £ |
|---|---:|
| Trade receivables | 33,000 |
| Less: allowance for receivables | (1,320) |
| **Trade receivables, net** | **31,680** |

Gross profit is **not** affected by any of this.

*Easy way to see it:* the three accounts tell one story. The receivables account shows who still owes us, the allowance account shows how much of that we doubt, and the expense account adds up the cost of the doubt and the losses.

### Example 4: Adjusting the trial balance

An initial trial balance (extract) shows: trade receivables £45,200 (debit); allowance for receivables £1,500 (credit); irrecoverable debts expense £400 (debit, write-offs already recorded in the year); suspense £250 (credit). Profit before these adjustments is £20,000. Still to do:

- (i) Write off a further debt of **£1,200**.
- (ii) Make the allowance **3%** of the remaining receivables.
- (iii) The £250 in suspense is cash received from a customer whose debt was written off last year.

Workings: receivables after write-off = 45,200 − 1,200 = 44,000. Allowance = 44,000 × 3% = 1,320. The old allowance was £1,500, so it **decreases** by £180.

| Account (£) | Initial Dr | Initial Cr | Adjust Dr | Adjust Cr | Final Dr | Final Cr |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Trade receivables | 45,200 | | | 1,200 | 44,000 | |
| Allowance for receivables | | 1,500 | 180 | | | 1,320 |
| Irrecoverable debts expense | 400 | | 1,200 | 430 | 1,170 | |
| Suspense | | 250 | 250 | | | |
| **Total of the adjustments** | | | **1,630** | **1,630** | | |

The £430 credit on the expense line is the £180 decrease plus the £250 recovery. Final expense = 400 + 1,200 − 180 − 250 = **£1,170**.

Profit = 20,000 − (1,170 − 400) = 20,000 − 770 = **£19,230**.

*Easy way to see it:* the expense line started at £400. It went up by the new write-off (£1,200), then came down by the fall in the allowance (£180) and the recovery (£250). The suspense account is cleared to nil.

### Example 5: Finding the cash received from customers

A business had opening receivables of **£20,000**, credit sales of **£90,000**, debts written off of **£1,000**, and closing receivables (after the write-off) of **£24,000**. The allowance rose by £400 in the year.

Cash received = 20,000 + 90,000 − 1,000 − 24,000 = **£85,000**.

*Why the write-off is subtracted:* the £1,000 left the receivables account without any cash arriving. *Why the allowance is ignored:* it was never in the receivables account, so it does not change the cash.

## 8.5 Exam traps

1. **Charging the whole allowance instead of the change.** If the allowance goes from £2,700 to £3,000, only **£300** is charged. The full £3,000 is not an expense of this year.
2. **Using the wrong receivables figure.** The allowance is a percentage of the receivables **after** the write-offs, and **before** deducting the allowance. Calculate the write-offs first.
3. **Putting the allowance in the wrong place.** The allowance is **not** posted to the trade receivables account, and it is **not** a liability. It is deducted from receivables in the statement of financial position.
4. **Wrong entry for a recovery.** Cash from a customer already written off is credited to **irrecoverable debts expense**, not to trade receivables and not to sales.
5. **Writing off too early.** A part payment, a disputed invoice, or a dishonoured cheque does **not** automatically mean the debt is irrecoverable. A dishonoured cheque is first put back into receivables (Dr Trade receivables, Cr Cash at bank).
6. **Thinking gross profit changes.** Irrecoverable debts and allowance changes are administrative expenses. They change **net profit**, never revenue or gross profit.
7. **Reading the sign wrongly.** When recoveries and a fall in the allowance are bigger than the write-offs, the net amount is a **credit** (income). Always say debit or credit when asked.
8. **Cash flow confusion.** Write-offs and allowance movements involve **no cash**. Working out cash from net receivables (after the allowance) gives the wrong answer. Use the **gross** receivables account with the write-offs.

## 8.8 Answers and explanations

### Answers: extra practice questions

**Q1. Answer: C (£1,700)**

- **Why:** The write-off of £1,400 is a full expense. The new allowance needed is 5% of £60,000 = £3,000, but £2,700 is already there, so only the **increase of £300** is charged.
- **Formula:** 1,400 + (3,000 − 2,700) = **£1,700**.
- **Easy example:** Your tea tin should hold £3,000 but already holds £2,700. You only add the missing £300.
- **Why not the others:** A (£1,100) takes away the increase instead of adding it. B (£1,400) ignores the allowance. D (£4,400) charges the whole new allowance of £3,000 instead of the increase.

**Q2. Answer: A (£350 credit)**

- **Why:** The write-off (£900) is a debit. The recovery (£450) and the fall in the allowance (£800) are both credits. The credits are bigger, so the net figure is a **credit**, which adds to profit.
- **Formula:** 900 − 450 − (2,000 − 1,200) = 900 − 450 − 800 = **(350)**, a credit of £350.
- **Easy example:** You lose £900 on one customer, but you get back £450 and release £800 of caution money you no longer need. You end up £350 better off.
- **Why not the others:** B (£350 debit) has the right amount but the wrong side. C (£1,250 debit) adds the fall in the allowance as if it were an increase. D (£100 debit) forgets the £450 recovery.

**Q3. Answer: A and D**

- **Why:** A debt is irrecoverable when it is **not expected to be paid**. In A the liquidator confirms there will be no payment. In D the customer cannot be found and cannot be pursued.
- **Easy example:** A is like a shop owner who closes and leaves no money. D is a customer who has vanished with your goods.
- **Why not the others:** B is a payment problem that may be solved by presenting the cheque again; the debt should be reinstated, not written off. C is a dispute that may still be settled.

**Q4. Answer: B (£77,600)**

- **Why:** First write off the £4,000. Then calculate the allowance on what remains, £80,000, and deduct it.
- **Formula:** 84,000 − 4,000 = 80,000. Allowance = 80,000 × 3% = 2,400. Net = 80,000 − 2,400 = **£77,600**.
- **Easy example:** Remove the customer who will never pay from your list first. Then guess how many of the **remaining** customers might fail.
- **Why not the others:** A (£77,480) calculates the allowance on £84,000 (£2,520), before the write-off. C (£80,000) forgets to deduct the allowance. D (£81,600) deducts only the allowance and forgets the write-off.

**Q5. Answer: A and D**

- **Why:** A lower allowance means a smaller expense (it is credited to irrecoverable debts expense), so **net profit rises**. The allowance is deducted from receivables, so a smaller deduction means **net receivables rise**.
- **Formula:** Net profit up by £800; net receivables up by £800.
- **Easy example:** You decide you do not need such a big safety cushion. The cushion shrinks, so profit and the net asset figure both go up.
- **Why not the others:** B is wrong because irrecoverable debts expense is not part of gross profit. C is wrong because the allowance account is debited; the trade receivables account is not used at all.

**Q6. Answer: A**

- **Why:** The write-off credits trade receivables with £900. The allowance changes from £1,800 to 5% × £42,000 = £2,100, an **increase of £300**. The expense is 900 + 300 = £1,200.
- **Formula:** Dr Irrecoverable debts expense 1,200 = Cr Trade receivables 900 + Cr Allowance 300.
- **Easy example:** Cross out the bad customer (£900). Then top up the cushion by the £300 that is missing.
- **Why not the others:** B charges the whole new allowance (£2,100) instead of the increase. C leaves the debt in trade receivables and credits the allowance instead. D treats the £300 as a decrease (900 − 300 = 600), which is the wrong direction.

**Q7. Answer: B (£202,500)**

- **Why:** Cash is the balancing figure in the receivables account. The write-off took £2,500 out of receivables without any cash coming in. The allowance is not in the receivables account, so it does not matter.
- **Formula:** 30,000 + 210,000 − 2,500 − 35,000 = **£202,500**.
- **Easy example:** Of the money that "disappeared" from the customer list, some was paid, and some (£2,500) was simply crossed out.
- **Why not the others:** A (£202,250) takes the £250 allowance increase off, but the allowance involves no cash. C (£205,000) ignores the write-off. D (£207,500) adds the write-off instead of subtracting it.

**Q8. Answer: A (£23,850)**

- **Why:** (i) The write-off is an extra expense: profit falls by £1,200. (ii) The £300 recovery is credited to irrecoverable debts expense: profit rises by £300. (iii) A dishonoured cheque is put back into receivables. There is **no effect on profit**. (iv) The increase in the allowance is an expense: profit falls by £250.
- **Formula:** 25,000 − 1,200 + 300 − 250 = **£23,850**.
- **Easy example:** The cheque is like a letter returned for a missing stamp. You post it again; you do not close the customer's account.
- **Why not the others:** B (£23,450) also writes off the £400 cheque. C (£24,100) ignores the allowance increase. D (£23,250) treats the £300 recovery as an expense.

**Q10. Answer (Tahmina Traders)**

- Closing allowance = 52,000 × 5% = **£2,600**.
- Opening allowance £2,880, so the allowance **decreases** by 2,880 − 2,600 = £280.
- Irrecoverable debts expense = 3,500 − 600 − 280 = **£2,620** (a debit, so an expense).

| Trade receivables account: debit side | £ | Credit side | £ |
|---|---:|---|---:|

| | | Balance c/d | 52,000 |
| | 358,000 | | 358,000 |

**Requirement 3: total cash from customers**

302,500 + 600 (recovery, banked but not in the receivables account) = **£303,100**.

- Net receivables at 1 April 20X5 = 48,000 − 2,880 = 45,120. Net receivables at 31 March 20X6 = 52,000 − 2,600 = 49,400.
- Assistant's answer = 45,120 + 310,000 − 49,400 = **£305,720**.
- Difference from the correct £302,500 = 305,720 − 302,500 = **£3,220**.
- Why: he counts the **£3,500 written off** as if it were cash received, and he lets the **£280 fall in the allowance** reduce his answer. Neither involves cash: 3,500 − 280 = 3,220.

| | £ |
|---|---:|
| Credit sales | 310,000 |
| Less: irrecoverable debts expense | (2,620) |

### Answers: true or false

| # | Answer | Reason and easy example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **False** | The entry is the other way round: **debit** irrecoverable debts expense, **credit** trade receivables. The customer owes less (credit receivables) and we record a cost (debit expense). |
| 2 | **True** | Dr Cash at bank, Cr Irrecoverable debts expense. The debt was already removed from receivables, so receivables is not touched. It is like finding money you had given up for lost. |
| 3 | **False** | The allowance is **deducted from trade receivables** (a current asset). It is not a liability. It is a reduction in the value of an asset. |
| 4 | **True** | First write off the debts that will not be paid. Then apply the percentage to what remains. |
| 5 | **False** | The sale did happen, so revenue and gross profit stay the same. A write-off is an administrative expense and reduces **net profit** only. |
| 6 | **False** | A dishonoured cheque may be an administrative problem. Unless told otherwise, **reinstate** the debt (Dr Trade receivables, Cr Cash at bank). It is not automatically irrecoverable. |
| 7 | **True** | The entry is Dr Irrecoverable debts expense, Cr Allowance for receivables. The trade receivables account is not used. |
| 8 | **True** | The reduction is debited to the allowance account and credited to irrecoverable debts expense. A smaller expense means a higher net profit. |
