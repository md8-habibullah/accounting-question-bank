# Chapter 15: Introduction to Public Financial Management

## 15.1 Start here: the idea in plain English

Imagine your neighbourhood forms a small committee to look after the street lights and the children's playground. Every household pays a monthly contribution. At a general meeting the members agree a **plan**: how much will be collected and what it may be spent on. The treasurer may spend only on the agreed items. At the end of the year the treasurer shows the members where every taka went, and a trusted outsider checks the books.

A government works in the same way, only much bigger. The **public sector** is all levels of government plus the enterprises that the government controls. It does not exist to make a profit. It exists to serve the citizens. The laws, organisations, systems and procedures used to raise money, plan how to spend it, spend it, record it and have it checked are called **public financial management (PFM)**.

Because nobody "owns" a government, the question "did we make a profit?" is not the main test. The main test is **accountability**: was the public's money used as the legislature allowed, and can the government prove it? That is why this chapter has its own words (budget, fund, GPFR, IPSAS). This chapter is about 5% of the exam, and the workbook says the questions are knowledge-type multiple choice. So it is mostly **learning lists, definitions and "which is which"**. Learn everything in the order of the PFM cycle.

| Word | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Public sector** | All levels of government plus government-controlled enterprises |
| **Public (non-marketable) goods** | Services the government gives citizens, such as public education or law enforcement; people do not buy them in a market |
| **Marketable goods** | Goods citizens can buy from government-controlled enterprises |
| **PFM** | The laws, organisations, systems and procedures for securing and using public resources effectively, efficiently and transparently |
| **Budget** | A plan of revenues and expenditures, normally for one year |
| **Deficit** | Expenditure minus receipts |
| **MTBF** | Medium Term Budgetary Framework: the budget year plus projections for two later years |
| **Fund (government meaning)** | A separate fiscal and accounting entity with its own self-balancing accounts |
| **GPFR** | General purpose financial reports, for users who cannot demand reports made just for them |
| **IPSAS / IPSASB** | International Public Sector Accounting Standards / the board that issues them |
| **IFMIS** | Integrated Financial Management Information System: automation of budget and treasury work |

> **KEY FACT** The PFM cycle runs: fiscal framework, then budget formulation, then budget execution, then accounting and reporting, then external scrutiny and audit. In the workbook's summary diagram the legal framework sits in the centre of this cycle.

> **REMEMBER** A business asks "how much profit did we make?" A government asks "was the public money spent as approved, and can we show it?" Almost every fact in this chapter comes back to accountability.

## 15.2 Key facts to remember

- **Public sector**
    - All levels of government plus government-controlled enterprises. It provides **non-marketable** goods (public goods, such as public education, healthcare, law enforcement, the military, public transit and infrastructure) and **marketable** goods.
    - Its boundary changes with a country's economic, cultural and political context. It generally includes: core organisations at central, intermediate and local layers; state-owned commercial and non-commercial enterprises (SOEs); autonomous government agencies; and bodies outside the government that it regulates and operates.
    - Differences from the private sector (objective, ownership, funds, operation, goods): see the table in 15.3.
- **PFM**
    - Its five functions, around the annual budget cycle: (1) fiscal framework, (2) budget formulation, (3) budget execution, (4) accounting and reporting, (5) external scrutiny and audit. *(The workbook prints the last as "external security and audit", so an exam may print it that way. It means the outside check on the accounts. The workbook's opening overview lists only four functions and leaves out the fiscal framework, and its summary diagram calls (2) "budget preparation" and (5) "control and audit". Learn the five above.)*
    - PFM also covers taxes, other revenue, borrowing and debt, but its **main focus is expenditure management**, especially through public budgeting.
    - **Fiscal framework:** the national rules, regulations and procedures that influence how budget policy is planned, approved, carried out, monitored and evaluated.
    - **Budget execution** stages: authorisation, commitment, verification, payment order, payment, accounting. Line ministries and spending agencies carry it out within controls set by the ministry of finance.
    - **Audit** checks the accounts of all government departments, agencies and public bodies on behalf of taxpayers and reports to parliament as the law requires.
- **Legal framework**
    - It rests on tax laws, budget system laws, local government finance laws and the constitution, and varies by country.
    - If the constitution does not say so, the **budget system law (BSL)** should state that all taxation and spending rest on law.
    - The **constitution is at the highest level**. Regulations, orders and ministerial instructions explain the higher laws. If laws conflict, follow the constitution.
- **The budget**
    - A comprehensive plan of revenues and expenditures, usually for one year. Here "budget" means the central government budget authorised by the **legislature**. The legislature is generally supreme in budget matters. All taxation and spending must rest on law, and even revenue above the forecast cannot be spent without approval.
    - **Bangladesh:** Articles 87 and 89 of the Constitution guide the Annual Financial Statement (AFS), commonly called the budget. Article 10 of the Public Money and Budget Management Act 2009 (PMBA) requires the finance minister to place the budget in Parliament **before the start of each fiscal year**. Other rules used: the Rules of Procedure of Parliament 1973, the General Financial Rules 1951 (updated 1998) and the Secretariat Instruction on preparing the budget (1976, updated 2008).
    - **Eleven characteristics of a good budget system** (alphabetical in the workbook): accountability, annual basis, authoritativeness, balance, common pooling of revenues, comprehensiveness, performance, specificity, stability, transparency, unity. Meanings are in 15.3.
- **Two approaches to budgeting**
    - **Input / means oriented** (traditional): basic approach and builds a culture of discipline; but a heavy bureaucratic process.
    - **Result oriented** (modern): reflects the actual objective of budgeting; but needs a solid institutional framework and its effectiveness depends on the country's context.
- **Budget preparation:** three phases (strategy setting; guidelines and submissions; review, negotiation and reconciliation), then approval by the legislature. Influencing factors: constitutional provisions, political context and traditions, and available resources.
    - **Strategy setting** considers: limits on total expenditure (in line with medium-term fiscal objectives); sectoral priorities for services and public investment; ceilings on expenditure for spending ministries; assumptions and fiscal risks.
    - **Guidelines and submissions:** budget circulars (macro-fiscal targets, priorities, ceilings and procedure) and ministries submitting budgets within their ceilings.
    - **Review, negotiation and reconciliation:** the ministry of finance (MOF) checks submissions for compliance with policy, limits and effectiveness, negotiates revisions, and reconciles final submissions with the ceilings. The Cabinet or President approves the draft budget. The full activity table is in 15.3.
- **Bangladesh budget practice**
    - The Ministry of Finance mainly prepares the budget and places it before Parliament. Three divisions are involved: the Finance Division, the Economic Relations Division and the Internal Resources Division (National Board of Revenue). The Planning Commission, line ministries, divisions and agencies also take part.
    - The Finance Division prepares the **Medium Term Macroeconomic Framework (MTMF)** to forecast resources. A Coordination Council chaired by the Finance Minister approves it and fixes total resources. The total is shared among ministries, divisions and institutions (MDIs) as ceilings (spending limits), sent through **Budget Circular 1 (BC1)**, which is issued in **December** each year as the first official document for preparing the budget.
    - MDIs prepare detailed budgets inside the ceilings. The Finance Division, the Planning Commission and the ministry review them in a meeting, and **BC2** communicates the final ceiling.
    - **MTBF** started in 2005-06 as the mechanism of result-oriented budgeting. It covers the proposed budget year plus projections for **two outer years** (operating and development parts), rolled forward each year. Before 2006 the budget was single-year, top-down and input-based. The result-based approach was adopted in FY2006. *(The workbook gives the start both as "2005-06" and as "FY2006". Treat them as the same start. Its own answer to Interactive Question 2 says 2005-06, so use that year.)* PFM reform with development partners has run since 1990.
    - **Basic concepts:** revenue = tax + non-tax. Expenditure = revenue (running) spending + development spending. Expenditure − receipts = deficit. A deficit is financed by borrowing from **external** sources (loans, grants, project aid) or **internal** sources (banks and non-bank sources).
- **Budget classification**
    - Why it matters: policy formulation and analysis; compliance with legislative authorisations and financial regulations; day-to-day administration. It is a normative framework for policy decisions and for parliamentary and financial accountability, and it works as a basis for accounting (budget and accounting are integrated).
    - Codes: revenue codes follow economic categories. Expenditure codes are used at **every stage** from appropriation to payment. Codes are often decimal and hierarchical (ministry, directorate, division).
    - A basic expenditure classification has at least an **administrative** classification, an **object (line-item)** classification (usually by economic category) and, in aid-dependent countries, a **financing source** classification.
    - Modern systems are **multidimensional**, with at least four dimensions: **administrative unit, function, economic feature, programme**. Classification by economic feature is one of the most important for central government. Bangladesh's Ministry of Finance has a "Budget and Accounting Classification Manual" and prepares the budget through automation.
    - International standards for fiscal reporting: **GFSM 2001** (IMF) and the **Classification of Functions of Government (COFOG)**.
- **Public sector accounting and reporting**
    - Accounting comes **after budgeting and before auditing**. Its **objective is accountability for spending public money**. Under IPSAS, reports must be useful for accountability and for decision-making.
    - Practice varies: some countries use cash basis, some accrual, some are moving from cash to accrual. Governments and not-for-profit bodies keep accounts on a **fund basis**.
    - The **IPSASB** (set up under IFAC) issues **IPSAS**: **cash basis** (only one standard; few governments use it because it needs consolidated statements for all controlled bodies) and **accrual basis** (revenue, cost, assets, liabilities and equity, not only cash flow; most based on an equivalent IFRS). The IPSASB also issues Recommended Practice Guidelines, studies and research papers. The workbook says the IPSASB was established in May 1986 and began the IPSAS project in late 1996. The USA's GASB sets standards for US state and local governments.
    - IPSAS applies to entities that (a) deliver services for the public and/or redistribute income and wealth, (b) mainly finance themselves by taxes, transfers, social contributions, debt or fees, and (c) have no primary objective of profit. It does not apply to immaterial items.
    - The Conceptual Framework covers public sector entities **other than** government business enterprises (GBEs). It gives guidance where IPSAS is silent but does **not override** IPSAS.
    - Public sector characteristics the IPSASB considered in developing it: non-exchange transactions; approved budgets (often a constitutional duty to publish a budget approved by the legislature); the nature and longevity of programmes (many are long term and need future taxation); the nature and purpose of assets and liabilities (held for service potential, not cash flows); the regulatory role of public sector entities; and the relationship to statistical reporting (government finance statistics).
- **GPFR and users**
    - GPFR give information on financial performance, financial position and cash flows. **Primary users:** service recipients and their representatives, and resource providers and their representatives. Others: statisticians, analysts, the media, financial advisers, lobby groups. A reporting entity can be a government, organisation, programme or activity without a separate legal identity.
    - Information needs: **lenders and creditors** want to judge liquidity and whether repayment will be as agreed; **donors** want to know whether resources are used economically, efficiently, effectively and as intended; **resource providers** generally want to know whether the entity met its objectives, paid for current operations from current-period funds, and will need more or fewer resources later.
    - Uses of public sector reports (workbook list): statutory compliance; stewardship of assets; accountability and performance; planning future policy and funding bids; viability and value for money; public relations; a source of facts and figures.
    - Typical users (the workbook's answer to Interactive Question 7): funders and financial supporters, politicians, the public, service users, senior civil servants, lenders and suppliers, and employees.
    - A complete set under IPSAS: (a) statement of financial position, (b) statement of financial performance, (c) statement of changes in net assets/equity, (d) cash flow statement, (e) when the approved budget is made public, a comparison of budget and actual amounts, (f) notes, (g) comparative information.
- **Funds and the two kinds of statements**
    - A government fund is a separate fiscal and accounting entity with its own resources, liabilities, activity and self-balancing accounts, set up for specific activities under special rules or restrictions.
    - Three categories: **governmental, proprietary, fiduciary** (types in 15.3).
    - **Fund statements** show **fiscal accountability**; **government-wide statements** show **operational accountability** (Table 3.1 in 15.3). Governmental funds use **current financial resources** and **modified accrual**. Their balance sheet shows only current assets, current liabilities and the fund balance.
    - The workbook's self-test answer 22: internal service funds are generally reported in the **Business-type Activities** column of the government-wide statements (and in the proprietary fund statements).
    - **Dual track:** transactions are recorded in the funds during the year, then converted (often by spreadsheet) to the accrual basis for the government-wide statements. Used for governmental and proprietary funds, not fiduciary funds.
- **Bangladesh accounts**
    - Sections 80 to 92 of the Constitution deal with government accounts. Two funds: the **Consolidated Fund** and the **Public Account of the Republic**. Two annual reports: **Finance Accounts** and **Appropriation Accounts** (table in 15.3). Article 85 requires all payments to be made by parliamentary act or presidential rule.
    - The government promised to adopt **cash basis IPSAS** in 2017, using the iBAS++ system (Integrated Budget and Accounting Systems). As the Constitution and IPSAS ask for different things, a 2021 presidential order required the government's statements to follow reformed accounting (IPSAS) together with the constitutional requirements. The workbook's self-test answer: government accounting in Bangladesh follows the **cash basis**.
- **IFMIS:** automation solutions to plan, execute and monitor the budget; they record all transactions, apply controls and track financial events. The first step is usually the general ledger and chart of accounts. Automation is **a means to an end, not an end in itself**: it does not by itself guarantee transparent, accountable public finances.

## 15.3 Formulas and layouts

*Money in this chapter is shown in £ to match the rest of this book. The workbook's own examples use BDT (taka). The arithmetic is identical. All country and council names below are invented.*

> **FORMULA** Revenue (receipts) = Tax revenue + Non-tax revenue

> **FORMULA** Total expenditure = Revenue (running) expenditure + Development expenditure

> **FORMULA** Deficit = Total expenditure − Receipts (a negative answer is a surplus)

> **FORMULA** Deficit financing: Deficit = External sources (loans, grants, project aid) + Internal sources (banks, non-bank)

> **FORMULA** Budget variance = Approved budget − Actual (background: for spending, a positive answer means under-spent)

> **FORMULA** Budget used (%) = Actual ÷ Approved budget × 100 (background)

> **FORMULA** Accrual-basis revenue = Cash received − Opening amount owed to us + Closing amount owed to us (Chapter 9 idea)

> **FORMULA** Fund balance (governmental fund) = Current assets − Current liabilities

> **FORMULA** Agency fund: Total assets = Total liabilities, so net assets = 0

> **FORMULA** MTBF window = Budget year + 2 outer years (3 years in all)

**Public sector against private sector**

| Area | Public sector | Private sector |
|---|---|---|
| Objective | Serve the citizens of the country | Earn profit from business |
| Ownership | Owned by, and operates on behalf of, the public | Owned by individuals or entities |
| Source of funds | Tax, excise and other duties, bonds, treasury bills | Owners' capital, loans, shares, debentures |
| Operation | For the common benefit of the people; important for state policy | For the profit of the owner |
| Goods provided | Mostly public (non-marketable) goods; marketable goods often | Marketable goods to individuals, businesses and organisations |

**Government against business accounting**

| Point | Business | Government (public sector) |
|---|---|---|
| Main aim of reporting | Profit and return to owners | Accountability for public money, and decisions by users |
| Meaning of "fund" | Working capital or part of it; one single accounting entity | A separate fiscal and accounting entity with its own self-balancing accounts |
| Statements | One set for the whole business | Two sets: fund statements and government-wide statements |
| Why assets are held | To generate cash flows | Mainly for service potential |
| Typical transactions | Exchange transactions | Many non-exchange transactions (value received without giving equal value) |
| Budget | An internal plan | Often a constitutional duty to publish a legislature-approved budget |
| Basis of accounting | Accrual | Varies: cash, accrual, and modified accrual in governmental funds |
| Standards | IFRS | IPSAS (cash basis and accrual basis) |

*The business column is background knowledge from earlier chapters, except the meaning of "fund", which the workbook gives. The government column is from the workbook.*

**The eleven characteristics of a good budget system (short meanings)**

| Characteristic | In a few words |
|---|---|
| Accountability | The executive reports to the legislature on fiscal performance; an independent external audit body reports yearly on budget execution |
| Annual basis | Authority is normally for 12 months; the law may allow exceptions (multi-year appropriations, year-end carry-overs) |
| Authoritativeness | Clear decision-making authority; no tax or spending without the legislature's approval |
| Balance | Well defined: payments balanced by receipts (cash basis) or expenses by revenues and financing (accrual basis); no financing gap |
| Common pooling of revenues | All resources are fungible and go into one common fund |
| Comprehensiveness | Clear scope (including extra-budgetary funds); all revenues and expenditures shown gross, not netted off |
| Performance | Past results and expected results of programmes are reported in the budget documents |
| Specificity | Revenues and expenditures are given in some detail |
| Stability | Short- and medium-term macro-fiscal stability and long-term sustainability are indicated |
| Transparency | Timely, regular, public budget information; clear roles for all authorities |
| Unity | One annual budget law approves all receipts and payments; no "dual" budget (current against capital) |

**Budget preparation: three phases**

| Phase | Activities (from the workbook's table) |
|---|---|
| Strategy setting | Ministry of Finance (MOF) prepares forecasts; MOF prepares fiscal strategy with ceilings; Cabinet approves the fiscal strategy and ceilings; legislature approves the fiscal strategy |
| Guidelines and submissions | MOF issues the budget circular; ministries submit budget requests within ceilings |
| Review, negotiation and reconciliation | MOF reviews and negotiates with ministries and reconciles submissions with ceilings; Cabinet (or President) approves the draft budget |
| Approval | Budget submitted to the legislature; legislature approves it |

**Funds: categories and types**

| Category | Type | What it accounts for (my wording) |
|---|---|---|
| Governmental | General Fund | Most day-to-day departmental running, such as police, parks, schools, administration. Unless another fund is required, use this one. A government has only one General Fund (it may carry another name) |
| Governmental | Special revenue fund | Taxes, grants or gifts legally restricted to a particular operating purpose, such as running a library |
| Governmental | Debt service fund | Money set aside to pay principal and interest on general long-term debt |
| Governmental | Capital projects fund | Money earmarked for building, for example roads, bridges or parks |
| Governmental | Permanent fund | An endowment: the gift's principal is invested and kept; the earnings are used for a public purpose |
| Proprietary | Internal service fund | Services to other departments, repaid at cost, such as central purchasing, a motor pool or data processing |
| Proprietary | Enterprise fund | Services to the public for a fee that is the fund's main income, such as a municipal swimming pool |
| Fiduciary | Agency fund | Cash held for an outside party, such as tax collected for another government. Assets = liabilities |
| Fiduciary | Investment trust fund | Outside participants' share of a government's investment pool |
| Fiduciary | Pension trust fund | Assets held in trust to pay retirement benefits to employees |
| Fiduciary | Private-purpose trust fund | Trust for the benefit of private people, such as a scholarship fund |

**Table 3.1: the two kinds of accountability**

| | Operational accountability | Fiscal accountability |
|---|---|---|
| Statements | Government-wide statements (governmental and business-type activities), plus proprietary and fiduciary fund statements | Governmental fund statements |
| Measurement focus | Flow of economic resources | Flow of current financial resources |
| Basis of accounting | Accrual basis | Modified accrual basis |
| Revenue recognised when | An exchange of economic resources occurs, or by IPSAS rules for non-exchange items such as taxes, contributions and grants | Measurable **and** available for current spending |
| Expenses (accrual) or expenditures (modified accrual) recognised when | An exchange of economic resources occurs (same rule as revenue) | An obligation to spend current financial resources is incurred |

*Note:* fiduciary activities are reported only in the **fiduciary fund statements** (statement of fiduciary net assets and statement of changes in fiduciary net assets), **not** in the government-wide statements.

**Bangladesh: funds and annual reports**

| Item | Contents |
|---|---|
| **Consolidated Fund** | All tax and non-tax revenue receipts; all domestic borrowing (except national savings schemes) and external borrowing; food account receipts and similar |
| **Public Account of the Republic** | State provident fund; national savings schemes; renewal, reserve and depreciation fund; deposit accounts and similar |
| **Finance Accounts** | The government's annual financial statements: total receipts and payments of the year (cash basis) |
| **Appropriation Accounts** | Government expenditure compared with the approved budget |

## 15.4 Worked examples

### Example 1: Budget totals, deficit, financing and the MTBF

The Government of Zedland (invented) plans the following for 20X5, in £ million: tax revenue 640, non-tax revenue 110, running (revenue) expenditure 560, development expenditure 330. It will finance the deficit by external sources 85 (loans, grants and project aid) and internal sources 55 (bank loans 40, non-bank loans 15).

1. Receipts = 640 + 110 = **750**.
2. Total expenditure = 560 + 330 = **890**.
3. Deficit = 890 − 750 = **140**.
4. Financing check: external 85 + internal (40 + 15 = 55) = **140**. It agrees, so there is no financing gap.
5. MTBF: the proposed budget year is 20X5 and the framework also projects **20X6 and 20X7**. A year later it rolls forward: 20X6 becomes the budget year, 20X7 stays, and **20X8 is added**.

**Answer: deficit £140 million, financed £85 million externally and £55 million internally.**

*Easy way to see it:* a household plans to earn 750 and spend 890. The missing 140 must be borrowed, either from abroad or at home.

### Example 2: Approved budget against actual spending

A ministry's approved budget and actual spending for 20X5, in £ million. Variance = budget − actual.

| Line | Approved budget | Actual | Variance | Budget used |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Salaries | 420 | 438 | (18) | 104.29% |
| Buildings | 300 | 255 | 45 | 85.00% |
| Supplies | 80 | 61 | 19 | 76.25% |
| **Total** | **800** | **754** | **46** | **94.25%** |

A figure in brackets means **over-spent**. Salaries exceeded the approved amount by 18, a point the report would draw attention to. Buildings and supplies are under-spent by 45 and 19. In total the ministry spent £46 million less than approved. *(This kind of comparison of spending with the approved budget is what the workbook says the Appropriation Accounts focus on.)*

**Answer: total under-spent by £46 million; salaries over-spent by £18 million.**

### Example 3: Cash basis and accrual basis for a district council

Hillview District Council (invented), in £000. It collected taxes of 1,500 in cash and paid 800 in cash for road maintenance. Taxes owed by citizens were 100 at the start and 140 at the end. Unpaid maintenance invoices were 60 at the start and 90 at the end.

1. Accrual revenue = 1,500 − 100 + 140 = **1,540**.
2. Accrual expense = 800 − 60 + 90 = **830**.

| £000 | Cash basis | Accrual basis |
|---|---:|---:|
| Taxes | 1,500 | 1,540 |
| Road maintenance | (800) | (830) |
| **Result** | **700** | **710** |

The results differ for the Chapter 9 reason. Revenue is 40 higher because the taxes owed to the council rose from 100 to 140. Expenses are 30 higher because the council's unpaid invoices rose from 60 to 90. Net: the accrual result is 10 higher.

**Answer: cash-basis result 700; accrual-basis result 710.**

### Example 4: Dual track for a loan and a purchase

A town council (invented) borrows £480,000 from a bank and pays £450,000 in cash for equipment (a long-lived asset). Entries in £000:

| Step | General Fund (fund statements) | Government-wide statements |
|---|---|---|
| Loan received | Dr Cash 480; Cr Other financing sources: loan 480 | Dr Cash 480; Cr Loan payable 480 |
| Equipment bought | Dr Expenditures: capital outlay 450; Cr Cash 450 | Dr Equipment 450; Cr Cash 450 |

| Result (£000) | General Fund | Government-wide |
|---|---:|---:|
| Cash | 30 | 30 |
| Equipment | nil | 450 |
| Loan payable | nil | (480) |
| Net result (fund balance / net assets) | 30 | nil |

The fund records only the flow of current financial resources. It sees +480 of financing and 450 of spending, so its balance rises by 30. The government-wide statements record the asset and the debt as a business would, so net assets do not change.

**Answer: fund balance up £30,000; government-wide net assets unchanged.**

## 15.5 Exam traps

1. **The objective of the public sector.** It is to serve the citizens and give common benefits, not to earn profit. "To earn profit" is the private sector. Do not be tempted by "All of the above".
2. **"Who prepares the budget?"** The workbook's overview and self-test answer say the **legislature** (it authorises and approves; the legislature is supreme in budget matters). In Bangladesh practice the Ministry of Finance prepares the draft and places it before Parliament, which approves it. Read the wording. If the options are citizens, councillors, a board or the legislature, choose the legislature.
3. **Which law wins?** The **constitution** is at the top. A presidential order or a tax law does not beat it.
4. **Traditional against modern budgeting.** "Basic approach" and "builds a culture of discipline" are the good points of the **traditional** (input / means) approach. "Reflects the actual objective" is the good point of the **result-oriented** (modern) approach. "Needs a solid institutional framework" is the weakness of the modern approach; "heavy bureaucratic process" is the weakness of the traditional one.
5. **Timetable facts.** **BC1** (first official document, **December**) starts the process. **BC2** comes after the review and communicates the **final** ceiling. The MTBF covers the budget year **plus two outer years**, not just one year.
6. **Classification against standards.** The four dimensions are administrative unit, function, economic feature, programme. The international standards for fiscal reporting named in the budget-classification section are **GFSM 2001** and **COFOG**. IPSAS and IFRS are accounting standards, not the answer to "fiscal reporting standard".
7. **The word "fund" and the two sets of statements.** In government a fund is a separate accounting entity, not working capital. **Fund statements = modified accrual, current financial resources, fiscal accountability. Government-wide = accrual, economic resources, operational accountability.** Capital assets of governmental activities appear in the **government-wide** statements only. Fiduciary items appear in the **fiduciary fund** statements, not in the government-wide statements. An agency fund has **no net assets**.
8. **Bangladesh basics.** Government accounting is on the **cash basis** (the workbook's self-test answer). There are two funds (Consolidated Fund, Public Account) and two annual reports. If a self-test lists special revenue, debt service and Consolidated Fund, the Bangladesh fund is the **Consolidated Fund**. If the options are accrual, cash, modified accrual or "all of the above", the workbook's answer is **cash basis**. **Finance Accounts** = all receipts and payments. **Appropriation Accounts** = spending against the approved budget. Do not swap them.

## 15.8 Answers and explanations

### Answers: extra practice questions

**Q1. Answer: A and C**

- **Why:** The public sector is all levels of government plus government-controlled enterprises. That includes core organisations such as ministries and state-owned commercial and non-commercial enterprises.
- **Easy example:** A ministry and a state-owned bus company both answer to the government. A neighbour's factory does not.
- **Why not the others:** B is owned by one family, D by private shareholders. Both are private sector.

**Q2. Answer: B (taxes and excise duties)**

- **Why:** The public sector's funds come from tax, excise and other duties, bonds, treasury bills and others. A private business gets its capital from its owners, loans, shares and debentures.
- **Easy example:** A government can collect a tax from citizens. A tea stall can only ask its owner or a lender for money.
- **Why not the others:** A (shares), C (owner's capital) and D (debentures) are the private sector's sources.

**Q3. Answer: B (after budgeting and before auditing)**

- **Why:** Budgeting sets the plan, accounting records what actually happened, and audit then checks the accounts. Accounting and reporting is one of the five PFM functions.
- **Easy example:** The neighbourhood committee agrees a plan, the treasurer records what was spent, and an outsider then checks the records.
- **Why not the others:** A puts the record before the plan. C: an audit needs finished accounts to check. D: accounting is inside the cycle, as function (4) of five.

**Q4. Answer: A (£120m)**

- **Why:** Add the two receipts, add the two kinds of spending, then subtract.
- **Formula:** Receipts = 520 + 80 = 600. Expenditure = 450 + 270 = 720. Deficit = 720 − 600 = **£120m**.
- **Easy example:** You earn 600 and plan to spend 720. You are 120 short.
- **Why not the others:** B (£150m) leaves out development expenditure (600 − 450). C (£200m) leaves out non-tax revenue (720 − 520). D (£1,320m) adds receipts and expenditure together.

**Q5. Answer: A and C**

- **Why:** Result-oriented budgeting reflects the actual objective of budgeting but needs a solid institutional framework, and its effectiveness depends on the country's context.
- **Easy example:** A school judged on how well pupils learn (the result) needs good teachers and systems first. Judging only the hours spent is simpler but misses the point.
- **Why not the others:** B and D are about the **traditional** input / means approach: a basic approach and a discipline culture (good), and heavy bureaucracy (bad).

**Q6. Answer: A**

- **Why:** Strategy setting includes the ministry of finance preparing forecasts and a fiscal strategy with ceilings, the Cabinet approving the strategy and ceilings, and the legislature approving the fiscal strategy.
- **Easy example:** Before a family plans each room's spending, the family first agrees the total limit.
- **Why not the others:** B and C belong to **guidelines and submissions**. D (Cabinet approves the **draft budget**) belongs to **review, negotiation and reconciliation**. This is the tricky part: the Cabinet appears twice, in two different phases.

**Q7. Answer: A (BC1, issued in December each year)**

- **Why:** Budget Circular 1 is the first official document between the Finance Division and ministries to prepare a budget, and it is issued in December. BC2 follows later and communicates the finalised resource ceiling.
- **Easy example:** The first letter says "here is your limit". The second says "after discussion, here is your final limit."
- **Why not the others:** B has the right month but the wrong document: BC2 comes later, after the review, to give the final ceiling. C has the wrong timing: BC1 starts the preparation, long before Parliament approves anything. D has the wrong document and order: the MTMF is approved first, then ceilings go out through BC1.

**Q8. Answer: C (economic feature, object or line item)**

- **Why:** Codes such as salaries, electricity and equipment say what kind of cost a payment is. That is the economic (object or line-item) classification.
- **Easy example:** A shopping list grouped as rice, oil and soap is grouped by the kind of item.
- **Why not the others:** A tells you which ministry or division is responsible. B tells you the purpose, such as health or education. D tells you which programme it supports.

**Q9. Answer: B**

- **Why:** The governmental fund only records the use of current financial resources, so the cash paid is spending (expenditure). The government-wide statements show the long-term effect, so the vehicle is an asset, as in a business.
- **Easy example:** The fund asks "how much cash did we use this year?" The government-wide view asks "what do we now own?"
- **Why not the others:** A reverses the two. C and D treat both records the same, which ignores the dual-track idea.

**Q10. Answer: B**

- **Why:** Finance Accounts show the total receipts and payments of the year. Appropriation Accounts focus on spending compared with the approved budget.
- **Easy example:** Finance Accounts are the bank passbook (all money in and out). Appropriation Accounts are the list of what the plan allowed against what was actually spent.
- **Why not the others:** A and D each give one report the other report's job. C is wrong because the workbook says the Finance Accounts are prepared on the cash basis.

**Q11. Answer (Government of Taraland)**

**Requirement 1: variances (budget − actual), £ million**

| Line | Budget | Actual | Variance | Description |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|
| Tax revenue | 4,800 | 4,560 | 240 | Shortfall (less received than planned) |
| Non-tax revenue | 700 | 780 | (80) | Excess (more received than planned) |
| **Total receipts** | **5,500** | **5,340** | **160** | **Shortfall** |
| Running expenditure | 3,900 | 3,846 | 54 | Under-spend |
| Development expenditure | 2,600 | 2,240 | 360 | Under-spend |
| **Total expenditure** | **6,500** | **6,086** | **414** | **Under-spend** |

**Requirement 2: deficit and financing**

| £ million | Budget | Actual |
|---|---:|---:|
| Total expenditure | 6,500 | 6,086 |
| Total receipts | (5,500) | (5,340) |
| **Deficit** | **1,000** | **746** |
| External sources | 600 | 410 |
| Internal: bank | 300 | 270 |
| Internal: non-bank | 100 | 66 |
| **Total financing** | **1,000** | **746** |
| External share of deficit | 60% | 55% |

Actual: 410 ÷ 746 = 54.96%, which is 55%. The actual deficit is **254** smaller than planned.

**Requirement 3:** (a) administrative unit; (b) function; (c) economic feature (object or line item); (d) programme.

**Requirement 4:** The **Appropriation Accounts**. They focus on government expenditure compared with the approved budget, which is what the expenditure lines of the table do. (The Finance Accounts show total receipts and payments.)

**Requirement 5:** Next round: **20X6** as the budget year, with outer years **20X7 and 20X8**. The 20X7 projection is kept and updated, and 20X8 is the new outer year.

*Marking tips:* the trap is the meaning of a positive variance. For receipts, budget minus actual positive means a **shortfall** (bad news). For spending, it means an **under-spend**. Do not treat both the same.

*Check:* deficit difference 1,000 − 746 = 254 = expenditure under-spend 414 − receipts shortfall 160. Financing 410 + 270 + 66 = 746 agrees to the actual deficit.

| **Total receipts** | **1,350** |

| Item | Working | £000 |

| Wages | 520 − 20 + 35 | (535) |

| | £000 |
|---|---:|

| Wages: more owed at year-end (+35 − 20) | (15) |
| Insurance: bigger prepayment (+12 − 8) | 4 |

| **Accrual surplus** | **328** |

Check: 334 + 20 − 15 + 4 − 15 = 328, which agrees to requirement 2. (Cash operating result = 1,100 − 520 − 210 − 36 = 334.)

**Requirement 4: dual-track records, £000**

| | General Fund | Government-wide |
|---|---|---|
| Loan | Dr Cash 250; Cr Other financing sources: loan 250 | Dr Cash 250; Cr Loan payable 250 |
| Equipment | Dr Expenditures: capital outlay 270; Cr Cash 270 | Dr Equipment 270; Cr Cash 270 |
| Depreciation (as in a business) | None: the fund does not record long-lived assets | Dr Depreciation expense 15; Cr Accumulated depreciation 15 |

*Effect:* The General Fund balance falls by **20** (250 − 270). Government-wide net assets fall by **15** only, which is the depreciation. Working: equipment 255 (270 − 15) plus the cash movement (250 − 270 = (20)) gives assets of 235. The loan liability is 250. Net assets change = 235 − 250 = (15).

**Requirement 5: comparison**

| Point | Cash-basis summary | Government-wide statements (accrual) |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 1,100 collected | 1,120 earned (adds 20 more owed by citizens) |
| Loan | Part of receipts (250) | A liability of 250, not revenue |
| Equipment | A payment of 270 | An asset of 270, less depreciation 15 = 255 |
| What it shows | Cash received, cash paid, cash balance: transparent about the cash only | Revenue, cost, assets and liabilities: the full cost of running the council and what it owns and owes |
| What is missing | Amounts owed to and by the council (60 taxes, 35 wages), the prepaid 12, the equipment, the loan | These are all shown, but it needs much fuller records (background) |

*Primary users:* **service recipients and their representatives**, and **resource providers and their representatives** (the resource providers include lenders, creditors and donors).

*Marking tips:* the traps are treating the loan as revenue, treating the equipment as an expense of the year, and charging twelve months of depreciation instead of six.

*Check:* the cash operating result 334 less net accrual adjustments of 6 (+20 − 15 + 4 − 15) equals the accrual surplus 328.
