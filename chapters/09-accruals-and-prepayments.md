# Chapter 9: Accruals and prepayments

## 9.1 Start here: the idea in plain English

Imagine you pay your gym **£120 on 1 January for the whole year**. Is the whole £120 a January cost? No. You use the gym a little each month, so each month "uses up" £10 of what you paid. At the end of March you have used £30 and still hold **£90 of gym membership you have paid for but not yet used**.

Now the opposite. You use electricity in November and December, but the bill arrives in January. You have **already used the power, but not yet paid for it**. The cost belongs to November and December, even though the cash leaves your bank next year.

Accounting follows the same common sense. This is called the **accruals basis** (also called the *matching* idea):

> **REMEMBER** A cost goes into the statement of profit or loss in the period it is **used or incurred**, not in the period it is **paid**. Income goes into the period it is **earned**, not the period the cash arrives.

Two new words you must know:

| Word | Plain meaning | What it is in the accounts |
|---|---|---|
| **Accrual** (accrued expense) | Used, **not yet paid** | A **liability** (we owe it) |
| **Prepayment** | Paid, **not yet used** | An **asset** (we have a right to future benefit) |

And the same two ideas on the **income** side:

| Word | Plain meaning | What it is in the accounts |
|---|---|---|
| **Accrued income** | Earned, **not yet received** | An **asset** (someone owes us) |
| **Deferred income** (income received in advance) | Received, **not yet earned** | A **liability** (we owe them the service) |

> **KEY FACT** Memory trick: **P**repayment = **P**aid first = **P**ositive for the balance sheet (an asset). **A**ccrual = **A**rrives later = **A**mount owed (a liability).

## 9.2 Key facts to remember

- **Accrued expense:** Debit the expense, credit **Accruals** (current liability). Profit goes **down**.
- **Prepayment:** Debit **Prepayments** (current asset), credit the expense. Profit goes **up** (the expense is smaller).
- **Accrued income:** Debit **Accrued income** (current asset), credit the income account. Profit goes **up**.
- **Deferred income:** Debit the income account, credit **Deferred income** (current liability). Profit goes **down**.
- In the **statement of financial position** (balance sheet):
    - prepayments and accrued income sit in **trade and other receivables** (current assets);
    - accruals and deferred income sit in **trade and other payables** (current liabilities).
- **Opening balances reverse in the new year.** Last year's accrual is "used up" when the bill is paid this year. Last year's prepayment is "used up" as the new year starts.
- A closing accrual or prepayment of this year **becomes the opening balance of next year**.
- Only adjust for amounts that matter (**materiality**). A £3 prepayment is normally ignored.
- A **time apportionment** is simply "months used ÷ months paid for". Always count the months carefully.

## 9.3 Formulas and layouts

> **FORMULA** Expense in the statement of profit or loss = Cash paid + Closing accrual − Opening accrual − Closing prepayment + Opening prepayment

> **FORMULA** Income in the statement of profit or loss = Cash received + Closing accrued income − Opening accrued income − Closing deferred income + Opening deferred income

> **FORMULA** Prepayment at year-end = Amount paid × (months NOT yet used ÷ total months paid for)

> **FORMULA** Accrual at year-end = Cost per month × number of months used but unpaid

> **FORMULA** Cash paid (working backwards) = Expense + Closing prepayment − Opening prepayment − Closing accrual + Opening accrual

**The T-account (ledger) layout.** Use it when a question gives you the balances and asks for "the amount transferred to profit or loss". Put the known items in, then the missing figure is the balancing figure.

| Electricity account: debit side | £ | Credit side | £ |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Cash paid in the year | 4,150 | Opening accrual b/d | 420 |
| Closing accrual c/d | 510 | **Statement of profit or loss (balancing figure)** | **4,240** |
| | 4,660 | | 4,660 |

Check: 4,150 + 510 = 4,660. Then 4,660 − 420 = **4,240**. 

> **REMEMBER** In an expense account, a **closing accrual (c/d)** sits on the **same side as the cash paid** (it adds to the expense). A **closing prepayment (c/d)** sits on the **opposite side** (it takes away from the expense). Opening balances (b/d) are the other way round: an opening accrual is on the credit side, an opening prepayment on the debit side.

## 9.4 Worked examples

### Example 1: A prepayment (rent paid in advance)

Aisha runs a boutique. Her year ends on **31 December 20X1** (*20X1 just means "some year"*). On **1 October 20X1** she pays **£6,000** rent for the next **12 months**.

1. Months used by the year-end: October, November, December = **3 months**.
2. Rent expense for 20X1 = 6,000 × 3/12 = **£1,500**.
3. Prepayment at 31 December = 6,000 × 9/12 = **£4,500** (an asset).

Journal: Dr Prepayments £4,500, Cr Rent expense £4,500 (taking the unused part out of the expense).

### Example 2: An accrual (electricity)

Cash paid for electricity in 20X2 was **£3,400**. At 1 January 20X2 there was an **opening accrual of £400** (the last bill of 20X1, paid in January 20X2). At 31 December 20X2 the Oct–Dec bill of **£520** was **unpaid**.

Expense = 3,400 − 400 + 520 = **£3,520**.

*Easy way to see it:* of the £3,400 paid, £400 was paying last year's bill (so it is not a 20X2 cost). But £520 of 20X2 electricity is still unpaid (so it must be added).

### Example 3: Opening and closing prepayments together (insurance)

Cash paid for insurance in the year: **£5,400**. Prepayment at the start: **£900**. Prepayment at the end: **£1,100**.

Expense = 5,400 + 900 − 1,100 = **£5,200**.

*Why:* the opening £900 was paid last year but is used up this year (add). The closing £1,100 is paid this year but belongs to next year (subtract).

### Example 4: The income side

A company rents out a spare room. Cash received in the year: **£12,000**. Opening deferred income: **£2,000** (received last year for this year). Closing deferred income: **£3,000** (received this year for next year).

Income = 12,000 + 2,000 − 3,000 = **£11,000**.

If instead there was **opening accrued income £1,000** and **closing accrued income £1,500** (and no deferred income), income = 12,000 − 1,000 + 1,500 = **£12,500**.

### Example 5: How it looks in the statements

| Extract: statement of financial position (current items) | £ |
|---|---:|
| Trade and other receivables: prepayments (rent £4,500) | 4,500 |
| Trade and other payables: accruals (electricity £520) | 520 |

| Extract: statement of profit or loss | £ |
|---|---:|
| Rent expense | 1,500 |
| Electricity expense | 3,520 |

## 9.5 Exam traps

1. **Mixing up the direction.** A prepayment is an **asset**, not a liability. An accrual is a **liability**, not an asset.
2. **Forgetting the opening balance.** Cash paid is never the expense by itself when there are opening/closing balances.
3. **Counting months wrongly.** From 1 October to 31 December is **3** months, not 2 or 4. Re-read the dates.
4. **Wrong effect on profit.** Accrual → profit **down**. Prepayment → profit **up**. Accrued income → profit **up**. Deferred income → profit **down**. A common trick question is "omitting an accrual": expenses too low, so profit is **overstated**.
5. **Mid-year price changes.** Rent or insurance that changes in the year must be split by month at the right rate.
6. **Wrong heading in the balance sheet.** Accruals are **payables**; prepayments are **receivables**. Neither is "inventory" or a "non-current asset".
7. **Mixing up "accrued income" and "deferred income".** Accrued = earned, not received (asset). Deferred = received, not earned (liability).

## 9.8 Answers and explanations

### Answers: extra practice questions

**Q1. Answer: B (£1,800)**

- **Why:** The policy runs 1 July 20X5 to 30 June 20X6. By 31 December, 6 months are used. 6 months are left.
- **Formula:** Prepayment = 3,600 × 6/12 = **£1,800**.
- **Easy example:** Pay £12 for a 12-month magazine. After 6 months you still hold £6 of magazines you have not received.
- **Why not the others:** A (£1,200) uses 4 months left. C (£2,400) uses 8 months left. D (£3,600) is the whole payment, ignoring the 6 months already used.

**Q2. Answer: C (£7,470)**

- **Why:** The opening accrual was last year's bill paid this year, so take it away. The closing accrual is this year's cost not yet paid, so add it.
- **Formula:** 7,300 − 640 + 810 = **£7,470**.
- **Easy example:** You paid £7,300. £640 of that cleared an old bill; £810 of this year's calls is still unpaid.
- **Why not the others:** A (£7,130) adds the opening and subtracts the closing: the wrong way round. B (£7,300) is just the cash. D (£8,110) ignores the opening accrual.

**Q3. Answer: B and C**

- **Why:** B is an accrual (we owe the supplier). C is deferred income (we owe the tenant the use of the property).
- **Easy example:** B is like an unpaid electricity bill sitting on your desk. C is like taking a deposit from someone for a service you have not yet given.
- **Why not the others:** A (rent paid in advance) is a **prepayment**, an asset. D (interest earned but not received) is **accrued income**, an asset.

**Q4. Answer: A (overstated by £500)**

- **Why:** Omitting the accrual makes expenses too low, so profit is **too high by £900**. Omitting the prepayment makes expenses too high, so profit is **too low by £400**.
- **Formula:** +900 − 400 = **overstated by £500**.
- **Easy example:** On paper your shop made £10,000. But you forgot an unpaid £900 phone bill (real profit is lower) and forgot that £400 of insurance belongs to next year (real profit is higher). The two mistakes partly cancel, so the paper profit is £500 too high.
- **Why not the others:** B reverses the direction. C and D add the two numbers instead of netting them.

**Q5. Answer: A**

- **Why:** The wages are an expense (debit). We owe them, so a liability (accruals) is created (credit). No cash has moved, so the bank is not involved.
- **Easy example:** It is like writing "pay the staff on Monday" in your diary. The promise is recorded before any money leaves your pocket.
- **Why not the others:** B reverses the entry. C credits the bank, but nothing has been paid. D treats it like a prepayment, which is the wrong idea.

**Q6. Answer: B**

- **Why:** The £4,800 covers 6 months, so £800 a month. November and December = 2 months = **£1,600** is earned. The remaining 4 months = **£3,200** is received in advance, a **liability** (deferred income).
- **Formula:** Income = 4,800 × 2/6 = 1,600. Deferred income = 4,800 × 4/6 = 3,200.
- **Easy example:** A tenant pays you six months up front. After two months you have earned two months. The other four months are like a gift voucher you still owe him.
- **Why not the others:** A counts all the cash as income. C uses 4 months for income. D calls the £3,200 an asset; but we have the cash already and owe the tenant a service, so it is a liability.

**Q7. Answer: B (trade and other receivables)**

- **Why:** A prepayment is a **current asset**. In the statement of financial position, amounts paid in advance are grouped with the amounts owed to the business under "trade and other receivables".
- **Easy example:** When you pay rent in advance, the landlord "owes" you the use of the shop. Things that other people owe you are receivables.
- **Why not the others:** A is for liabilities. C is for goods held for sale. D is for long-term assets such as buildings.

**Q8. Answer: C (£42,650)**

- **Why:** (i) The accrual is an extra expense, so profit falls by £600. (ii) The prepayment reduces the expense, so profit rises by £450. (iii) Accrued income is extra income, so profit rises by £300.
- **Formula:** 42,500 − 600 + 450 + 300 = **£42,650**.
- **Easy example:** Start with the profit you have. Take away every cost you forgot, and add every benefit you forgot.
- **Why not the others:** A takes away all three. B treats the income as an expense. D adds all three.

**Q9. Answer (Karim & Sons Hardware)**

**Requirement 1: amounts for the statement of profit or loss**

| Item | Working | £ |
|---|---|---:|
| Rent expense | Jan–Aug: 8 × 1,000 = 8,000; Sep–Dec: 4 × 1,200 = 4,800 | **12,800** |
| Electricity expense | 4,150 − 420 + 510 | **4,240** |
| Insurance expense | Old policy Jan–Mar: 1,350; new policy 9/12 × 5,760 = 4,320 | **5,670** |
| Rental income | 5,500 − 500 + 1,000 (or 12 × 500) | **6,000** |

*Check rent with the formula:* cash 13,000 (3,000 + 3,000 + 3,400 + 3,600) + opening prepayment 1,000 − closing prepayment 1,200 = **12,800**. The closing prepayment is January 20X4 rent, paid on 1 November: 1 × £1,200.

**Requirement 2: closing balances**

| Balance | Working | £ |
|---|---|---:|
| Prepaid rent | January 20X4 at the new rate | 1,200 |
| Prepaid insurance | 3/12 × 5,760 (January to March 20X4) | 1,440 |
| **Total prepayments** | 1,200 + 1,440 | **2,640** |
| **Accrued income** | November and December: 2 × 500 | **1,000** |
| **Accruals** | Electricity bill | **510** |

**Requirement 3: difference from the cash figures**

| | Accruals basis (£) | Cash basis (£) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Expenses: rent + electricity + insurance | 12,800 + 4,240 + 5,670 = 22,710 | 13,000 + 4,150 + 5,760 = 22,910 |
| Rental income | 6,000 | 5,500 |
| Net (income − expenses) | (16,710) | (17,410) |

Profit is **£700 higher** under the accruals basis (expenses £200 lower, income £500 higher).

*Marking tips:* the trap in (a) is the rent rise on 1 September, and in (c) is remembering the **opening** insurance prepayment of £1,350.

**Q10. Answer (Zubair Stationers)**

1. **Cash paid for rent** = Expense + closing prepayment − opening prepayment = 18,000 + 2,000 − 1,500 = **£18,500**.
2. **Cash paid for wages** = Expense − closing accrual + opening accrual = 46,500 − 1,300 + 2,100 = **£47,300**.
3. **Cash received for rental income** = Income + closing deferred income − opening deferred income = 4,800 + 900 − 600 = **£5,100**.
4. **Reconciliation of profit to cash:**

| | £ |
|---|---:|
| Profit from operations | 30,000 |
| Increase in prepaid rent (cash went out, but not yet an expense) | (500) |
| Decrease in wages accrued (we paid more cash than the expense) | (800) |
| Increase in rental income received in advance (cash came in, but not yet income) | 300 |
| **Cash generated from operations (before other changes)** | **29,000** |

*Check:* cash received 5,100 − rent 18,500 − wages 47,300 = (60,700). The statement of profit or loss shows 4,800 − 18,000 − 46,500 = (59,700). The difference is (1,000), which is exactly the three adjustments: (500) + (800) + 300.

*The cash flow rule:* an **increase in an asset** or a **decrease in a liability** takes cash **away**. A **decrease in an asset** or an **increase in a liability** brings cash **in**.
