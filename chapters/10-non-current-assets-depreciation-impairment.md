# Chapter 10: Non-current assets, depreciation and impairment

## 10.1 Start here: the idea in plain English

Imagine you run a small courier business. You buy a motorbike for **£1,200** and you expect to use it for **4 years**. Is the whole £1,200 a cost of the first year? If you said yes, year 1 would look terrible and years 2, 3 and 4 would look wonderful, even though the bike earns money in **all four years**. Common sense says each year should carry its fair share, about **£300 a year**. That yearly share is called **depreciation**.

The bike is a **non-current asset**: something the business owns (or controls) and will use for **more than one year**. A building, a machine, a delivery van and a computer are all non-current assets. If you can touch it, it is **tangible** and the accounting name is **property, plant and equipment** (PPE), covered by the standard IAS 16. If you cannot touch it (a licence, a patent, a brand), it is **intangible**. The **cost** of an asset is everything you must spend to get it ready for use: the price, the delivery, the fitting. Staff training and later repairs are **not** part of the cost.

Two warnings. First, depreciation is **not** a way of showing what the asset could be sold for today, and it is **not** a way of saving cash to buy a new asset. It is only a way of **sharing the cost over the years of use**. Second, an asset can lose value suddenly (the bike is smashed in an accident, or the machine becomes out of date). Then the business must write the asset down at once. This is called **impairment**. Lastly, when the asset is sold, you compare the money you receive with its **carrying amount** (cost less depreciation so far). The difference is a **profit or loss on disposal**.

> **KEY FACT** Depreciation spreads the cost of an asset over the years it helps to earn income (the accruals or matching idea). It is not valuation and it is not saving up money to replace the asset.

| Word | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Non-current asset** | An asset used for more than one reporting period |
| **Property, plant and equipment (PPE)** | Tangible non-current assets: land, buildings, machines, vehicles, fixtures |
| **Capital expenditure** | Spending that buys or improves an asset (shown in the statement of financial position, also called the balance sheet) |
| **Revenue expenditure** | Day-to-day running costs and repairs (an expense in the statement of profit or loss) |
| **Useful life** | How long the business expects to use the asset |
| **Residual value** | What the business expects to get when it disposes of the asset at the end of its useful life (assume nil unless told) |
| **Depreciable amount** | Cost less residual value |
| **Accumulated depreciation** | Total depreciation charged on the asset so far |
| **Carrying amount** | Cost less accumulated depreciation (less any impairment loss) |
| **Part-exchange (trade-in)** | Handing over an old asset to the seller as part payment for a new one |
| **Impairment** | The asset is worth less than its carrying amount, so it is written down |
| **Recoverable amount** | The higher of (a) fair value less costs of disposal and (b) value in use |
| **Amortisation** | The same idea as depreciation, but for an intangible asset |
| **Asset register** | A detailed list of every non-current asset, kept for internal control |

> **REMEMBER** Carrying amount = cost − accumulated depreciation. Profit or loss on disposal = proceeds − carrying amount. Both of these are needed again and again in this chapter.

## 10.2 Key facts to remember

- **What is in the cost of PPE (IAS 16):** purchase price (after trade discount), delivery, import duties and other taxes that cannot be recovered, site preparation, installation and assembly, professional fees (legal, architects) and the cost of testing that the asset works.
    - **Not in the cost:** staff training, general overheads and administration costs, fuel in a vehicle on delivery, running costs such as road tax or an annual operating licence (these are expenses of the period), and abnormal costs such as the cost of a design error.
    - **VAT** is left out of the cost because it can be recovered, **except on cars**, where it is not usually recoverable and so is part of the cost.
- **Capital or revenue?** Spending that **adds to or restores** the benefits of an asset (a major improvement or major overhaul) is capital: add it to cost. Ordinary repairs, servicing and maintenance are revenue: an expense in the statement of profit or loss. Treating a capital item as an expense makes profit too low in that year (and the asset too low).
- **Paying for assets:** by cash, on credit, by loan, by lease, or by part-exchange. **Cost of the new asset = cash paid + part-exchange allowance** (the full price of the new asset).
- **Why depreciate:** to match the cost of the asset with the income it helps to earn (accruals concept). All tangible assets have a limited life **except freehold land**, which is not depreciated.
- **Straight-line method:** the same charge every month. The ICAB workbook asks you to use the **monthly** calculation: (cost − residual value) ÷ (useful life in years × 12). Multiply by the number of months owned in the year.
- **Reducing balance method:** a fixed percentage of the **carrying amount at the start of the year**. The charge is biggest in year 1 and falls each year. **Ignore residual value** in this method; the percentage is given.
- **Months to count:** count the month of purchase, and count months up to the date of sale. Follow any rule written in the question (for example "no charge in the month of disposal").
- **Consistency and review:** use the same method year after year. Review method, useful life and residual value every year.
- **Changing the method, useful life or residual value is a change in accounting estimate.** Apply it from now on. **Do not restate earlier years.** New charge = (carrying amount at the date of change − new residual value) ÷ remaining useful life.
- **Double entry for depreciation:** Debit **Depreciation expense** (statement of profit or loss), Credit **Accumulated depreciation** (statement of financial position). The asset **cost** account does not change. Accumulated depreciation is a **credit** balance, set against cost to give the carrying amount.
    - Depreciation is usually worked out after the first trial balance is drawn up, so it is entered by a year-end journal.
    - Depreciation is shown in administrative expenses or distribution costs, depending on how the asset is used.
- **Right-of-use assets (leases):** the cost is given in the question. Depreciate it over the **lease term**. It is shown within non-current assets. (Lease liability and lease payments are not tested here.)
- **Impairment (IAS 36):** the business must check at each year-end for signs of impairment.
    - **Recoverable amount** = higher of fair value less costs of disposal and value in use. The exam gives you this figure.
    - **Impairment loss = carrying amount − recoverable amount.** Debit **Impairment loss** (statement of profit or loss), Credit the **carrying amount of the asset**.
    - Afterwards, depreciate the **new, lower carrying amount** over the **remaining useful life**.
    - Signs of impairment: obsolete or damaged asset, a change in use that hurts the asset, a fall in market value, new technology, results worse than expected, net assets worth more than the company's market value.
- **Disposals:** remove the **cost** and the **accumulated depreciation up to the date of sale**. Profit or loss = net proceeds (money received less any costs of sale) − carrying amount.
    - A **profit** is shown as other income; a **loss** is shown in administrative expenses (or distribution costs). It is a capital item, so it is **never part of revenue or gross profit**.
    - Use a **disposals account** as a temporary account. Its balance is the profit or loss, which goes to the statement of profit or loss.
    - In a **part-exchange**, the allowance for the old asset is its disposal proceeds.
- **Asset register:** a list of each non-current asset kept for **internal control**. It records the reference number, serial number, description, location, department, date bought, cost and later improvements, depreciation method and life, and carrying amount. It is used to check that assets exist, to find them, to calculate depreciation and to agree to the ledger accounts.
- **Intangible assets (IAS 38):** no physical substance.
    - **Purchased** licences, patents and brands are **capitalised at cost** and **amortised** over their useful life. A brand with no fixed life is not amortised but is tested for impairment every year.
    - **Development costs** must be capitalised **if the criteria are met**, then amortised. **Research** costs are always an expense.
    - **Internally generated brands and goodwill** cannot be capitalised.
- **Statement of financial position:** a single line "Property, plant and equipment" at carrying amount. A **note** gives the detail. **Disposal proceeds and profits or losses on disposal do not appear in the note.**
- **Statement of cash flows:** cash paid for new assets and cash received on disposals are **investing activities**. **Depreciation, amortisation, impairment and profit or loss on disposal are not cash flows.** The workbook does not cover revaluation of assets in this chapter.

## 10.3 Formulas and layouts

> **FORMULA** Cost of an asset = purchase price (after trade discount) + delivery + installation + site preparation + professional fees + testing (+ irrecoverable taxes)

> **FORMULA** Depreciable amount = Cost − Residual value

> **FORMULA** Straight-line depreciation per month = (Cost − Residual value) ÷ (Useful life in years × 12)

> **FORMULA** Straight-line charge for the year = Monthly charge × Number of months owned in the year

> **FORMULA** Reducing balance charge for the year = Carrying amount at start of year × Percentage

> **FORMULA** Carrying amount = Cost − Accumulated depreciation (− Impairment losses)

> **FORMULA** New annual charge after a change of estimate = (Carrying amount at date of change − New residual value) ÷ Remaining useful life

> **FORMULA** Impairment loss = Carrying amount − Recoverable amount, where Recoverable amount = higher of (Fair value less costs of disposal, Value in use)

> **FORMULA** Profit or (loss) on disposal = Net proceeds − Carrying amount at the date of disposal

> **FORMULA** Part-exchange allowance = Price of new asset − Cash paid. Cost of new asset = Cash paid + Part-exchange allowance

> **FORMULA** Closing cost = Opening cost + Additions − Cost of assets disposed of. Closing accumulated depreciation = Opening + Charge for the year − Accumulated depreciation on assets disposed of

> **FORMULA** Cash paid for new assets = Additions at cost − Part-exchange allowances − Increase in amounts owed to suppliers of assets

> **FORMULA** Disposal proceeds = Carrying amount of assets sold + Profit on disposal (or − Loss on disposal)

## 10.4 Worked examples

### Example 1: What goes into the cost?

Rina's Tailoring buys an industrial sewing machine. The supplier's invoice is **£8,000**. In the same month Rina also pays: delivery to the shop **£250**; fitting the machine to its bench **£400**; an electrician to wire a special socket **£350**; training for her staff **£300**; the first year's insurance **£200**; and a share of her general office overheads **£500**.

| Payment | Part of cost? | £ |
|---|---|---:|
| Invoice price | Yes | 8,000 |
| Delivery | Yes | 250 |
| Fitting (installation) | Yes | 400 |
| Wiring for the machine (site preparation) | Yes | 350 |
| **Cost of the machine** | | **9,000** |
| Training, insurance, overheads | No: expenses of the year (300 + 200 + 500) | 1,000 |

Journal: Debit Plant and machinery (cost) £9,000; Debit Expenses £1,000; Credit Cash £10,000.

Two years later Rina pays **£600** for a routine service (revenue expenditure: expense) and **£2,500** for an attachment that lets the machine do more work (capital expenditure: add to cost, which becomes **£11,500**).

*Easy way to see it:* if you spend money to get the asset ready to do its job, it is part of the asset. If you spend money to keep going, to teach people or to run the office, it is a normal cost.

### Example 2: Calculating depreciation and amortisation

**(a) Straight-line, monthly.** A machine costs **£24,000** and is bought on **1 April 20X1**. The year-end is 31 December. Useful life 4 years, residual value £2,400.

1. Monthly charge = (24,000 − 2,400) ÷ (4 × 12) = 21,600 ÷ 48 = **£450**.
2. 20X1: April to December = 9 months, 9 × 450 = **£4,050**. 20X2: 12 × 450 = **£5,400**.

| Year | Charge (£) | Accumulated depreciation (£) | Carrying amount (£) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 20X1 | 4,050 | 4,050 | 19,950 |
| 20X2 | 5,400 | 9,450 | 14,550 |

**(b) Reducing balance.** A machine costing **£15,000** is bought on 1 January 20X1 and depreciated at **20%** reducing balance. Residual value is ignored.

| Year | Calculation | Charge (£) | Carrying amount (£) |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| 20X1 | 20% × 15,000 | 3,000 | 12,000 |
| 20X2 | 20% × 12,000 | 2,400 | 9,600 |
| 20X3 | 20% × 9,600 | 1,920 | 7,680 |

**(c) Amortisation (same idea, intangible asset).** A business buys a licence on **1 July 20X5** for **£86,000** and pays **£4,000** legal fees to secure it. Life 5 years, year-end 31 December. Cost = 86,000 + 4,000 = **£90,000**. Monthly = 90,000 ÷ 60 = £1,500. Charge for 20X5 = 6 × 1,500 = **£9,000**. Carrying amount = **£81,000**. Entry: Debit Amortisation expense £9,000, Credit Accumulated amortisation £9,000.

### Example 3: Disposals, including a part-exchange

**(a) Sale for cash.** A machine cost **£30,000** on **1 October 20X1** and is depreciated straight-line at 10% a year on cost, monthly, nil residual value. It is sold on **31 March 20X4** for **£21,000**.

1. Months owned: October to December 20X1 (3) + 20X2 (12) + 20X3 (12) + January to March 20X4 (3) = **30 months**.
2. Monthly charge = 30,000 × 10% ÷ 12 = £250. Accumulated depreciation = 30 × 250 = **£7,500**.
3. Carrying amount = 30,000 − 7,500 = **£22,500**. Proceeds £21,000. **Loss on disposal £1,500.**

**(b) Part-exchange.** A van that cost **£18,000** with accumulated depreciation of **£11,000** is handed in for a new van with a price of **£27,000**. The business pays **£20,500** in cash.

1. Part-exchange allowance = 27,000 − 20,500 = **£6,500**.
2. Carrying amount of old van = 18,000 − 11,000 = £7,000. Loss = 7,000 − 6,500 = **£500**.
3. **Cost of the new van = £27,000** (not the £20,500 cash).

| Disposals account: debit side | £ | Credit side | £ |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Vans cost account (old van) | 18,000 | Accumulated depreciation (old van) | 11,000 |
| | | Part-exchange allowance | 6,500 |
| | | Statement of profit or loss (loss) | 500 |
| | **18,000** | | **18,000** |

Journals: Debit Vans cost £27,000, Credit Cash £20,500, Credit Disposals £6,500 (the new van). Debit Disposals £18,000, Credit Vans cost £18,000 (old van cost out). Debit Accumulated depreciation £11,000, Credit Disposals £11,000. Debit Statement of profit or loss £500, Credit Disposals £500 (the loss).

*Easy way to see it:* the old van is "sold" to the dealer for £6,500, but you never see that money. It just lowers what you pay for the new van.

### Example 4: Changing an estimate, and impairment

**(a) Change of estimate.** A machine cost **£40,000**, life 10 years, residual value £4,000, straight-line. The yearly charge is (40,000 − 4,000) ÷ 10 = **£3,600**. After **4 years** the carrying amount is 40,000 − 14,400 = **£25,600**. The business now expects it to last **8 more years** with a residual value of **£2,000**.

New charge = (25,600 − 2,000) ÷ 8 = **£2,950 a year**, from now on. The £14,400 already charged is **not** changed.

**(b) Impairment.** A factory building cost **£500,000** on 1 January 20X1 (life 25 years, nil residual value, straight-line). At **31 December 20X5**, after 5 years:

| | £ |
|---|---:|
| Cost | 500,000 |
| Accumulated depreciation (5 × 20,000) | (100,000) |
| **Carrying amount** | 400,000 |
| Fair value less costs of disposal | 330,000 |
| Value in use | 360,000 |
| **Recoverable amount (the higher)** | 360,000 |
| **Impairment loss** (400,000 − 360,000) | **40,000** |

Journal: Debit Impairment loss (statement of profit or loss) £40,000, Credit carrying amount of the building £40,000. From 20X6, depreciation = 360,000 ÷ 20 remaining years = **£18,000 a year**.

### Example 5: How it all shows in the statements

Plant at **1 January 20X2**: cost **£80,000**, accumulated depreciation **£32,000**. On 1 January 20X2 the business bought a new machine for **£20,000** cash and sold an old machine (cost **£10,000**, accumulated depreciation **£6,000**) for **£5,000** cash. Depreciation is 10% a year on cost, with a full year's charge on every asset still owned at 31 December 20X2 (including the new machine) and no charge on the machine sold, because it was sold on the first day of the year.

1. Depreciation = 10% × (80,000 − 10,000 + 20,000) = **£9,000**.
2. Disposal: carrying amount 10,000 − 6,000 = 4,000. Proceeds 5,000. **Profit £1,000.**

| Note: plant | £ |
|---|---:|
| Cost at 1 January 20X2 | 80,000 |
| Additions | 20,000 |
| Disposals | (10,000) |
| **Cost at 31 December 20X2** | **90,000** |
| Accumulated depreciation at 1 January 20X2 | 32,000 |
| Charge for the year | 9,000 |
| Disposals | (6,000) |
| **Accumulated depreciation at 31 December 20X2** | **35,000** |
| Carrying amount at 1 January 20X2 | 48,000 |
| **Carrying amount at 31 December 20X2** | **55,000** |

| Statement | Line | £ |
|---|---|---:|
| Statement of financial position | Property, plant and equipment | 55,000 |
| Statement of profit or loss | Depreciation expense | 9,000 |
| Statement of profit or loss | Profit on disposal (other income) | 1,000 |
| Statement of cash flows, operating | Add depreciation 9,000; deduct profit on disposal (1,000) | 8,000 |
| Statement of cash flows, investing | Purchase of plant (20,000); proceeds of sale 5,000 | (15,000) |

*Check:* 48,000 + 20,000 − 4,000 − 9,000 = **55,000**.

## 10.5 Exam traps

1. **Wrong things in the cost, or capital treated as an expense.** Staff training, general overheads and repairs are **expenses**, not cost. Delivery, installation, site preparation, testing and professional fees **are** cost. Read each item one by one. If a capital item is wrongly expensed, profit is too low by the amount less the depreciation that should have been charged.
2. **Thinking depreciation is valuation or saving money.** Depreciation only spreads cost. It does not show market value and it does not put cash aside.
3. **Forgetting the residual value, or using it in the wrong method.** Straight-line: take it off the cost first. Reducing balance: ignore it. Also count the months carefully: the month of purchase counts.
4. **Part-exchange mistakes.** The new asset's cost is **cash + allowance**, not just the cash. The allowance (price − cash) is compared with the **carrying amount** of the old asset, not with its cost and not with the cash paid.
5. **Profit or loss on disposal.** Compare proceeds with the **carrying amount** (cost less depreciation to the date of sale), **not** with cost. Check the direction: proceeds higher = profit, proceeds lower = loss. It is **not** part of revenue or gross profit.
6. **Changing an estimate.** Do **not** restate earlier years and do **not** make a catch-up charge. Take the carrying amount now and spread it over the remaining life.
7. **Impairment.** Compare the carrying amount (not cost) with the **higher** of fair value less costs of disposal and value in use. Never use the lower figure. After impairment, depreciate the new lower amount.
8. **Cash flow confusion.** Depreciation and profit or loss on disposal are **not cash**. The cash effect of a purchase or a sale is the actual money paid or received. Watch for unpaid invoices and for part-exchange allowances, which are not cash.

## 10.8 Answers and explanations

### Answers: extra practice questions

**Q1. Answer: A, B and D**

- **Why:** The cost includes everything needed to bring the asset to its place and make it ready to work: delivery, preparing the site and testing. Training and general overheads are not part of the asset.
- **Easy example:** When you buy a washing machine, the delivery, the plumbing and the first test wash are part of "getting it ready". The lessons you give your family on how to use it are not.
- **Why not the others:** C is a staff cost, not a cost of the oven itself. E is an overhead that cannot be linked directly to the oven.

**Q2. Answer: C (£3,600)**

- **Why:** The cost includes the delivery and the installation. The month of purchase is counted, so September to December is 4 months.
- **Formula:** Cost = 57,000 + 1,500 + 1,500 = 60,000. Monthly charge = (60,000 − 6,000) ÷ 60 = 900. Charge = 4 × 900 = **£3,600**.
- **Easy example:** A laptop paid for on the 1st of a month gets a full month of "use" that month. So four months of use means four months of depreciation.
- **Why not the others:** A (£2,700) uses 3 months and forgets that the month of purchase counts. B (£3,400) leaves out the delivery and installation. D (£4,000) forgets the residual value.

**Q3. Answer: B (loss of £1,000)**

- **Why:** Reducing balance charges 25% of the carrying amount each year. After 2 years the carrying amount is £18,000, which is more than the £17,000 received, so there is a loss.
- **Formula:** Year 1: 32,000 × 25% = 8,000, so carrying amount 24,000. Year 2: 24,000 × 25% = 6,000, so carrying amount 18,000. Loss = 18,000 − 17,000 = **£1,000**.
- **Easy example:** A machine loses value fastest in its first year. Selling it after two years for £17,000, when your books say £18,000, is a small loss.
- **Why not the others:** A uses straight-line depreciation (carrying amount £16,000), which gives a profit. C uses only one year of depreciation. D compares the proceeds with the cost (32,000 − 17,000) and ignores depreciation.

**Q4. Answer: A (profit of £1,100)**

- **Why:** The allowance for the old van is the price of the new van less the cash paid. Compare that allowance with the old van's carrying amount.
- **Formula:** Allowance = 26,000 − 19,500 = 6,500. Carrying amount = 15,000 − 9,600 = 5,400. Profit = 6,500 − 5,400 = **£1,100**.
- **Easy example:** The dealer values your old van at £6,500, while your books say it is worth £5,400. You are £1,100 better off than your books said.
- **Why not the others:** B reverses the sign. C compares the allowance with the cost (6,500 − 15,000). D compares the cash paid with the carrying amount (19,500 − 5,400), but the cash is payment for the new van, not for the old one.

**Q5. Answer: A (£6,000)**

- **Why:** Impairment loss = carrying amount − recoverable amount, and recoverable amount is the **higher** of the two figures given.
- **Formula:** Carrying amount = 80,000 − (3 × 8,000) = 56,000. Recoverable amount = higher of 44,000 and 50,000 = 50,000. Loss = 56,000 − 50,000 = **£6,000**.
- **Easy example:** Your car is in your books at £56,000. You could sell it for £44,000 or keep using it and earn the equivalent of £50,000. A sensible owner picks the better option, £50,000, so it is only £6,000 overvalued.
- **Why not the others:** B uses the lower figure (56,000 − 44,000). C compares the cost with the recoverable amount (80,000 − 50,000). D compares the cost with the lower figure (80,000 − 44,000).

**Q6. Answer: A and C**

- **Why:** The legal fees are a cost of getting the licence, so they are added to its cost. A licence has a limited life, so it is amortised.
- **Formula:** Cost = 120,000 + 6,000 = 126,000. Monthly = 126,000 ÷ 48 = 2,625. Charge = 4 months × 2,625 = **£10,500**.
- **Easy example:** When you buy a gym membership you also pay a joining fee. Both are part of the cost of getting the right to use the gym, and you spread them over the length of the membership.
- **Why not the others:** B is wrong because directly attributable fees are capitalised. D is wrong because intangible assets with a finite life are amortised just as tangible assets are depreciated.

**Q7. Answer: C (understated by £35,100)**

- **Why:** The £36,000 was charged to expenses, so profit is too low by £36,000. But a correct entry would have charged 3 months of depreciation, so the true profit is lower by £900. The net understatement is £35,100.
- **Formula:** Depreciation = 36,000 × 10% × 3/12 = 900. Understatement = 36,000 − 900 = **£35,100**.
- **Easy example:** You buy a £36,000 shelf unit and wrongly write it off as an expense in one go. Your profit looks very low, but a small part (£900) really should have been an expense this year.
- **Why not the others:** A has the wrong direction. B takes off a full year of depreciation (3,600) instead of three months. D forgets the depreciation.

**Q8. Answer: C (£10,000)**

- **Why:** A change in useful life is a change in estimate. Take the carrying amount now and spread it over the remaining years. Do not go back and change earlier years.
- **Formula:** Original charge = 48,000 ÷ 8 = 6,000. Carrying amount at the start of 20X4 = 48,000 − 18,000 = 30,000. Remaining life = 6 − 3 = 3 years. Charge = 30,000 ÷ 3 = **£10,000**.
- **Easy example:** You planned a 5-year plan to repay a loan, but after 2 years you decide to finish in 3 more years. You simply divide what is left by the new time left.
- **Why not the others:** A ignores the change. B applies the new life to the whole cost (48,000 ÷ 6). D makes a catch-up charge: 4 years at £8,000 = 32,000, less the 18,000 already charged.

### Answers: true or false

| # | Answer | Reason and easy example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **False** | Depreciation spreads cost over the years of use. It does not give a market value. A 3-year-old car may be worth more or less than its carrying amount. |
| 2 | **False** | Routine servicing keeps the asset going. It is revenue expenditure, an expense of the year. Only improvements and major overhauls that add benefits are capitalised. |
| 3 | **True** | The month of purchase counts, so March, April and so on to December make 10 months. |
| 4 | **True** | A change in useful life is a change in estimate. The carrying amount at the date of change is spread over the remaining life. Earlier years stay as they are. |
| 5 | **False** | Recoverable amount is the **higher** of fair value less costs of disposal and value in use. A business would take the better of selling or using the asset. |
| 6 | **True** | The allowance is part of the price paid for the new asset. If a £27,000 van costs £20,500 cash plus an allowance of £6,500, the van is recorded at £27,000. |
| 7 | **False** | Internally generated brands cannot be capitalised, because their cost cannot be separated from the general costs of the business. A brand **bought** from another business can be capitalised. |
| 8 | **True** | The register lists each asset with its number, location and cost. Staff can walk round and tick the assets off to check they exist, and it can be agreed to the ledger accounts. |
