## A.3 Answers and explanations

**Q1. Answer: A (buying inventory on credit)**

- **Why:** Buying inventory on credit gives the business a new asset (inventories £2,000) and a new obligation (trade payables £2,000). No cash moves and capital does not change.
- **Formula:** Assets + £2,000 = Capital + (Liabilities + £2,000).
- **Easy example:** You take a bag of rice from the shop and promise to pay at the end of the week. You now have more rice and you also owe more money.
- **Why not the others:** B (The owner pays £5,000 of her own savings into the ...) raises assets and capital, not liabilities. The owner's money is capital. C (A customer pays £900 that it owed from an earlier credit ...) swaps one asset (trade receivables) for another (cash), so the totals do not change. D (The business pays a supplier £1,500 for goods bought ...) lowers both cash and trade payables, so assets and liabilities both go down.
- **Revise:** Chapter 2

**Q2. Answer: D (comparability)**

- **Why:** Comparability lets users see similarities and differences between periods and between entities. Using a different method each year hides whether profit really changed. Consistent methods help to achieve comparability.
- **Easy example:** If a shop weighs rice in kilograms one month and in pounds the next, you cannot tell whether sales really went up. The same measure every time lets you compare.
- **Why not the others:** A (Verifiability) means independent people could agree that the figure is a fair picture. It is about checking a figure, not comparing years. B (Timeliness) means the information is available in time to influence decisions. The problem here is not delay. C (Understandability) means the information is clearly classified and presented. The figures can be clear and still not comparable.
- **Revise:** Chapter 1

**Q3. Answer: C (£18,450)**

- **Why:** Profit is gross profit less expenses. Cost of sales is opening inventories plus purchases less closing inventories. The drawings are what the owner took for private use, not a cost of the business.
- **Formula:** Cost of sales = £7,900 + £58,300 − £9,600 = £56,600. Gross profit = £96,400 − £56,600 = £39,800. Profit = £39,800 − £21,350 = £18,450.
- **Easy example:** You count the cakes on the shelf at the start and end of the year. What you bought, adjusted for what is left, is what you sold. Money you take home for the family is not a cost of baking.
- **Why not the others:** A (Loss of £750) adds the closing inventories to cost of sales instead of deducting them. B (£13,450) deducts the £5,000 drawings. Drawings are not an expense. D (£26,350) leaves out the opening inventories.
- **Revise:** Chapter 5

**Q4. Answer: D (Dr Trade payables 690; Cr Purchases 600; Cr VAT 90)**

- **Why:** A return reverses the original purchase. The business now owes the supplier £690 less (£600 plus 15% VAT), purchases fall by £600 and the VAT that the business could reclaim falls by £90.
- **Formula:** VAT = £600 × 15% = £90. Total = £600 + £90 = £690.
- **Easy example:** You hand back a £690 shirt that included tax. The shop owes you £690 less. £600 of it was the price and £90 was tax, so the tax part goes back to the tax account.
- **Why not the others:** A (Debit Trade payables £600; credit Purchases £600.) leaves out the VAT, so trade payables would still show the £90 VAT as owed to the supplier. B (Debit Purchases £690; credit Trade payables £690.) is the entry for buying goods on credit, the wrong way round. C (Debit Trade payables £690; credit Purchases £690.) puts the VAT into purchases. The £90 belongs in the VAT account.
- **Revise:** Chapter 4

**Q5. Answer: B (£2,208)**

- **Why:** The trade discount comes off first. VAT is charged on the discounted price. Trade receivables are recorded gross, so they include the VAT.
- **Formula:** List value = 150 × 16 = £2,400. After 20% discount = £1,920. VAT = £1,920 × 15% = £288. Total = £1,920 + £288 = £2,208.

- **Easy example:** A customer's tab at your shop goes up with every purchase and down with every refund and every payment. If you know the start, the end and the purchases and refunds, the payments are what is left.
- **Why not the others:** B (£40,500) takes the VAT out of the credit notes. Receivables include VAT, so the whole credit notes of £2,300 must be deducted. C (£42,500) forgets to deduct the credit notes. D (£44,800) adds the credit notes instead of deducting them.
- **Revise:** Chapter 4

**Q40. Answer: D (£19,300)**

- **Why:** This year's tax expense is the estimate for this year plus any under-provision from last year (or minus any over-provision). The old liability was too low by the difference.
- **Formula:** Under-provision = £16,100 − £15,200 = £900. Expense = £18,400 + £900 = £19,300.
- **Easy example:** You set aside £15,200 to pay last year's electricity bill, but the bill was £16,100. The extra £900 shows up as a cost in this year's budget.
- **Why not the others:** A (£16,100) is the amount paid for 20X0. It is not the expense for 20X1. B (£17,500) deducts the difference. The 20X0 estimate was too low (under-provision), so the extra cost is added to this year's expense. C (£18,400) ignores the under-provision for 20X0.
- **Revise:** Chapter 11

**Q41. Answer: A (Dr Cash at bank; Cr Irrecoverable debts expense)**

- **Why:** The debt was removed earlier and charged as an expense. The cash now received reverses part of that expense, so the credit goes to irrecoverable debts expense and net profit rises.
- **Easy example:** You wrote off a loan to a friend as lost. When he pays it back you do not pretend that you made a new sale. You simply say that your loss was smaller than you thought.
- **Why not the others:** B (Debit Cash at bank £700; credit Trade receivables £700.) credits trade receivables a second time. The debt was removed when it was written off, so the customer would look as if he had overpaid. C (Debit Cash at bank £700; credit Revenue £700.) treats the receipt as a new sale, but the sale was recorded in the earlier year. D (Debit Cash at bank £700; credit Allowance for ...) uses the allowance, which is an estimate for other debts, not a place for a recovery.
- **Revise:** Chapter 8

**Q42. Answer: B (£14,000)**

- **Why:** Closing capital is opening capital plus capital introduced plus profit (or minus a loss), minus drawings.
- **Formula:** £18,000 + £3,000 − £2,600 − £4,400 = £14,000.
- **Easy example:** Think of a money jar. You start with £18,000, add £3,000 more of your savings, lose £2,600 through bad trading and take £4,400 out for the family.
- **Why not the others:** A (£8,000) deducts the capital introduced. It must be added. C (£18,400) forgets the drawings. D (£19,200) adds the loss instead of deducting it.
- **Revise:** Chapter 5

**Q43. Answer: D (overdraft of £10,500)**

- **Why:** Closing cash and cash equivalents = opening cash and cash equivalents + net increase (or − net decrease). With an overdraft counted as negative cash, the opening figure is −£6,500.
- **Formula:** Net change = £28,000 − £41,000 + £9,000 = −£4,000. Closing = −£6,500 + (−£4,000) = −£10,500 (an overdraft of £10,500).
- **Easy example:** You start the year £6,500 in the red. During the year you end up another £4,000 down. You are now £10,500 in the red, not £4,000 and not in the black.
- **Why not the others:** A (Overdraft of £4,000) is only the net decrease for the year. The opening overdraft is also part of the position. B (Cash of £2,500) treats the opening overdraft as positive cash. C (Overdraft of £2,500) takes the net decrease of £4,000 off the £6,500 opening overdraft instead of adding it to it.
- **Revise:** Chapter 13

**Q44. Answer: B, C and E**

- **Why:** The statement of changes in equity shows how the owners' stake moved. It goes up with profit and new shares (capital and premium) and down with dividends paid.
- **Easy example:** Equity is the owners' stake. It rises when they put in money or the business earns a profit, and it falls when profit is paid out. Borrowing from a bank or buying a machine does not change the owners' stake.
- **Why not the others:** A (The purchase of a new machine for cash) changes assets (cash and machinery), not equity. D (Proceeds of a new bank loan) is a liability, not equity.
- **Revise:** Chapter 12

**Q45. Answer: C (revenue and a trade receivable)**

- **Why:** Under the accruals basis, income is recorded when it is earned, not when the cash arrives. The service was given in December, so the revenue belongs to 20X1. The customer still owes the money, so it is an asset: a trade receivable.
- **Easy example:** You cut a customer's hair on 30 December and she says, 'I will pay you after the holiday'. You earned the money in December, but you are still waiting for it. It is money owed to you, not money in your pocket.
- **Why not the others:** A (No revenue is recognised in 20X1, because no cash has ...) this is the cash basis. The accruals basis records income when it is earned. B (Revenue of £2,400 is recognised in 20X1 and cash at bank ...) wrongly shows cash received. The cash arrives in January 20X2, so the bank balance does not rise in 20X1. D (Revenue of £2,400 is recognised in 20X1 and deferred ...) mixes up deferred income (cash received before the service) with this case, where the service came first.
- **Revise:** Chapter 1

**Q46. Answer: D (£37,000)**

- **Why:** Work the carrying amount like a T-account: opening + additions − depreciation − carrying amount of disposals = closing. The additions are the balancing figure.
- **Formula:** Additions = £102,000 − £85,000 + £14,000 + £6,000 = £37,000.
- **Easy example:** Your garage held tools worth 85 at the start and 102 at the end. Tools wore out by 14 and you sold one worth 6. To end with 102 you must have bought 37 of new tools.
- **Why not the others:** A (£23,000) forgets the depreciation. B (£31,000) forgets the carrying amount of the machine sold. C (£36,000) uses the sale proceeds instead of the carrying amount of the machine sold.
- **Revise:** Chapter 13

**Q47. Answer: B and D**

- **Why:** Two of the characteristics of a good budget system are unity (one budget law for all receipts and payments) and an annual basis (authority normally for 12 months). Others include accountability, transparency and comprehensiveness.
- **Easy example:** A family does best when it keeps one budget for the year for all income and spending, and tells everyone at home what it is. It does not hide the budget, and it does not keep two separate books.
- **Why not the others:** A (Dual budgets: one budget for current spending and a ...) is the opposite of unity. A good system has all receipts and payments in one annual budget law, with no dual budget. C (Secrecy: budget information is kept from the public ...) is the opposite of transparency. Timely information on the budget should be publicly available.

**Q48. Answer: A (complement IFRS Accounting Standards)**

- **Why:** The ISSB was formed by the IFRS Foundation in 2021. Its sustainability standards are meant to be used together with IFRS Accounting Standards and to serve investors. Whether a company must use them depends on local law and regulators.
- **Easy example:** Think of a school report with two parts: the marks (the financial statements) and a note on effort and behaviour (sustainability information). The second part adds to the first. It does not replace it.
- **Why not the others:** B (The ISSB can force every company in the world to publish ...) overstates the ISSB's power. It can force no one: local law and regulators decide who must apply its standards. C (The ISSB standards replace IFRS Accounting Standards.) is wrong because the standards sit alongside IFRS Accounting Standards. They do not replace them. D (Sustainability reporting is concerned only with the ...) is too narrow. Sustainability covers environmental, social and governance (ESG) matters, not the environment alone.
- **Revise:** Chapter 1

**Q49. Answer: B (provision of £60,000)**

- **Why:** A provision is recognised when there is a present obligation from a past event, payment is probable (more than 50% likely) and the amount can be estimated. Then the full best estimate is provided.
- **Formula:** Provision = full estimate = £60,000 (not 70% × £60,000 = £42,000).
- **Easy example:** If a mechanic tells you that you will probably have to pay £60,000 for a damaged car, you set aside the full £60,000 now. You do not set aside only 70% of it.
- **Why not the others:** A (Recognise a provision of £42,000 (70% of £60,000).) multiplies the estimate by the probability. Once payment is probable, the full best estimate is provided. C (Make no provision, but describe the claim in a note as a ...) is the treatment for claims that are only possible, but here payment is probable. D (Make no entry and give no disclosure until the court ...) ignores the claim. Waiting for the court is not allowed when there is a present obligation and payment is probable.
- **Revise:** Chapter 11

**Q50. Answer: D (drawings)**

- **Why:** A sole trader and the business are one legal person. The tax on the profit is the owner's own liability. When the business bank account pays it, it is the owner taking value out for private use: drawings.
- **Easy example:** If you pay your own house rent from the shop's cash drawer, you are using the shop's money for your private life. The shop's books record it as money taken out by the owner.
- **Why not the others:** A (As an expense in the profit and loss account, reducing ...) treats the owner's own tax as an expense of the business. B (As a tax creditor in the balance sheet.) shows a liability that does not exist. The tax was paid, and it is not the business's tax. C (As a debtor (money owed to the business) in the balance ...) shows an asset, but nobody owes the business this money. It paid the owner's own bill.
- **Revise:** Chapter 14
