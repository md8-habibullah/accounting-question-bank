# Chapter 6: Errors and corrections to accounting records and financial statements

## 6.1 Start here: the idea in plain English

Imagine you keep a notebook of the money in your bank account. The bank keeps its own record too, called the **bank statement**. On 30 June your notebook says **£1,000** but the statement says **£1,300**. Who is wrong? Often nobody. You wrote a **£300** cheque and noted it at once, but the shop has not banked it yet, so the bank has not taken the money out. On another day the gap might be £20 of bank charges that you have not heard about yet. Finding and explaining the difference is called a **bank reconciliation**. In accounting, your notebook is the **cash at bank account** (some books call it the cash book).

Even careful people make mistakes. You write 7,520 as 7,250. You forget a sale. You write the water bill on the rent page. You buy a van but write it down as "repairs". Each kind of mistake has a name, and the exam asks you to **name it** and **correct it** with a **journal** (a short instruction to the bookkeeper: debit this account, credit that account). A correction can change profit, so you must also be able to work out the **corrected profit**.

Sometimes money arrives in the bank and nobody knows what it is for. You cannot leave it out, so you write it on a "to be sorted" page. In accounting that page is the **suspense account**. It is only a parking space. Before the final accounts are made, every parked item must be moved to its proper account.

| Word | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Cash at bank account** | Our own ledger account for the bank. A debit balance is an asset; a credit balance is an **overdraft** (a liability) |
| **Bank statement** (bank transaction report) | The bank's own record of our account |
| **Bank reconciliation** | A list that explains the difference between the two records |
| **Unpresented (uncleared) payment** | We have paid (cheque or electronic payment) but the bank has not yet taken the money |
| **Uncleared receipt (lodgement)** | We have banked money but the bank has not yet added it |
| **Dishonoured receipt** | A customer's cheque or transfer was refused by their bank: the money never came |
| **Journal** | A written correction showing the debits and credits |
| **Draft profit** | Profit calculated before the errors were found |
| **Trial balance** | A list of all ledger balances. Total debits should equal total credits |
| **Drawings** | Money or goods the owner takes out for personal use. It is **not** an expense |
| **Capital expenditure** | Buying something that lasts (an asset), such as a van. **Revenue expenditure** is a day-to-day running cost (an expense), such as repairs |

> **KEY FACT** Every difference between the cash at bank account and the bank statement is one of four things: (1) an item we have not recorded yet, (2) a mistake in our books, (3) a timing difference, or (4) a mistake by the bank. Only (1) and (2) change our ledger.

## 6.2 Key facts to remember

**Bank reconciliation**

- The bank statement is the **mirror image** of our books. If we have money in the bank, our ledger shows a **debit** (an asset). The bank shows a **credit**, because it owes us the money. If we are **overdrawn**, our ledger shows a **credit** (a current liability) and the statement shows a **debit**.
- **Items on the statement that we have not recorded yet** are put into the cash at bank account with a journal:
    - bank charges: Dr Bank charges (expense), Cr Cash at bank. Profit goes **down**;
    - bank interest received: Dr Cash at bank, Cr Interest income. Profit goes **up**;
    - direct debit or standing order paid: Dr the expense (or liability) concerned, Cr Cash at bank;
    - a receipt paid straight into the bank (for example a dividend or a customer's transfer): Dr Cash at bank, Cr Investment income or Trade receivables;
    - a **dishonoured receipt**: Dr Trade receivables, Cr Cash at bank. The customer owes us again. There is **no effect on profit**.
- **Our own mistakes** (for example digits swapped, or a payment left out) are corrected in the cash at bank account with a journal.
- **Timing differences** (unpresented payments and uncleared receipts) are already correct in our ledger. They appear **only in the reconciliation**. **No journal.**
- **Bank mistakes** are reported to the bank. They appear **only in the reconciliation**. **No journal.**
- The **statement of financial position** (balance sheet) shows the **corrected cash at bank account balance**, never the bank statement figure. A debit balance is a current asset. An overdraft is a current liability.

**Supplier statement reconciliation**

- A **supplier statement** is an **external document** sent by a credit supplier. It lists invoices, returns, discounts and payments. We compare it with our **payables ledger** (the personal account of each supplier).
- The statement is written from the **supplier's** point of view. What we owe is a **debit** balance on the statement, because we are the supplier's receivable.
- Typical reconciling items: a payment we sent that the supplier has not yet recorded; goods we returned before the supplier issued a credit note; a discount we took that the supplier has not allowed; an invoice we forgot to enter.
- We correct **our own** ledger only for **our own** omissions or errors. Items the supplier must fix are followed up with the supplier, not journalised. Disputed items are raised with the supplier.
- Customer statements are produced by us, so they are **not** external documents. They are a weaker check.
- The receivables ledger and payables ledger are **memorandum** records: they are not part of the double entry. In a computerised system they post automatically to the trade receivables and trade payables accounts, so differences are unlikely, but checking against external documents is still useful.

**Types of error (the five types in the ICAB workbook)**

- **Transposition:** two digits are the wrong way round (7,520 recorded as 7,250).
- **Omission:** a transaction is not recorded at all.
- **Principle:** an entry breaks an accounting rule, usually by putting the item in the wrong **class** of account. Examples: capital expenditure treated as revenue expenditure (a van debited to motor expenses), or the owner's private spending treated as a business expense instead of **drawings**.
- **Commission:** an entry is put in the wrong account, but **in the right class** (water bill debited to the rent account: both are expenses).
- **Compensating:** two errors are equal and opposite, so they cancel out and hide each other.
- None of these five stops total debits equalling total credits, so a **trial balance still balances**. The accounts are wrong even though the trial balance agrees. (In a manual system a one-sided entry or an entry with different amounts on each side does upset the trial balance. A computerised system will not accept an unbalanced entry.)
- Some books also name "error of original entry" (a wrong amount on both sides) and "reversal of entries" (debit and credit swapped). The ICAB workbook uses the five types above. All of them leave the trial balance balanced and are corrected in the same way.

**Correcting errors**

- Every error is put right with a **journal**. Three steps: (1) what was recorded? (2) what should have been recorded? (3) what journal moves us from (1) to (2)?
- A computerised system will not accept a one-sided entry, so the trial balance always balances. That does **not** mean the records are complete or correct.

**Suspense account**

- A suspense account is a **temporary** account. It is opened by the computerised system when it cannot match a bank transaction, or by the bookkeeper who does not know where to post one side of an entry.
- The cash at bank side is already correct. A **receipt** is recorded Dr Cash at bank, **Cr Suspense**. A **payment** is recorded **Dr Suspense**, Cr Cash at bank.
- The suspense account can hold **several unrelated items**, and its balance can be debit or credit. Each item needs its own correcting journal. After all journals the balance must be **nil**.
- A suspense account must **never appear in the financial statements**. Clear it, and make all corrections, **before** the statements are prepared.

**Effect on profit**

- Only corrections that touch an **income** or **expense** account change profit. Drawings, capital, receivables, payables, non-current assets, cash at bank and suspense do **not**.
- Debit to an expense: profit **down**. Credit to an expense: profit **up**. Credit to income: profit **up**. Debit to income: profit **down**.
- Sales, purchases, cost of sales and inventories change **gross profit** (and so net profit). Other expenses change only net profit.
- If many corrections all move profit upwards, an accountant should use **professional scepticism**: it might mean the figures were being manipulated to reach a target.

**Trial balance**

- The **initial trial balance** is adjusted with debit and credit **adjustments columns**. Final balance = initial balance + adjustments. Each column of adjustments must have equal debit and credit totals. After the journals, the suspense account shows nil.

## 6.3 Formulas and layouts

> **FORMULA** Corrected cash at bank balance = Balance brought forward + receipts not yet recorded (direct receipts, interest) − payments not yet recorded (bank charges, direct debits, dishonoured receipts) plus or minus our own errors

> **FORMULA** Balance per bank statement + uncleared receipts (lodgements) − unpresented payments plus or minus bank errors = Corrected cash at bank balance

> **FORMULA** Bank error: the bank credited us wrongly = deduct from the statement balance. The bank debited us wrongly = add to the statement balance

> **FORMULA** Suspense account balance = Total debits posted to suspense − Total credits posted to suspense

> **FORMULA** Corrected profit = Draft profit + corrections that increase profit − corrections that decrease profit

> **FORMULA** Wrong amount recorded: correcting journal = Amount that should have been recorded − Amount that was recorded

> **FORMULA** Entry posted on the wrong side (an invoice recorded as a credit note): correcting journal = 2 × the amount

> **FORMULA** Effect on profit of an expense wrongly capitalised = −Amount + Depreciation charged on it

> **FORMULA** Corrected payables ledger balance = Supplier statement balance − payments we made that the supplier has not recorded − returns not yet credited by the supplier

> **FORMULA** Final trial balance = Initial trial balance + Adjustments (debit and credit columns)

## 6.4 Worked examples

### Example 1: Name the error, check the trial balance, write the journal

| # | What happened | Type | Debits = credits? | Correcting journal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A sale of £7,520 was entered as £7,250 in the sales and trade receivables accounts | Transposition | Yes | Dr Trade receivables £270, Cr Sales £270 |
| 2 | A £380 purchase invoice (goods on credit) was never recorded | Omission | Yes (both sides short by £380) | Dr Purchases £380, Cr Trade payables £380 |
| 3 | A water bill of £210 was debited to the rent expense account | Commission | Yes | Dr Water £210, Cr Rent £210 |
| 4 | The owner's home electricity bill of £150 was paid from the business bank and debited to electricity expense | Principle | Yes | Dr Drawings £150, Cr Electricity £150 |
| 5 | A delivery van costing £9,000 was debited to motor expenses | Principle | Yes | Dr Motor vehicles £9,000, Cr Motor expenses £9,000 |
| 6 | Salaries were entered as £4,100 instead of £4,600, and advertising as £2,300 instead of £1,800 | Compensating | Yes | Dr Salaries £500, Cr Advertising £500 |

*Easy way to see it:* in every row the double entry still balances, so the trial balance gives **no warning**. These errors are found only by checking, for example by reconciliations and by reviewing the accounts.

For comparison, in a **manual** system a payment of £600 credited to the bank with **no debit at all** would make the trial balance £600 out. The difference would be parked in suspense until the missing debit was found.

### Example 2: Correcting journals and the effect on profit

Rashid Traders has a **draft net profit of £30,000**. Using the three steps on error 2: (1) recorded: Dr Machinery £650, Cr Bank £650; (2) should have been: Dr Repairs £650, Cr Bank £650; (3) correction: take the debit out of Machinery and put it in Repairs.

| # | Error | Correcting journal | Effect on profit (£) |
|---|---|---|---:|
| 1 | Purchase invoice for goods, £1,200, omitted | Dr Purchases £1,200, Cr Trade payables £1,200 | (1,200) |
| 2 | Repairs of £650 debited to machinery | Dr Repairs £650, Cr Machinery £650 | (650) |
| 3 | Owner's home electricity £420 paid by the business, debited to electricity | Dr Drawings £420, Cr Electricity £420 | 420 |
| 4 | Telephone £180 debited to electricity | Dr Telephone £180, Cr Electricity £180 | 0 |
| 5 | Credit sale of £2,400 omitted (ignore inventory) | Dr Trade receivables £2,400, Cr Sales £2,400 | 2,400 |
| 6 | £500 cash from a customer credited to sales instead of trade receivables | Dr Sales £500, Cr Trade receivables £500 | (500) |
| | **Net effect on profit** | | **470** |

Corrected net profit = 30,000 + 470 = **£30,470**.

*Easy way to see it:* look **only** at the income and expense accounts in each journal. Error 4 swaps one expense for another, so profit does not move. Error 6 had counted the same sale twice (once when invoiced, again when paid).

In the statement of financial position: trade payables **up** £1,200; machinery **down** £650; trade receivables **up** £1,900 (2,400 − 500); drawings **up** £420, so the owner's capital is lower.

### Example 3: Opening and clearing a suspense account

Lakshmi Kitchenware's system could not match four items on the bank transaction report. Each was correctly entered in cash at bank, with the other side posted to suspense:

- (a) a receipt of £2,000: later found to be a part payment from a credit customer;
- (b) a receipt of £400: bank interest received;
- (c) a receipt of £1,500: the owner paying extra money into the business (capital);
- (d) a payment of £750: the owner's personal spending (drawings).

Receipts were credited to suspense, and the payment was debited. Credits £3,900 − debits £750 = a **credit balance of £3,150**.

| Suspense account: debit side | £ | Credit side | £ |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| (d) Payment from bank | 750 | (a) Receipt in bank | 2,000 |
| Clearing (a): to Trade receivables | 2,000 | (b) Receipt in bank | 400 |
| Clearing (b): to Interest income | 400 | (c) Receipt in bank | 1,500 |
| Clearing (c): to Capital | 1,500 | Clearing (d): to Drawings | 750 |
| | **4,650** | | **4,650** |

Clearing journals: Dr Suspense £2,000, Cr Trade receivables £2,000. Dr Suspense £400, Cr Interest income £400. Dr Suspense £1,500, Cr Capital £1,500. Dr Drawings £750, Cr Suspense £750.

The balance is now **nil**. Only (b) touches an income account, so profit goes **up by £400**.

### Example 4: A full bank reconciliation

At 30 June 20X2, Nasrin's Tailoring has a **debit balance of £4,260** in its cash at bank account. The bank statement shows **£5,535** in credit. Differences found:

1. Bank charges of £45 are on the statement only.
2. A standing order for insurance of £120 is on the statement only.
3. A customer's transfer of £680 is on the statement only.
4. A customer's transfer of £250, recorded earlier in the ledger, is shown on the statement as dishonoured.
5. A customer's receipt of £540 was entered in the ledger as £450.
6. Cheques of £1,340 sent to suppliers have not yet cleared.
7. Takings of £720 banked on 30 June were credited by the bank on 1 July.
8. The bank credited £300 to the account in error (it belongs to another customer).

**Step 1: update the cash at bank account** (items 1 to 5 only).

| Cash at bank: debit side | £ | Credit side | £ |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Balance b/d | 4,260 | (1) Bank charges | 45 |
| (3) Direct transfer from customer | 680 | (2) Standing order: insurance | 120 |
| (5) Correction: 540 − 450 | 90 | (4) Dishonoured transfer | 250 |
| | | **Balance c/d** | **4,615** |
| | 5,030 | | 5,030 |

Journals: (1) Dr Bank charges, Cr Cash at bank £45. (2) Dr Insurance, Cr Cash at bank £120. (3) Dr Cash at bank, Cr Trade receivables £680. (4) Dr Trade receivables, Cr Cash at bank £250. (5) Dr Cash at bank, Cr Trade receivables £90.

**Step 2: reconcile the statement to the corrected balance** (items 6 to 8 only).

| Bank reconciliation at 30 June 20X2 | £ |
|---|---:|
| Balance per bank statement | 5,535 |
| Less: bank error, credited in error (8) | (300) |
| Add: uncleared receipts (7) | 720 |
| Less: unpresented payments (6) | (1,340) |
| **Balance per corrected cash at bank account** | **4,615** |

Statement of financial position: cash at bank **£4,615** (current asset).

*Easy way to see it:* the statement has not caught up with us (items 6 and 7), and the bank made a slip (item 8). Those three change nothing in our books. Items 1 to 5 are about us, so we fix our books.

### Example 5: A supplier statement reconciliation

Kamal Traders receives a statement from its supplier Brook Wholesale at 30 November. It shows **£6,430** owed. Kamal's payables ledger shows **£4,160**. Differences found:

1. A payment of £1,500 sent on 29 November is not yet on the statement.
2. Goods of £300 were returned, and Kamal has recorded the return. Brook has not issued the credit note.
3. An invoice of £470 is on the statement but Kamal never entered it.

| Account: Brook Wholesale at 30 November | £ |
|---|---:|
| Balance per supplier's statement | 6,430 |
| Less: payment sent, not yet recorded by Brook | (1,500) |
| Less: goods returned, credit note not yet issued | (300) |
| **Balance that should be in Kamal's payables ledger** | **4,630** |

Kamal's ledger: 4,160 + 470 (omitted invoice) = **£4,630**. The two agree.

Only item 3 is Kamal's mistake, so only item 3 needs a journal: Dr Purchases £470, Cr Trade payables £470. Profit goes **down** by £470. Items 1 and 2 are for Brook to fix.

## 6.5 Exam traps

1. **Journalising timing differences and bank errors.** Unpresented payments, uncleared receipts and bank mistakes never go through the cash at bank account. They appear only in the reconciliation.
2. **Forgetting to update the ledger for bank-only items.** Bank charges, interest, direct debits, standing orders and direct receipts must be recorded. A **dishonoured receipt** is credited to cash at bank and **debited to trade receivables** (the customer owes us again). It does not reduce income.
3. **Wrong signs with overdrafts.** An overdraft is a **debit** on the statement but a **credit** in our ledger. Write overdrafts in brackets and follow the formula carefully. The statement of financial position uses the **corrected ledger balance**, not the statement balance.
4. **Mixing up commission and principle.** Right class but wrong account (water bill on the rent page) is **commission**. Wrong class (asset vs expense, business vs owner) is **principle**. Compensating errors hide each other, and none of the five types stops the trial balance balancing.
5. **Not doubling.** If an invoice was recorded as a credit note, the entry was on the wrong side, so the correction is **twice** the amount. The same applies to a discount credited when it should have been debited.
6. **Wrong effect on profit.** Only income and expense lines move profit. Receipts from customers, drawings, capital, non-current assets and suspense do not. When an expense was wrongly capitalised, profit also rises by any depreciation already charged on it (so net effect = −amount + depreciation).
7. **Leaving a suspense balance in the statements.** It must be cleared with **every** item identified and journalised, so the final balance is nil. Do not "plug" the difference.
8. **Supplier statement viewpoint.** The statement is written from the supplier's side, so what we owe is a **debit**. Adjust only **our own** mistakes. Payments in transit and returns awaiting credit notes are the supplier's to record.

**Tahmina Bakery** has a year-end of **30 April 20X6**. The draft net profit is **£21,500**. The cash at bank account shows a **debit balance of £420** before any adjustments. The bank statement shows an **overdraft of £116**. Comparing the two records shows:

## 6.8 Answers and explanations

### Answers: extra practice questions

**Q1. Answer: C (error of principle; profit understated; non-current assets understated)**

- **Why:** A laptop is an asset (capital expenditure). Debiting it to stationery expense is putting it in the wrong **class** of account, so it is an error of principle. Expenses are too high, so profit is too low. The asset is missing from non-current assets.
- **Formula:** Correcting journal: Dr Computer equipment £1,200, Cr Stationery £1,200.
- **Easy example:** You buy a motorbike for the shop but write it on the "petrol and snacks" page. The shop looks poorer and shows no bike.
- **Why not the others:** A and B say commission, but an asset and an expense are different classes. A also gets both directions wrong. D has the right type but the directions are reversed (that would happen if an expense were capitalised).

**Q2. Answer: A and C**

- **Why:** A is how the ICAB workbook says a suspense account can start. C is the rule: no suspense account may remain in the statements.
- **Easy example:** A "to be sorted" tray must be empty before the accounts go to the owner.
- **Why not the others:** B is wrong because a suspense balance must be cleared and never appears in the statements, whether it is a debit or a credit. D is wrong because only items touching income or expense change profit (a customer part payment does not). E is wrong because the balance can be debit or credit.

**Q3. Answer: B (£5,810)**

- **Why:** Items 1 and 2 are for Orchid to record, so they are taken off the statement balance. Item 3 is Tanvir's own omission, so Tanvir adds it to its ledger.
- **Formula:** 7,260 − 1,100 − 350 = **£5,810**. Check: 5,190 + 620 = **£5,810**.
- **Easy example:** The shop's bill says £7,260, but you have already paid £1,100 and returned £350 of goods. You really owe £5,810, and you forgot one £620 bill in your own notebook.
- **Why not the others:** A (£4,570) subtracts the omitted invoice instead of adding it. C (£6,430) adds the omitted invoice to the statement balance, but it is already in that balance. D (£5,190) leaves Tanvir's ledger uncorrected.

**Q4. Answer: A, B and D**

- **Why:** Timing differences (A and B) and bank errors (D) are already correct in the ledger. They stay in the reconciliation. C and E are missing from the ledger, so they are journalised, and then they are no longer reconciling items.
- **Easy example:** A cheque in the post (A) and a lodgement on the way (B) are only a matter of days. The bank's mistake (D) is theirs to fix.
- **Why not the others:** C (bank charges) and E (direct debit) must be entered in the cash at bank account first.

**Q5. Answer: A (£3,030)**

- **Why:** The charges, the dishonoured transfer and the direct debit all reduce our balance. The unpresented cheques are already in the ledger, so they are **not** deducted again.
- **Formula:** 3,640 − 60 − 400 − 150 = **£3,030**.
- **Easy example:** You have already written "cheque given" in your notebook. Do not write it a second time.
- **Why not the others:** B (£3,430) forgets the dishonoured transfer. C (£3,830) adds the dishonoured transfer instead of deducting it. D (£2,530) deducts the unpresented cheques again.

**Q6. Answer: C (£1,695 overdrawn)**

- **Why:** First update the ledger for the interest charge. Then work from the statement to the corrected ledger balance, so the statement balance can be found.
- **Formula:** Corrected ledger: −1,250 − 85 = (1,335). Statement S: S − 300 + 1,600 − 940 = (1,335). So S = (1,335) + 300 − 1,600 + 940 = **(1,695)**.
- **Check:** (1,695) − 300 + 1,600 − 940 = (1,335).
- **Easy example:** The bank is wrongly £300 better off for us (deduct it), but has not yet seen our £1,600 lodgement (add it) or our £940 of cheques (deduct them).
- **Why not the others:** A (£375) reverses the signs of items 2 and 3. B (£1,610) forgets the £85 interest. D (£2,295) deducts the bank error instead of adding it back when working backwards.

**Q7. Answer: D**

- **Why:** The payment should have reduced payables by £760 and cash by £760, but only £670 was recorded. We need a further £90 debit to payables and £90 credit to cash.
- **Formula:** 760 − 670 = **£90**.
- **Easy example:** You paid £760 but wrote £670 in your notebook, so you must reduce what you owe by £90 more and the cash in your notebook by £90 more.
- **Why not the others:** A reverses the entry. B involves purchases, which are not affected: only the payment amount is wrong. C uses the sum of the two amounts.

**Q8. Answer: B (£53,800)**

- **Why:** (1) Repairs are an expense, so profit falls by £8,000, but the depreciation of £2,000 charged on them in error is added back. (2) An invoice was treated as a credit note, so purchases are wrong by **twice** £1,100.
- **Formula:** 62,000 − 8,000 + (8,000 × 25%) − (2 × 1,100) = 62,000 − 8,000 + 2,000 − 2,200 = **£53,800**.
- **Easy example:** You booked a bill as a refund. You must cancel the refund and then add the bill, so the bill moves your figures twice.
- **Why not the others:** A (£54,900) counts the invoice error only once. C (£51,800) forgets the depreciation add-back. D (£49,800) deducts the depreciation instead of adding it back.

### Answers: true or false

| # | Answer | Reason and easy example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **False** | Bank charges are an item we had not yet recorded, so we **do** need a journal (Dr Bank charges, Cr Cash at bank). A real timing difference would be a cheque still in the post. |
| 2 | **True** | We already took the payment out in our books, but the bank has not yet. Statement balance + uncleared receipts − unpresented payments = our corrected balance. |
| 3 | **True** | Two errors that are equal and opposite cancel, so total debits still equal total credits. The trial balance gives no warning, but the accounts are wrong. |
| 4 | **False** | A suspense balance must be cleared. It never appears in the financial statements, because it is not a real asset or liability. |
| 5 | **True** | Repairs are an expense, so the original error made expenses too high. Moving it to the asset removes the expense and profit goes **up** (before any depreciation). |
| 6 | **True** | The money never arrived, so cash at bank falls (credit) and the customer owes us again (debit trade receivables). Profit is not affected. |
| 7 | **False** | Our books are right. We tell the bank. The mistake is shown only in the reconciliation. |
| 8 | **False** | The statement is the supplier's view. What we owe is a **debit** on it, because to the supplier we are a receivable. |
