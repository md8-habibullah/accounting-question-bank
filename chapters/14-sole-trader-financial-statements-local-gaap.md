# Chapter 14: Sole trader financial statements under local GAAP

## 14.1 Start here: the idea in plain English

Imagine Rubina runs a tailoring shop on her own. There are no partners and no company. She is a **sole trader**. In law, Rubina and her shop are **one and the same person**. The shop's profit is her profit, and the shop's debts are her debts.

In Chapter 5 you already learned to build the two statements from a trial balance. This chapter adds the "local GAAP" flavour and three things that make a sole trader's accounts different from a company's:

1. **No tax.** The tax on Rubina's profit is *her own* personal bill, not the shop's. So the shop's accounts show no tax expense and no tax liability.
2. **A free-style profit and loss account.** The law does not force a sole trader to group costs under fixed headings. Rubina can list wages, rent and electricity one by one.
3. **A capital section instead of share capital and reserves.** There are no shareholders, no dividends and no reserves. The owner's stake is shown as capital: what she put in, plus profit, minus what she took out (**drawings**).

(*GAAP* means "generally accepted accounting principles (or practice)", the accepted rules and habits for preparing accounts. The workbook does not name one particular standard for this chapter. It simply uses the older, familiar vocabulary, so you must recognise both the old names and the IFRS names you met in the company chapters.)

Why prepare accounts at all, if nobody forces Rubina to publish them? Because banks, other lenders and regulatory authorities ask for them. The exam does **not** ask you to prepare a full sole trader set in the long-form question. It asks multiple-choice questions on this topic, with two main skills: use the **capital equation** to find profit (or any other missing figure), and **recognise local GAAP words** for the figures in the profit and loss account and balance sheet.

| Local GAAP word (this chapter) | IFRS word (Chapters 11 and 12) | Plain meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Profit and loss account | Statement of profit or loss | Income minus costs for the year |
| Balance sheet | Statement of financial position | Assets, liabilities and capital on one day |
| Sales | Revenue | Goods or services sold |
| Stock | Inventories | Goods held for sale |
| Debtors | Trade receivables | Customers who owe us |
| Creditors | Trade payables | Suppliers we owe |
| Fixed assets | Non-current assets | Things kept for more than a year |
| Net book value (an older name you may meet in other books) | Carrying amount | Cost less accumulated depreciation |
| Bad debt; allowance (provision) for doubtful debts | Irrecoverable debt; allowance for receivables | Debts we will not collect; the estimate of more to come |
| Net profit | Profit for the year | The last line of the profit and loss account |
| Ownership interest (capital) | Equity | The owner's stake in the business |

| Other new word | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Sole trader** | One person who owns and runs the business and has no separate legal identity from it |
| **Drawings** | Cash or goods (at cost) the owner takes for private use. Not an expense |
| **Capital introduced** | Money or assets the owner puts in during the year. Not income |
| **Net assets** | Assets minus liabilities. For a sole trader this always equals closing capital |
| **Ownership interest** | The section of the balance sheet that shows the owner's capital |

> **KEY FACT** For a sole trader, **net assets = closing capital**. The balance sheet has a top half (assets minus liabilities = net assets) and a bottom half (the ownership interest that explains where that figure came from).

## 14.2 Key facts to remember

- **What a sole trader is**
    - One person owns and runs the business. There are no shareholders.
    - The business has **no legal identity separate from its owner**. In law the owner and the business are one person.
    - Accounting still keeps the business and the owner's private life apart in the books (the business entity idea). That is why **drawings** exist: they record the owner taking value out for private use.
    - A *general partnership* has two or more owners. The workbook groups sole traders and general partnerships together as far less regulated than companies. This chapter gives no partnership calculations.
- **Regulation and purpose**
    - Sole traders and general partnerships do not have to produce and file **published accounts**.
    - They usually prepare only a profit and loss account and a balance sheet, because regulatory authorities and banks or other providers of finance need them.
- **Same as company accounts under local GAAP**
    - The terminology and format are very similar. Depreciation, accruals, prepayments, stock, irrecoverable debts and the double entry all work exactly as in the earlier chapters.
- **The three differences**
    - **Tax:** there is **no tax expense** in the profit and loss account and **no tax creditor** in the balance sheet. The sole trader has no separate legal identity, so the liability to pay tax on the profits belongs to the **owner**, not the business. If the owner pays her personal tax from the business bank account, it is **drawings**.
    - **Format of the profit and loss account:** there are **no formal requirements for headings**. Expenses can be listed separately. A company under IFRS must classify its expenses (Chapter 12).
    - **Ownership interest:** no share capital or reserves. The section shows opening capital, capital introduced, net profit (or loss), drawings and closing capital.
- **No statement of changes in equity** is required for a sole trader. The ownership interest section already shows the movement.
- **Drawings**
    - Drawings may be **cash** or **stock taken at cost**. They are deducted from capital.
    - They are **never** an expense. An owner's "salary", private bills and personal tax paid from the business account are all drawings.
    - Double entry: cash taken: Dr Drawings, Cr Bank. Goods taken: Dr Drawings, Cr Purchases (at cost). At the year-end the drawings account is closed to the capital account.
- **Capital introduced** can be cash or another asset (for example the owner's own van). Dr Bank (or the asset), Cr Capital introduced. It is not income and not in profit.
- **Profit and capital:** a profit adds to capital (Dr profit and loss account, Cr Capital). A loss reduces it.
- **Opening capital = net assets at the start of the year.** Closing capital = net assets at the end.
- **Working backwards:** if you know the opening and closing net assets, add back drawings and take off capital introduced to find the profit.
- **Balance sheet layout in the workbook:** fixed assets (cost, accumulated depreciation, carrying amount), then current assets, then current liabilities, then **net current assets**, then **non-current liabilities**, giving **net assets**. Below that comes the ownership interest.
- **Interest on a business loan** is an expense of the business. It is not the owner's drawings.

## 14.3 Formulas and layouts

> **FORMULA** Net assets = Fixed assets (carrying amount) + Current assets − Current liabilities − Non-current liabilities = Closing capital

> **FORMULA** Net current assets = Current assets − Current liabilities

> **FORMULA** Closing capital = Opening capital + Capital introduced + Net profit − Drawings (a loss is deducted instead of a profit added)

> **FORMULA** Net profit = Closing net assets − Opening net assets − Capital introduced + Drawings

> **FORMULA** Gross profit = Sales − Cost of sales, where Cost of sales = Opening stock + Purchases − Closing stock

> **FORMULA** Net profit = Gross profit − Expenses (there is no tax line for a sole trader)

> **FORMULA** Cost of goods taken by the owner = Selling price × 100 ÷ (100 + mark-up %). With a margin: Cost = Selling price × (100 − margin %) ÷ 100

> **FORMULA** Carrying amount of a fixed asset = Cost − Accumulated depreciation

**Which formula for which missing figure?** Start from Closing capital = Opening capital + Introduced + Profit − Drawings and rearrange.

| Missing figure | Working |
|---|---|
| Closing capital | Opening + Introduced + Profit − Drawings |
| Profit | Closing − Opening − Introduced + Drawings |
| Drawings | Opening + Introduced + Profit − Closing |
| Capital introduced | Closing − Opening − Profit + Drawings |
| Opening capital | Closing − Introduced − Profit + Drawings |

**Sole trader and company side by side**

| Topic | Sole trader (this chapter) | Company (Chapters 11 and 12) |
|---|---|---|
| Owner's section of the balance sheet | Ownership interest: capital, introduced, profit, drawings | Equity: share capital, share premium, retained earnings, other reserves |
| Owner takes value out | **Drawings**: deducted from capital, not an expense | **Dividends**: deducted from retained earnings, not an expense |
| Tax | None in the accounts (the owner's personal liability) | Tax expense in the profit or loss, tax payable in liabilities |
| Expense headings | No formal headings; list expenses | IFRS: classified (cost of sales, distribution costs, administrative expenses, finance costs) |
| Statement of changes in equity | Not required | Part of a full IFRS set (Chapter 12) |
| Regulation | Far less regulated; no published accounts required | Heavily regulated (Chapter 11) |

> **REMEMBER** A sole trader's balance sheet always ends with **closing capital = net assets**. If your two totals do not agree, find the error before you go further.

## 14.4 Worked examples

### Example 1: The ownership interest section

At 1 January 20X1 Farida's business had assets of £30,000 and liabilities of £11,600. During the year she paid in **£3,000** of new capital, the business made a net profit of **£21,700**, and she took **£14,500** as drawings.

| | £ |
|---|---:|
| Opening capital (net assets: 30,000 − 11,600) | 18,400 |
| Add: capital introduced | 3,000 |
| Add: net profit for the year | 21,700 |
| Less: drawings | (14,500) |
| **Closing capital = net assets at 31 December 20X1** | **28,600** |

If instead the business made a **net loss of £6,200** and drawings were **£4,000**, the closing capital would be 18,400 + 3,000 − 6,200 − 4,000 = **£11,200**.

*Easy way to see it:* the capital is Farida's jar. She put in £3,000, the business added £21,700, and she took £14,500 out. A loss takes money out of the jar, just like drawings.

### Example 2: Working backwards to find the profit (drawings in kind)

Imran keeps no full ledger. At 1 January his assets were £41,200 and his liabilities £15,700. At 31 December his assets were £52,800 and his liabilities £17,300. During the year he paid in **£4,000** of capital. He took **£900 a month** in cash. He also took goods from the shop for his family with a **selling price of £1,500**. The shop sells at a **mark-up of 50% on cost**.

1. Opening net assets = 41,200 − 15,700 = **£25,500**. Closing net assets = 52,800 − 17,300 = **£35,500**.
2. Cash drawings = 12 × 900 = **£10,800**.
3. Goods taken at **cost** = 1,500 × 100 ÷ 150 = **£1,000**. Total drawings = 10,800 + 1,000 = **£11,800**.
4. Net profit = 35,500 − 25,500 − 4,000 + 11,800 = **£17,800**.

*Check by rolling capital forward:* 25,500 + 4,000 + 17,800 − 11,800 = 35,500.

*Easy way to see it:* net assets rose by £10,000. Imran added £4,000 himself (so the business earned £4,000 less), and took £11,800 out (so the business must have earned £11,800 more). 10,000 − 4,000 + 11,800 = 17,800. If you used the selling price of £1,500 instead of cost, you would get £18,300: wrong.

### Example 4: Where does each item go?

A sole trader's bookkeeper meets six items. The table shows the correct entry and the effect of that **correct** entry on net profit and on closing capital.

| Item | Correct entry | Effect on net profit | Effect on closing capital |
|---|---|---|---|
| (a) The owner pays her personal income tax of £1,800 from the business bank account | Dr Drawings 1,800, Cr Bank 1,800 | None. Not an expense | Down £1,800 |
| (b) The owner takes goods that cost £400 (selling price £520) for home | Dr Drawings 400, Cr Purchases 400 | None. The cost of the goods is not charged to profit | Down £400 |
| (c) The owner's family holiday costs £900, paid from the business bank (if it was wrongly debited to sundry expenses, correct it with Dr Drawings, Cr Sundry expenses) | Dr Drawings 900, Cr Bank 900 | None. Not an expense | Down £900 |
| (d) The owner pays herself a "salary" of £700 a month | Dr Drawings (not wages) | None | Down £8,400 for the year |
| (e) The owner brings her own van, worth £6,000, into the business | Dr Fixed assets 6,000, Cr Capital introduced 6,000 | None | Up £6,000 |
| (f) The business pays £500 of interest on its own bank loan | Dr Loan interest 500, Cr Bank 500 | Down £500 | Down £500 |

*Easy way to see it:* ask one question: "**Is this the business's cost, or the owner's own use of value?**" The business's cost is an expense and reduces profit. The owner's own use is drawings and reduces capital directly. The owner's own tax is the owner's own use.

Notice that (a) to (d) all reduce capital **without touching profit**. Only the business's own costs, such as the loan interest in (f), go through the profit and loss account.

### Example 5: Finding capital introduced

Opening net assets were £52,000. Net assets grew by £9,000 over the year. The net profit was £26,500 and drawings were £19,000. How much capital did the owner introduce?

| | £ |
|---|---:|
| Closing capital = 52,000 + 9,000 | 61,000 |
| Opening capital | 52,000 |
| Net profit | 26,500 |
| Drawings | 19,000 |
| Capital introduced = 61,000 − 52,000 − 26,500 + 19,000 | **1,500** |

*Check:* 52,000 + 1,500 + 26,500 − 19,000 = 61,000.

## 14.5 Exam traps

1. **Looking for a tax line.** A sole trader's accounts have **no tax expense** and **no tax creditor**. The tax is the owner's own liability. A question may give a tax rate and a "tax charge" as bait. The answer for the business is nil.
2. **Drawings treated as an expense.** Drawings are deducted from **capital**, never from profit. This includes the owner's "salary", private bills and personal tax paid out of the business account.
3. **Goods taken at selling price.** Drawings in stock are valued at **cost**. If a mark-up is given, divide the selling price by (100 + mark-up) ÷ 100. Do not mix up mark-up (on cost) and margin (on selling price).
4. **Capital introduced mishandled.** It is not income and not in profit. When you work backwards from net assets, you **subtract** it. It can also be an asset (such as the owner's van), not only cash.
5. **Net assets and total assets.** Net assets = assets **minus liabilities** = closing capital. Do not use total assets as the capital.
6. **Unfamiliar local GAAP words.** Debtors, creditors, stock, fixed assets, profit and loss account and balance sheet are the same items as trade receivables, trade payables, inventories, non-current assets, statement of profit or loss and statement of financial position. Do not reject an option only because the name is old-fashioned. The workbook's own example even mixes "fixed assets" with "non-current liabilities".
7. **Accrual and prepayment swapped.** Treating a £400 accrual as a prepayment moves profit the wrong way twice: it should have **added** £400 to expenses but **took away** £400. Profit is **overstated by £800**, not £400.
8. **Looking for company items.** A sole trader has no share capital, share premium, reserves, dividends or statement of changes in equity. Net profit goes straight into the capital section.

## 14.8 Answers and explanations

### Answers: extra practice questions

**Q1. Answer: C (£48,000)**

- **Why:** A sole trader has no legal identity separate from the owner. The income tax is Hana's **own** bill. The business shows no tax expense and no tax creditor, so net profit stays at £48,000.
- **Formula:** Net profit = £48,000 − tax in the business accounts (nil) = **£48,000**.
- **Easy example:** A tea stall owner pays her own income tax from her own pocket. The stall's notebook never lists it.
- **Why not the others:** A (£9,600) is only the tax figure. B (£38,400) deducts tax as if the business were a company. D (£57,600) adds the tax to the profit.

**Q2. Answer: A, C and E**

- **Why:** A is correct because the tax belongs to the owner. C is correct because there are no formal heading requirements for a sole trader. E is correct because drawings reduce the capital in the ownership interest section.
- **Easy example:** The owner's jar of capital: profit goes in, drawings come out. There is no separate "shareholders" jar.
- **Why not the others:** B is wrong because a sole trader does not have to produce a statement of changes in equity. D is wrong because share capital and reserves belong to companies.

**Q3. Answer: B (£25,800)**

- **Why:** Add the capital introduced, take off the loss, and take off the drawings.
- **Formula:** 34,200 + 5,000 − 3,600 − 9,800 = **£25,800**.
- **Easy example:** She starts with £34,200 in the jar, adds £5,000, then loses £3,600 and takes out £9,800.
- **Why not the others:** A (£20,800) leaves out the capital introduced. C (£33,000) adds the loss instead of deducting it. D (£45,400) adds the drawings instead of deducting them.

**Q4. Answer: D (£22,300)**

- **Why:** Profit is the rise in net assets, plus everything Omar took out, minus the capital he put in himself. The goods are drawings at **cost**, and with a mark-up of 25% on cost, cost is selling price ÷ 1.25.
- **Formula:** Cost of goods = 3,000 × 100 ÷ 125 = 2,400. Cash drawings = 12 × 1,200 = 14,400. Profit = 75,500 − 60,000 − 10,000 + 14,400 + 2,400 = **£22,300**. Check: 60,000 + 10,000 + 22,300 − 16,800 = 75,500.
- **Easy example:** Omar's jar grew by £15,500, but £10,000 of that was his own money, and £16,800 was taken out, so the business must have earned £22,300.
- **Why not the others:** A (£19,900) ignores the goods. B (£22,150) treats the 25% as a margin on selling price (cost £2,250). C (£22,900) uses the selling price of £3,000.

**Q5. Answer: C**

- **Why:** The owner takes the goods out of the business. Drawings are debited at **cost** (£450) and the goods leave the stock, so credit purchases.
- **Easy example:** It is like a shopkeeper taking a packet of rice home. The business has lost what the rice cost it, not what it could sell for.
- **Why not the others:** A reverses the entry. B uses the selling price and records a sale that never happened. D credits capital, which would cancel the drawings, and the goods would still look as if they were in the business.

**Q6. Answer: A (£57,500)**

- **Why:** Net assets = fixed assets + net current assets − non-current liabilities, and net assets always equal closing capital.
- **Formula:** Net current assets = 31,000 − 18,500 = 12,500. Net assets = 85,000 + 12,500 − 40,000 = **£57,500**.
- **Easy example:** Everything the business owns, minus everything it owes, is what is left for the owner.
- **Why not the others:** B (£97,500) leaves out the loan. C (£76,000) forgets the current liabilities (85,000 + 31,000 − 40,000). D (£137,500) adds the loan instead of deducting it.

**Q7. Answer: B and D**

- **Why:** Stock is the older word for inventories. Fixed assets is the older word for non-current assets.
- **Easy example:** Like "lorry" and "truck": two names, the same thing.
- **Why not the others:** A swaps debtors (customers owe us, so receivables) for payables. C swaps creditors (we owe them, so payables) for receivables. E: the profit and loss account matches the statement of profit or loss, not the statement of cash flows.

**Q8. Answer: B (net profit up £1,200 and closing capital unchanged)**

- **Why:** School fees are the owner's private spending, so they are **drawings**, not a business expense. Removing them from expenses raises profit by £1,200. Adding them to drawings lowers capital by £1,200. The two cancel, and the net assets in the balance sheet do not change because the cash had already left.
- **Formula:** Profit 25,000 + 1,200 = 26,200. Closing capital: +1,200 (profit) − 1,200 (drawings) = **no change**.
- **Easy example:** Moving £1,200 from the "business cost" list to the "my own use" list changes the labels, not the money in the till.
- **Why not the others:** A forgets the extra drawings. C forgets that profit rises. D has the profit moving the wrong way.

### Answers: true or false

| # | Answer | Reason and easy example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **False** | The owner, not the business, is liable for the tax on the profit. A sole trader's accounts have no tax expense and no tax creditor. |
| 2 | **True** | Drawings reduce the owner's capital in the balance sheet. They are the owner taking value out of the jar, not a business cost. |
| 3 | **False** | The workbook says a sole trader has no requirement to produce a statement of changes in equity. The ownership interest section shows the movement. |
| 4 | **True** | Net assets (assets minus liabilities) and closing capital are the same figure, shown from two directions. |
| 5 | **False** | Goods taken are recorded at **cost**, not selling price. The business loses what the goods cost it. |
| 6 | **False** | There are no formal requirements for headings in a sole trader's profit and loss account, so expenses can be listed separately. |
| 7 | **True** | Debtors are customers who owe the business money, the older name for trade receivables. |
| 8 | **True** | Personal tax is the owner's own bill, so paying it from the business bank is the owner taking money out: Dr Drawings, Cr Bank. |
