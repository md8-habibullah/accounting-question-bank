# Chapter 5: Preparing basic financial statements

## 5.1 Start here: the idea in plain English

Imagine Meena sells snacks to offices. All year she writes every sale, purchase and payment in her books (the **ledger accounts**). On the last day of the year she wants two simple answers:

1. **Did I make money this year?** The answer is a report called the **statement of profit or loss**. It lists what the business earned (revenue) and what it cost (cost of sales and expenses). The result is the **profit for the year**.
2. **What do I own and what do I owe on the last day?** The answer is the **statement of financial position** (the old name is *balance sheet*). It is a photograph of the business on one single day.

Getting from the books to the two reports follows the same five steps every time:

1. Find the **balance** (the leftover amount) of every ledger account.
2. List the balances in a **trial balance**: debit balances in one column, credit balances in the other. The two columns must add up to the same total.
3. Make any last corrections or year-end adjustments (these are called *adjustment journals*).
4. Take the **income and expense** balances to the statement of profit or loss. The last line is the profit.
5. Take the **asset, liability and capital** balances to the statement of financial position. Add the profit to the owner's capital and take off the owner's **drawings**.

Think of the owner's **capital** as a money jar that the business keeps for the owner. The owner puts money in at the start (capital). Every profit the business earns drops into the jar. Every time the owner takes money or goods out for private use (**drawings**), the jar gets lighter. What is left at the end is the **closing capital**. A loss takes money out of the jar.

| Word | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Trial balance** | A list of all ledger balances in a debit column and a credit column. It is **not** part of the double entry; it is a list taken **from** it. |
| **Initial / final trial balance** | Initial = before adjustments. Final = after adjustment journals. The statements are built from the final one. |
| **Statement of profit or loss** | Revenue minus all costs for a period = profit or loss. |
| **Statement of financial position** | Assets, liabilities and capital on one date. |
| **Gross profit** | Revenue minus cost of sales (the cost of the goods actually sold). |
| **Drawings** | Money or goods taken out of the business by the owner. **Not an expense.** |
| **Balance c/d and b/d** | c/d = carried down (the closing balance). b/d = brought down (the same figure, starting the next period). |
| **Trade receivables / trade payables** | Receivables = customers who owe us money. Payables = suppliers we owe money to. |
| **Inventories** | Goods held for sale and not yet sold (also called stock). |
| **Capital introduced** | Extra money the owner pays into the business during the year. It is not income. |
| **Accumulated depreciation** | The total depreciation (wear and tear charged to profit) on an asset so far. |
| **Carrying amount** | A non-current asset's cost minus its accumulated depreciation. |

> **KEY FACT** Profit appears in **two** places: as the last line of the statement of profit or loss, and added to capital in the statement of financial position. Drawings appear in **one** place only: deducted from capital.

## 5.2 Key facts to remember

- **The trial balance**
    - It lists every ledger balance. **Debit balances**: assets, expenses and drawings. **Credit balances**: liabilities, income and capital.
    - If total debits equal total credits, the trial balance "balances". This does **not** prove that the books are error-free. Some errors do not upset the totals: a transaction left out completely (**omission**), a figure posted to the wrong account (**commission**), an entry in the wrong category (**error of principle**), and two errors that cancel each other (**compensating**). A **transposition** error (two digits swapped, for example 540 written as 450) usually *does* make the columns differ, unless the same wrong figure is posted to both the debit and the credit.
    - A computer cannot post an unbalanced entry, so its trial balance always balances. The figures can still be wrong, so a good accountant checks that each balance looks sensible (this is **professional scepticism**).
    - The **initial** trial balance is adjusted by journals to give a **final** trial balance. In a columnar layout: for a debit balance, add debit adjustments and subtract credit adjustments; for a credit balance, do the opposite.
- **Closing off the ledger accounts**
    - Balance off an account like this: total both sides, write the **larger total** on both sides, and put the difference as the **balance c/d** on the smaller side. The same figure is then written as **balance b/d** on the opposite side, below the totals.
    - **Income and expense** accounts are transferred to the statement of profit or loss account and end with a **nil** balance. They start the next year at zero.
    - **Asset, liability and capital** accounts are **carried down**. The closing balance becomes the opening balance of the next year.
    - In the statement of profit or loss ledger account, expenses are on the **debit** side and income on the **credit** side. If credits are bigger, the difference is a **profit**. If debits are bigger, it is a **loss**.
    - **Profit:** debit statement of profit or loss account, credit capital. **Loss:** debit capital, credit statement of profit or loss account. **Drawings:** debit capital, credit drawings.
- **Statement of profit or loss** (for a trading business)
    - Layout: Revenue, less cost of sales, gives **gross profit**. Add other income. Deduct expenses. The result is **profit for the year**.
    - **Cost of sales** = opening inventory + purchases − closing inventory. If a question gives no inventory, cost of sales is just purchases.
    - Expenses can be grouped as distribution costs (selling and delivery), administrative costs (office running) and finance costs (loan and overdraft interest). A simple list is acceptable in this chapter.
    - **Drawings are not an expense.** Goods taken by the owner are drawings too, even if the question does not use the word "drawings".
    - **VAT** (value added tax) is a tax added to invoices that the business collects for the tax authority. For a VAT-registered business, revenue and purchases are shown **without VAT**. VAT is not the business's income or cost; VAT owed to the tax authority is a liability.
- **Statement of financial position**
    - **Non-current assets** (kept for more than a year, for example land, buildings, vehicles, equipment) are shown at cost less accumulated depreciation.
    - **Current assets**: inventories, trade receivables, prepayments, cash at bank. **Current liabilities**: due within 12 months, for example trade payables, accruals, bank overdraft. **Non-current liabilities**: due after more than 12 months, for example a bank loan repayable in four years.
    - A **bank overdraft** is a credit balance on the cash at bank account. It is a **liability**, not an asset.
    - **Capital section:** opening capital + capital introduced + profit for the year − drawings = closing capital.
    - Total assets must equal total capital plus liabilities.
    - **Accounting equation:** assets = capital + liabilities. So capital = assets − liabilities. In a trial balance, capital is sometimes the balancing figure.
- **Working backwards (incomplete records):** if you know opening and closing net assets, add back drawings and take off capital introduced to find the profit.
- **Missing figures:** a receivables or payables account is a T-account. Put in what you know; the missing figure is the balancing figure.

## 5.3 Formulas and layouts

> **FORMULA** Assets = Capital + Liabilities, so Capital = Assets − Liabilities

> **FORMULA** Gross profit = Revenue − Cost of sales

> **FORMULA** Cost of sales = Opening inventory + Purchases − Closing inventory

> **FORMULA** Profit for the year = Gross profit + Other income − Expenses

> **FORMULA** Closing capital = Opening capital + Capital introduced + Profit for the year − Drawings (a loss is deducted instead)

> **FORMULA** Profit (working backwards) = Closing capital − Opening capital − Capital introduced + Drawings

> **FORMULA** Closing trade receivables = Opening receivables + Credit sales − Cash received from credit customers

> **FORMULA** Closing trade payables = Opening payables + Credit purchases − Payments to credit suppliers

> **FORMULA** Amount without VAT (VAT at 20%) = Amount including VAT × 100 ÷ 120 (this is the same as dividing by 1.2)

**Which side of the trial balance?**

| Debit column (Dr) | Credit column (Cr) |
|---|---|
| Non-current assets at cost, inventory, trade receivables, prepayments, cash at bank | Trade payables, accruals, bank overdraft, bank loan |
| Purchases, wages, rent and all other expenses | Sales (revenue) and other income |
| **Drawings** | **Capital**, and **accumulated depreciation** (it reduces an asset, so it is a credit) |

## 5.4 Worked examples

### Example 1: From transactions to the two statements

Meena starts a snack business on 1 January 20X1 (*20X1 just means "some year"*). During the year:

1. She pays **£4,000** into a new business bank account (capital).
2. She buys a stall (equipment) for **£1,800** cash.
3. She buys goods on credit for **£3,500**.
4. She pays suppliers **£2,900**.
5. Cash sales are **£4,100** (banked). Credit sales are **£2,300**.
6. Credit customers pay **£1,500**.
7. Other expenses paid by bank: **£1,200**.
8. She takes **£900** from the bank for herself (drawings).

By the year-end she has sold all the goods, so cost of sales is just the purchases.

**Step 1: balance the cash at bank account**

| Cash at bank: debit side | £ | Credit side | £ |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Capital introduced | 4,000 | Equipment | 1,800 |
| Cash sales | 4,100 | Trade payables (suppliers) | 2,900 |
| Trade receivables (customers) | 1,500 | Other expenses | 1,200 |
| | | Drawings | 900 |
| | | **Balance c/d** | **2,800** |
| | 9,600 | | 9,600 |

The debit total (9,600) is larger, so the balance of **£2,800** is a **debit** (cash in the bank). The other balances are: trade receivables 2,300 − 1,500 = **800** (debit), trade payables 3,500 − 2,900 = **600** (credit), sales 4,100 + 2,300 = **6,400** (credit).

**Step 2: the trial balance**

| Account | Debit £ | Credit £ |
|---|---:|---:|
| Cash at bank | 2,800 | |
| Equipment | 1,800 | |
| Purchases | 3,500 | |
| Trade receivables | 800 | |
| Other expenses | 1,200 | |
| Drawings | 900 | |
| Capital | | 4,000 |
| Trade payables | | 600 |
| Sales | | 6,400 |
| **Total** | **11,000** | **11,000** |

**Step 3: the statement of profit or loss for the year ended 31 December 20X1**

| | £ |
|---|---:|
| Revenue | 6,400 |
| Cost of sales (purchases) | (3,500) |
| **Gross profit** | **2,900** |
| Other expenses | (1,200) |
| **Profit for the year** | **1,700** |

**Step 4: the statement of financial position at 31 December 20X1**

| | £ |
|---|---:|
| Non-current assets: equipment | 1,800 |
| Current assets: trade receivables 800 + cash at bank 2,800 | 3,600 |
| **Total assets** | **5,400** |
| Opening capital | 4,000 |
| Add: profit for the year | 1,700 |
| Less: drawings | (900) |
| **Closing capital** | **4,800** |
| Current liabilities: trade payables | 600 |
| **Total capital and liabilities** | **5,400** |

Check: assets 5,400 = capital 4,800 + liabilities 600. **Closing capital £4,800.** Next year the capital account starts with a credit balance b/d of £4,800 and the cash account with a debit balance of £2,800.

*Easy way to see it:* Meena put in £4,000, earned £1,700 and took £900 out of the jar. £4,000 + £1,700 − £900 = £4,800 left in the jar.

### Example 2: Balancing off a ledger account, and where each balance goes

At 1 January the trade receivables account has a balance of **£3,000**. Credit sales in the year are **£9,500**. Cash received from credit customers is **£8,200**.

| Trade receivables: debit side | £ | Credit side | £ |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Opening balance b/d | 3,000 | Cash received | 8,200 |
| Credit sales | 9,500 | **Balance c/d (balancing figure)** | **4,300** |
| | 12,500 | | 12,500 |
| Balance b/d | 4,300 | | |

Debits 12,500 − credits 8,200 = **£4,300** debit balance. It goes into the statement of financial position as a current asset, and starts next year as the opening balance.

By contrast, a **sales** account and a **rent** account are **not** carried down. Their balances are moved to the statement of profit or loss account, and they start the new year at nil.

| Type of account | At the year-end | Next year starts with |
|---|---|---|
| Asset, liability, capital | Balance **carried down** to the statement of financial position | The same balance (b/d) |
| Income, expense | Balance **transferred** to the statement of profit or loss | Nil |
| Drawings | Transferred to the capital account | Nil |

### Example 4: Cost of sales and gross profit with inventory and VAT

Nadia runs a VAT-registered phone shop (VAT 20%). Revenue for the year, **excluding VAT**, is **£60,000**. Purchases, **including VAT**, are **£39,600**. Opening inventory is **£4,500** and closing inventory is **£5,500**.

1. Purchases without VAT = 39,600 × 100 ÷ 120 = **£33,000**.
2. Cost of sales = 4,500 + 33,000 − 5,500 = **£32,000**.

| Extract: statement of profit or loss | £ |
|---|---:|
| Revenue | 60,000 |
| Opening inventory | 4,500 |
| Purchases (without VAT) | 33,000 |
| Less: closing inventory | (5,500) |
| Cost of sales | (32,000) |
| **Gross profit** | **28,000** |

The closing inventory of £5,500 is also shown as a **current asset** in the statement of financial position. It is the cost of goods bought but not yet sold, so it is taken out of this year's cost and carried into next year.

### Example 5: Working backwards, with drawings in kind

Rafiq does not keep a full set of books. At 1 January his assets were £25,000 and his liabilities £9,000. At 31 December his assets were £31,500 and his liabilities £8,200. During the year he paid in **£1,500** of new capital. He took **£9,000** in cash and goods costing **£700** from the shop for his family.

| Step | £ |
|---|---:|
| Opening capital = 25,000 − 9,000 | 16,000 |
| Closing capital = 31,500 − 8,200 | 23,300 |
| Total drawings = 9,000 + 700 | 9,700 |
| Profit = 23,300 − 16,000 − 1,500 + 9,700 | **15,500** |

*Check by rolling capital forward:* 16,000 + 1,500 + 15,500 − 9,700 = 23,300.

*Easy way to see it:* the jar went up by £7,300. But Rafiq added £1,500 himself, and took £9,700 out. So the business must have earned £7,300 − £1,500 + £9,700 = £15,500 to produce that result.

## 5.5 Exam traps

1. **Drawings in the statement of profit or loss.** Drawings are **never** an expense. They reduce capital in the statement of financial position. Goods taken by the owner are drawings (at cost) even when the question never says "drawings".
2. **Profit and capital muddle.** Profit **adds** to capital; a loss **reduces** it; drawings **reduce** it. Adding drawings, forgetting the profit, or forgetting **capital introduced** are the usual slips. Also remember a loss is a *debit* to capital.
3. **Overdraft shown as an asset.** A **credit** balance on the cash at bank account is an overdraft. It is a **current liability**. Do not net it against anything.
4. **Accumulated depreciation.** It is a **credit** balance that reduces the cost of the non-current assets. It is not a liability and not an expense. The depreciation **charge** for the year is the expense.
5. **Inventory used twice or not at all.** Opening inventory is a debit balance in the trial balance and goes **into cost of sales**. Closing inventory is **deducted** in cost of sales **and** shown as a **current asset**.
6. **Wrong current / non-current split.** A bank loan due in four years is a **non-current** liability. Only amounts due within 12 months are current.
7. **"The trial balance balances, so it is right."** It does not prove that. An omission, a wrong account, a compensating error or an error of principle leave it balanced. (A transposition made on only one side would unbalance it.) These errors come back in the chapter on errors and corrections.
8. **VAT in the figures.** For a VAT-registered business, revenue and purchases in the statement of profit or loss exclude VAT. If purchases include 20% VAT, divide by 1.2 (not "take off 20%").

## 5.8 Answers and explanations

### Answers: extra practice questions

**Q1. Answer: B (£5,900)**

- **Why:** Only **credit** sales go through the receivables account. Cash sales never create a receivable. Customers' payments reduce the balance.
- **Formula:** 4,200 + 38,600 − 36,900 = **£5,900**.

- **Why not the others:** A (£1,700) forgets the opening balance (38,600 − 36,900). C (£12,900) adds the cash sales of £7,000, which never belonged in this account. D (£2,500) adds the receipts and takes off the sales: the wrong way round.

**Q2. Answer: A and C**

- **Why:** Capital is what the business owes the owner, so it is a credit. Accumulated depreciation reduces the cost of an asset, so it is also a credit. Drawings, opening inventory and purchases are debits.
- **Easy example:** Think of debit as "things the business has or has spent" and credit as "things it owes or has earned". Capital is owed to the owner.
- **Why not the others:** B (drawings) reduce capital and are a debit. D (opening inventory) is a debit because it is an asset at the start of the year. E (purchases) is an expense, so a debit.

**Q3. Answer: C (£53,400)**

- **Why:** Revenue is already without VAT. Purchases include VAT, so remove it first: divide by 1.2, because VAT is 20% **of the price without VAT**, so 1/6 of the VAT-inclusive price. Then add opening inventory and take away closing inventory.

**Q5. Answer: A, B and D**

**Q6. Answer: D**

- **Why:** The owner has taken stock for private use. That is drawings (a debit). The goods leave the business at **cost**, so purchases (cost of goods) are reduced by crediting that account. No sale is made and no profit is earned on goods the owner takes.

**Q7. Answer: B**

- **Formula:** 86,500 − 79,200 = £7,300 loss. Dr Capital 7,300, Cr Statement of profit or loss account 7,300.
- **Easy example:** The jar lost £7,300 during the year, so the owner's share in the jar goes down.

### Answers: true or false

| # | Answer | Reason and easy example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **False** | The initial trial balance is only a **list** taken from the ledger accounts. It is not part of the double entry. Like a class register: it lists the pupils, it is not a pupil. |
| 2 | **True** | Equal totals do not prove the books are right. An omission, a wrong account, a compensating error or an error of principle all leave the columns equal. |
| 3 | **True** | Drawings are the owner taking money out, not a cost of earning profit. They are deducted from capital. |
| 4 | **True** | Profit adds to capital; loss reduces it (debit capital). |
| 5 | **False** | Expense (and income) balances are transferred to the statement of profit or loss and start next year at **nil**. Only asset, liability and capital balances are carried down. |
| 6 | **True** | A credit balance on the bank account means the bank is owed money: an overdraft, which is a current liability. |
| 7 | **True** | Closing inventory is taken off the cost of sales (so profit is not reduced by goods still unsold) and shown as a current asset. |
| 8 | **False** | A loan due in more than 12 months is a **non-current** liability. Only the part due within 12 months is current. |
