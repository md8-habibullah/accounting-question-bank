# Chapter 11: Company financial statements

## 11.1 Start here: the idea in plain English

Imagine Rahim runs a tea stall alone. If the stall cannot pay its suppliers, they can take Rahim's own savings. In law, Rahim and the stall are **one and the same person**. This is a **sole trader**.

Now Rahim and 49 friends start **Padma Tea Ltd**. Each friend puts in money and receives **shares**. The company is a **separate legal person**. It signs contracts, owns the stall, owes money and pays tax **in its own name**. If it fails, the friends lose only the money they put in (**limited liability**). The friends (**shareholders**) **own** the company. A small group of **directors** runs it day to day. Because outsiders can only claim against the company, the law makes companies follow strict rules and set layouts when they prepare their accounts.

Think of the company as a pizza cut into equal slices. Each slice is a **share**. Each slice has a printed **nominal value** (also called **par value** or face value), for example 50p. It is only a label. The company may sell the slice for more, for example £1.20. The extra £0.70 is the **share premium**. If shareholders later sell their slices to each other at £5, that does **not** change the company's books: the company is not part of that sale.

A company's accounts look almost like a sole trader's. Three things change:

1. The owners' money section is called **equity** and has several parts (share capital, share premium, retained earnings).
2. Owners take profit out as **dividends**, not drawings.
3. There are two extra lines in the statement of profit or loss: **finance costs** (interest on loans) and **income tax** (the company pays tax on its profit).

| Word | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Share** | One equal slice of the ownership of the company |
| **Nominal (par) value** | The printed value of one share (for example 50p). Share capital is always recorded at this value |
| **Share premium** | What shareholders paid **above** the nominal value |
| **Dividend** | A share of profit paid to shareholders (a payout to owners, not a cost of doing business) |
| **Retained earnings** | Profits kept in the company (not paid out as dividends) |
| **Reserve** | Any part of equity other than share capital (share premium, retained earnings, revaluation surplus) |
| **Bonus issue** | Free new shares given to existing shareholders (also called a capitalisation issue or scrip issue) |
| **Rights issue** | New shares offered to existing shareholders for cash, usually cheaper than the market price |
| **Loan notes / debentures** | Borrowing from lenders; the company owes the money back and pays interest (the workbook also says loan stock or bonds) |
| **Provision** | A liability where the amount or timing is uncertain, but payment is probable |

> **KEY FACT** Equity = share capital (at nominal value) + share premium + retained earnings + other reserves. It also equals **total assets minus total liabilities**.

## 11.2 Key facts to remember

- **A limited company** is a separate legal entity. It can make contracts, sue and be sued, and it pays tax on its profits. Shareholders are not personally liable for its debts.
- **Public company** (name ends **plc**): may offer its shares and loan notes to the public. **Private company** (name ends **Ltd**): may not. Public companies are more strictly regulated.
- Company accounts are regulated: the workbook names **IFRS Accounting Standards** and company law (the Companies Act 1994) as the main sources.
- **Equity shares (ordinary shares):** each share is an equal part of ownership. There is **no fixed dividend**: the directors decide the amount each year.
- **Preference shares:** a fixed dividend rate, paid **before** any equity dividend.
    - **Irredeemable** (company need not buy them back): part of **equity**. The dividend is deducted from **retained earnings**.
    - **Redeemable** (company may or must buy them back): a **non-current liability**. The dividend is a **finance cost** in the statement of profit or loss.
- Share capital in the statement of financial position is the **called-up** amount at **nominal value**. If called-up money is still unpaid, the unpaid amount is an **other receivable** (an asset).
- **Share issue:** Debit Cash (number × issue price). Credit Share capital (number × nominal value). Credit Share premium (number × premium per share).
- **Number of shares** = share capital ÷ nominal value. Always work this out before calculating a dividend.
- **Retained earnings** = opening balance + profit for the year − dividends − any bonus issue charged to it. A loss reduces it.
- **Share premium** cannot be paid out as a dividend. It can be used for a **bonus issue**.
- **Other reserves** are also part of equity. Examples: a **revaluation surplus** (when non-current assets are revalued upwards) and a **general reserve** (a transfer from retained earnings: Debit Retained earnings, Credit Other reserves).
- **Dividends** are a distribution of profit, **not an expense**. Debit Retained earnings, credit Cash when the dividend is paid. This is how the workbook and the exam questions record it: the dividend is declared and paid in the same year.
    - Under IFRS (IAS 10) a dividend is recognised, and only then becomes a liability, when it is **declared**. A **final** dividend is declared when the shareholders approve it. An **interim** dividend is declared by the directors during the year and is usually paid at once.
    - A dividend that is only **proposed or declared after the year-end** is **not** a liability at the year-end. It is disclosed in the notes and recorded in the next year.
    - If a question gives a dividend that is declared but unpaid at the year-end, it is a payable and is already deducted from retained earnings. The workbook does not test this: unless told otherwise, assume dividends are paid in the year they are declared.
- **Bonus issue** ("a for b" means a new shares for every b held): no cash comes in. Debit Share premium (as far as it goes, in an exam), then Retained earnings. Credit Share capital. **Total equity does not change.**
- **Rights issue:** cash comes in. It is recorded exactly like a share issue (capital at nominal value, the rest to share premium). **Total equity increases.**
- **Loan notes, debentures, bank loans:** **non-current liabilities**. Any amount repayable **within 12 months** of the year-end is shown as a **current liability**.
- **Interest on loans** is a **finance cost** in the statement of profit or loss. Unpaid interest at the year-end is an **accrual** (within trade and other payables).
- **Provision** (IAS 37): create it only if (1) there is a **present obligation** from a past event and (2) payment is **probable** (more than 50% likely). Then provide the **full estimate**. Possible-only claims are **not** provided for (they are disclosed in the notes).
- **Provision vs accrual:** an accrual is for something already received where the amount is fairly certain (an electricity bill). A provision has uncertainty about the amount or timing (a legal claim).
- **Income tax:** the company's own expense (it is owed to the tax authority, called the Commissioner of Taxes in the workbook), shown after profit before tax. Unpaid tax is a **current liability** (tax payable). VAT and tax deducted at source owed are **other payables**, not tax payable.
- **Under-provision / over-provision:** last year's tax estimate was too low / too high. The difference goes through **this year's** tax expense (under-provision adds, over-provision reduces). Old accounts are not reopened.
- **Revenue (IFRS 15):** recognise it when **control** of the goods or services passes to the customer (for example on delivery). A signed order or a cash payment alone is not enough. Revenue is shown **net of discounts, refunds and VAT**.
    - Not revenue: a profit on disposal of a non-current asset, and interest received or dividends received (shown as **finance income**).
- **Presentation (IAS 1):** show the company name, the date or period (normally 12 months), **comparative figures**, and separate **current** and **non-current** items. Expenses are classified as **cost of sales** (including delivery inwards), **distribution costs** (delivery to customers, delivery vehicle depreciation), **administrative expenses** and **finance costs**. Gross profit less distribution costs and administrative expenses is **profit from operations** (the workbook also calls it operating profit).
- **IAS 8:** an accounting **policy** is a rule (for example "use FIFO for inventory"). An accounting **estimate** is a judgement (useful life, allowance for receivables, chance of winning a legal claim).

## 11.3 Formulas and layouts

> **FORMULA** Number of shares = Share capital ÷ Nominal value

> **FORMULA** On a share issue: Cash = Shares × Issue price; Share capital = Shares × Nominal value; Share premium = Shares × (Issue price − Nominal value)

> **FORMULA** New shares in an "a for b" bonus or rights issue = Existing shares × a ÷ b

> **FORMULA** Equity dividend = Number of shares × Dividend per share. Preference dividend = Preference share capital × Rate

> **FORMULA** Closing retained earnings = Opening retained earnings + Profit for the year − Dividends paid − Bonus issue charged to retained earnings

> **FORMULA** Dividends paid (working backwards) = Opening retained earnings + Profit for the year − Bonus issue charged to retained earnings − Closing retained earnings

> **FORMULA** Interest for the period = Loan × Interest rate × (Months ÷ 12)

> **FORMULA** Tax expense in profit or loss = This year's estimate + Under-provision − Over-provision

> **FORMULA** Tax paid (working backwards) = Opening tax payable + Tax expense − Closing tax payable

> **FORMULA** Provision expense for the year = Closing provision − Opening provision + Amounts paid out of the provision

## 11.4 Worked examples

### Example 1: Issuing shares (at a premium, and part unpaid)

Anwar Foods Ltd issues **150,000 ordinary shares of 25p each at 90p** per share. All the cash is received.

1. Cash = 150,000 × 90p = **£135,000**.
2. Share capital = 150,000 × 25p = **£37,500** (nominal value only).
3. Share premium = 150,000 × (90p − 25p) = 150,000 × 65p = **£97,500**.

| Journal | Debit (£) | Credit (£) |
|---|---:|---:|
| Cash at bank | 135,000 | |
| Share capital | | 37,500 |
| Share premium | | 97,500 |

Check: 37,500 + 97,500 = 135,000, the cash received.

*Easy way to see it:* a concert ticket has "£0.25" printed on it, but the organiser sells it for £0.90. The printed value is share capital. The extra £0.65 is premium.

*Variation (part unpaid):* Barua Ltd issues 100,000 £1 shares at par. Holders of 8,000 shares have not paid yet. Debit Cash £92,000, debit **Other receivables £8,000**, credit Share capital £100,000. The statement of financial position still shows share capital of **£100,000**. The £8,000 is an asset.

### Example 2: Bonus issue and rights issue side by side

A company has **500,000 shares of 20p** each. Share capital £100,000, share premium £60,000, retained earnings £140,000.

- **1 for 5 bonus issue:** 500,000 × 1/5 = 100,000 new shares × 20p = **£20,000**. Debit Share premium £20,000, credit Share capital £20,000.
- **1 for 5 rights issue at 80p:** 100,000 new shares. Cash = 100,000 × 80p = **£80,000**. Share capital = 100,000 × 20p = £20,000. Share premium = 100,000 × 60p = £60,000.

| £ | Before | After the bonus issue | After the rights issue |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Share capital | 100,000 | 120,000 | 120,000 |
| Share premium | 60,000 | 40,000 | 120,000 |
| Retained earnings | 140,000 | 140,000 | 140,000 |
| **Total equity** | **300,000** | **300,000** | **380,000** |
| Cash received | | nil | 80,000 |

Each issue is applied on its own to the "Before" position (they are alternatives, not one after the other).

*Easy way to see it:* a **bonus issue** cuts the same pizza into more slices (nobody pays, nothing new comes in). A **rights issue** sells extra slices to the existing members (cash comes in).

### Example 3: Dividends and retained earnings

**Part A.** Kadir Ltd has share capital of **£300,000 in 50p ordinary shares**, **£80,000 of 5% irredeemable preference shares** and **£50,000 of 8% redeemable preference shares**. It declares an equity dividend of **4p per share**. All dividends are declared and paid in cash in the year.

1. Number of ordinary shares = 300,000 ÷ 0.50 = **600,000**. Equity dividend = 600,000 × 4p = **£24,000**.
2. Irredeemable preference dividend = 80,000 × 5% = **£4,000**.
3. Debit **Retained earnings** £28,000 (24,000 + 4,000), credit Cash £28,000.
4. Redeemable preference dividend = 50,000 × 8% = **£4,000**. This is a **finance cost**, not a deduction from retained earnings.

**Part B (working backwards).** Retained earnings were £310,000 at the start and £350,000 at the end. Profit for the year was £95,000. A bonus issue of £40,000 was charged to retained earnings.

| Retained earnings: debit side | £ | Credit side | £ |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Bonus issue | 40,000 | Balance b/d | 310,000 |
| **Dividends paid (balancing figure)** | **15,000** | Profit for the year | 95,000 |
| Balance c/d | 350,000 | | |
| | 405,000 | | 405,000 |

Dividends paid = **£15,000**.

### Example 4: Loan interest, tax and a provision

**(a) Loan notes.** Zahid Ltd has **£200,000 of 10% loan notes** in issue all year. Interest for the 12 months to 31 March is paid each 31 March. The year-end is **31 December 20X5**. £50,000 of the notes is repayable on 30 June 20X6.

- Interest for the year = 200,000 × 10% = **£20,000** (finance cost).
- Interest accrued at 31 December = April to December = 9 months = 200,000 × 10% × 9/12 = **£15,000**. It is the same at the start of the year. Cash paid in the year was £20,000.

| Interest account: debit side | £ | Credit side | £ |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Cash paid (31 March) | 20,000 | Opening accrual b/d | 15,000 |
| Closing accrual c/d | 15,000 | **Statement of profit or loss** | **20,000** |
| | 35,000 | | 35,000 |

- Statement of financial position: **current liabilities** £50,000 (repayable within 12 months), **non-current liabilities** £150,000, and accrued interest £15,000 within trade and other payables.

**(b) Tax.** Tax payable at the start of the year was **£42,000**. The company paid **£40,000** in final settlement of that tax. It estimates **£50,000** tax on this year's profit.

- Over-provision = 42,000 − 40,000 = £2,000. Tax expense = 50,000 − 2,000 = **£48,000**. Tax payable in the statement of financial position = **£50,000**.
- Check with the account: debit side 40,000 + 50,000 = 90,000. Credit side 42,000 + 48,000 = 90,000.

**(c) Provision.** At 31 December 20X5, lawyers say a customer is **75% likely** to win a claim and expect to pay **£60,000**. A present obligation exists and payment is probable, so: debit Expense £60,000, credit Provision £60,000. In March 20X6 the claim is settled for **£52,000**: debit Provision £52,000, credit Cash £52,000. The remaining **£8,000** is released: debit Provision £8,000, credit Expense £8,000. (If it had cost £65,000, the extra **£5,000** would be a further expense.) If the lawyers had said only **25%** likely, there would be **no provision**, only a note.

## 11.5 Exam traps

1. **Using the issue price or market value as share capital.** Share capital is always **number of shares × nominal value**. The excess over nominal value goes to share premium. The market price never appears in the books.
2. **Assuming every share is £1.** If the share capital is £200,000 and the shares are 50p, there are **400,000** shares, not 200,000. Dividends per share and bonus issues use the **number of shares**.
3. **Treating dividends as an expense, or deducting a proposed dividend.** Dividends reduce **retained earnings**, not profit. A final dividend proposed **after** the year-end is not a liability at the year-end. Under IFRS a dividend is recorded when it is **declared** (a final dividend: when the shareholders approve it). In the workbook's examples a dividend is declared and **paid** in the same year, so deduct it in that year.
4. **Bonus issue confusion.** A bonus issue brings **no cash** and leaves total equity **unchanged**. Use share premium **first**, and only then retained earnings. A rights issue is the one that brings cash.
5. **Redeemable and irredeemable preference shares.** Redeemable preference shares are **liabilities** and their dividend is a **finance cost**. Irredeemable preference shares are **equity** and their dividend comes out of **retained earnings**.
6. **Tax: wrong direction on over- and under-provision.** Over-provision (paid less than estimated) **reduces** this year's tax expense. Under-provision **increases** it. Do not confuse the tax **expense** (profit or loss) with the tax **payable** (this year's estimate in the statement of financial position).
7. **Provisions: full amount, and only if probable.** If the claim is more than 50% likely, provide for the **whole** estimate (not 70% of it). If it is only possible, make **no** provision. Do not call a provision an accrual: a provision is uncertain.
8. **Interest and revenue timing.** Loan interest is a **finance cost** (not an administrative expense) and unpaid interest must be accrued. Revenue is recognised when **control passes** (usually delivery), not when the order is accepted or cash is received.

## 11.8 Answers and explanations

### Answers: extra practice questions

**Q1. Answer: C (£56,000)**

- **Why:** Each share is sold for £1.20 but its nominal value is only 50p. The difference is the premium.
- **Formula:** Premium per share = 1.20 − 0.50 = £0.70. Share premium = 80,000 × 0.70 = **£56,000**.
- **Easy example:** A ticket with "50p" printed on it is sold for £1.20. The extra 70p on each ticket is the premium.
- **Why not the others:** A (£16,000) treats the shares as £1 shares (80,000 × 0.20). B (£40,000) is the **share capital** (80,000 × 50p). D (£96,000) is the **total cash** received.

**Q2. Answer: C and D**

- **Why:** Loan notes are borrowing, so they are liabilities. **Redeemable** preference shares must or may be bought back, so they are treated like debt.
- **Easy example:** Redeemable preference shares are like a loan from a friend who gets a fixed "interest" and can ask for the money back. Ordinary and irredeemable shares are money that stays in the business for good.
- **Why not the others:** A (irredeemable preference shares) are part of **equity**. B (share premium) is a **reserve**, part of equity.

**Q3. Answer: D (Share capital £125,000; Share premium £80,000)**

- **Why:** Work out the rights issue first, then the bonus issue on the new total number of shares. The bonus issue is charged to share premium.
- **Formula:**
    - Rights issue: 300,000 × 1/3 = 100,000 new shares. Capital = 100,000 × 25p = £25,000. Premium = 100,000 × (85p − 25p) = £60,000.
    - After the rights issue: share capital £100,000 (400,000 shares); share premium 45,000 + 60,000 = £105,000.
    - Bonus issue: 400,000 × 1/4 = 100,000 shares × 25p = £25,000. Share capital = 100,000 + 25,000 = **£125,000**. Share premium = 105,000 − 25,000 = **£80,000**.
- **Easy example:** First the club sells extra membership cards to members (cash comes in). Then it gives each member free extra cards, paid for from the club's own reserve fund.
- **Why not the others:** A forgets to take the bonus issue out of share premium. B applies the bonus issue to only the original 300,000 shares. C treats the share numbers as pounds (100,000 + 100,000, and 105,000 − 100,000).

**Q4. Answer: B (Finance cost £6,000; deducted from retained earnings £35,000)**

- **Why:** The preference shares are **redeemable**, so they are a liability and their dividend is a finance cost. Only the equity dividend is taken from retained earnings.
- **Formula:** Equity dividend = 500,000 × 7p = **£35,000**. Preference dividend = 100,000 × 6% = **£6,000** (finance cost).
- **Easy example:** The redeemable preference payment is like interest on a loan. The equity dividend is like sharing out profit among the owners.
- **Why not the others:** A treats the preference shares as equity (as if irredeemable): £35,000 + £6,000 = £41,000 from retained earnings. C puts every dividend into finance costs. D ignores the preference dividend.

**Q5. Answer: A (£58,000)**

- **Why:** Finance cost is the interest **for the period**, whether or not it has been paid. The new notes were in issue for 9 months.
- **Formula:** Old notes: 500,000 × 8% = £40,000. New notes: 300,000 × 8% × 9/12 = £18,000. Total = **£58,000**.
- **Easy example:** You borrow for 9 months, so you owe 9 months of interest, even if you have only paid 6 months so far.
- **Why not the others:** B (£52,000) is the cash paid (the new notes' interest only for April to September, £12,000), so it leaves out the £6,000 accrued for October to December. C (£40,000) ignores the new notes. D (£64,000) charges a full year on the new notes.

**Q6. Answer: C (£18,500)**

- **Why:** The company paid £2,500 less than it had estimated for last year (an **over-provision**). That £2,500 reduces this year's expense.
- **Formula:** Over-provision = 18,000 − 15,500 = £2,500. Expense = 21,000 − 2,500 = **£18,500**.
- **Easy example:** You set aside £18,000 for a bill, but it was only £15,500. The £2,500 left over is a gain this year.
- **Why not the others:** A (£21,000) ignores the over-provision. B (£23,500) adds it instead of subtracting it. D (£15,500) is just the cash paid.

**Q7. Answer: A and D**

- **Why:** A provision needs a **present obligation** from a past event and a **probable** payment (more than 50% likely) that can be estimated. A and D meet both tests.
- **Easy example:** A: you have probably lost a court case, so you set money aside now. D: you have already sold the goods, so you owe repairs.
- **Why not the others:** B is only a **plan** for the future, so there is no obligation yet. C is only a 25% chance, which is not probable, so it is just a note (a contingent liability).

**Q8. Answer: C (£35,000)**

- **Why:** The bonus issue also reduces retained earnings, so it must be taken out before finding the dividend.
- **Formula:** 340,000 + 120,000 − 30,000 − Dividends = 395,000. Dividends = **£35,000**.
- **Easy example:** Start with the pot of kept profit and add this year's profit. Two things came out: the bonus issue and the dividend. Only the dividend is missing.
- **Why not the others:** A (£65,000) forgets the bonus issue. B (£95,000) adds the bonus issue instead of taking it out. D (£55,000) is only the increase in retained earnings (395,000 − 340,000).

### Answers: true or false

| # | Answer | Reason and easy example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **True** | The company is a separate legal person and has **limited liability**. Like joining a savings group: you can lose the money you put in, but the group's creditors cannot take your own house. |
| 2 | **False** | Share capital is always at **nominal value** (£1). The market price is a deal between two shareholders. The company is not part of it, so it is not recorded. |
| 3 | **False** | Dividends are a **share-out of profit** to owners. They reduce retained earnings and are not a cost of earning profit. |
| 4 | **True** | Redeemable preference shares are liabilities, so their dividend works like interest: Debit **Finance costs**. |
| 5 | **False** | A bonus issue gives **free** shares and brings **no cash**. It only moves amounts between equity items, so total equity does not change. |
| 6 | **True** | An over-provision means too much was set aside last year. The difference is taken off this year's tax expense. |
| 7 | **True** | Revenue is recognised when **control passes** to the customer, normally on delivery. An accepted order is only a promise. |
| 8 | **False** | A provision needs payment to be **probable** (more than 50% likely). At 30% the claim is only disclosed in a note. |
