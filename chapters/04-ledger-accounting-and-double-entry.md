# Chapter 4: Ledger accounting and double entry

## 4.1 Start here: the idea in plain English

Meet Sumon, who runs a tea stall. Every day he buys sugar and tea leaves, sells cups of tea, pays rent, and lets the office next door pay at the end of the week. If he wrote everything in one long list, he could never quickly answer "How much do I owe Rahat Traders?" So he keeps a notebook with **one page for each subject**: a page for cash in the bank, a page for sales, a page for rent, a page for what the office owes him, and so on. In accounting, each page is a **ledger account**, and the whole notebook is a **ledger** ("ledger" is just an old word for "book").

The second idea is **double entry**. Every transaction has **two sides**. If you buy a rickshaw for £1,000 cash, you gain a rickshaw but lose £1,000 of cash. If you borrow £1,000 to buy it, you gain a rickshaw but now owe £1,000. Nothing appears from nowhere. Accounting writes down **both sides** every time: one side as a **debit**, the other as a **credit**. So the total of all debits always equals the total of all credits.

**Debit** and **credit** are only labels for **left** and **right**. An account is drawn like the letter T: the left side is the debit side, the right side is the credit side. The words do not mean "good" or "bad", and they do not always mean "increase" or "decrease". It depends on the type of account:

| Type of account | A debit (left) | A credit (right) | Normal balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Asset (cash at bank, trade receivables, equipment) | **increases** | decreases | debit |
| Expense (rent, wages, purchases) | **increases** | decreases | debit |
| Drawings (owner takes money out) | **increases** | decreases | debit |
| Liability (trade payables, loan) | decreases | **increases** | credit |
| Capital (owner's money in the business) | decreases | **increases** | credit |
| Income (sales) | decreases | **increases** | credit |

> **REMEMBER** Debit = left, credit = right. **DEA** accounts (**D**rawings, **E**xpenses, **A**ssets) go **up** with a debit. **CLI** accounts (**C**apital, **L**iabilities, **I**ncome) go **up** with a credit. A decrease goes on the opposite side.

The last idea is about customers and suppliers. If Sumon sells to ten offices on credit, he needs a separate page for **each** office to know who owes what. These pages are **personal accounts**. They are kept in the **receivables ledger** (customers) and the **payables ledger** (suppliers). They are **memorandum records**: useful lists, but **not part of the double entry**. In the main ledger, called the **nominal ledger**, there is just one total account, **trade receivables**, and one total account, **trade payables**. Those two total accounts are what the double entry uses.

| Word | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Nominal ledger** | The main ledger: one account for each asset, liability, capital, income and expense |
| **Receivables ledger / payables ledger** | Lists of individual customer accounts / supplier accounts (memorandum, not double entry) |
| **Balance c/d, b/d** | The figure "carried down" at the end of a period and "brought down" to start the next |
| **Journal entry** | A standard way of writing down the debit(s) and credit(s) for a transaction |
| **Trade discount** | A reduction off the list price because of the type of deal (bulk, regular customer) |
| **Early settlement discount** | A reduction for paying quickly |
| **VAT (value added tax)** | A tax on sales that the business collects for the government; it is **not** the business's income or expense |

## 4.2 Key facts to remember

- **Ledger** means book. The **nominal ledger** is the main record. It holds one account for each asset, liability, capital, income and expense. Entries are dated, in time order, and built up into totals.
- Each ledger account has a name on top, a **left (debit) side** and a **right (credit) side**. Each line shows the date, the amount and the name of the **other account** in the double entry.
- **Every transaction** is recorded as at least one debit and one credit of the **same total**. This is the **dual effect** (duality) concept.
- **Debit entries:** increase assets, increase expenses (and drawings); decrease liabilities, capital and income.
- **Credit entries:** increase liabilities, capital and income; decrease assets and expenses.
- **Standard entries** (the table in section 4.3 lists the common ones):
    - Cash **received**: debit Cash at bank. Cash **paid**: credit Cash at bank.
    - No cash moves on a credit transaction, so **nothing goes through Cash at bank** until the customer or the business pays.
    - **Owner puts money in:** debit Cash at bank, credit Capital. **Owner takes money out (drawings):** debit Drawings, credit Cash at bank.
    - **Bank loan received:** debit Cash at bank, credit Loan. **Loan interest paid:** debit Loan interest (expense), credit Cash at bank.
    - **Credit note** (goods returned): it reverses part of the original entry. Customer returns goods: debit Sales (or Sales returns), credit Trade receivables. Business returns goods to a supplier: debit Trade payables, credit Purchases. If VAT was charged, the VAT is reversed too.
- **Balancing off:** add both sides. The larger total goes on both sides. The gap is written on the **smaller side** as **balance c/d** (carried down) and on the opposite side below the totals as **balance b/d** (brought down).
    - Assets and expenses normally have **debit** balances. Liabilities, capital and income normally have **credit** balances.
    - A **credit** balance on the Cash at bank account is a **bank overdraft** (a liability).
    - A **debit** balance on a **customer's** account means the customer **owes us**. A **credit** balance on a **supplier's** account means **we owe** the supplier.
- **Journal entry:** shows the date, the account to **debit**, the account to **credit**, the amounts and a short **narrative** (explanation). Total debits must equal total credits. A computerised system refuses an unbalanced journal. The name of the source document is **not** required.
- **Trial balance:** a list of all ledger balances in a debit column and a credit column. If the double entry is arithmetically right, the two totals are equal. It is **extracted from** the double entry; it is **not part of** it.
    - It **proves** only that total debits equal total credits.
    - It does **not** prove the ledgers are correct. An omitted transaction, a debit posted to the wrong account of a similar type, or two errors that cancel each other all leave it balanced. (Chapter 5 covers the trial balance and Chapter 6 covers errors.)
- **Receivables and payables ledgers** are **memorandum** accounts. They let the business answer customer queries, send monthly statements, check credit limits and agree supplier balances. The total of the individual accounts must equal the **trade receivables** (or **trade payables**) account in the nominal ledger. This total account is often called a **control account**.
- **Trade discount:** deducted from the list price. Record the sale or purchase **net** of it. Deduct it **before** working out any early settlement discount.
- **Early settlement discount:** the workbook method depends on what the business **expects**.
    - Discount **expected** to be taken: record the sale (or purchase) **net of the discount**. If the discount is then **not** taken, adjust: the extra cash goes to **Revenue** (or **Purchases**).
    - Discount **not expected**: record the **full** amount. If it is then taken, reduce Revenue (or Purchases) by the discount.
    - The business does **not** show the discount as a separate expense or income. It adjusts revenue or purchases.
- **VAT:**
    - **Output VAT** on sales: **credit** the VAT account. **Input VAT** on purchases and expenses: **debit** the VAT account.
    - Sales and expenses in the statement of profit or loss are **net of VAT**. Trade receivables and trade payables are **gross** (include VAT).
    - VAT is recorded when the **invoice** is raised or received, **not** when the cash moves.
    - The VAT account balance is a **credit** if more is owed to the tax authority (**other payables**) and a **debit** if the authority owes the business (**other receivables**). The business pays or recovers the **net** amount.
    - **Irrecoverable VAT** (for example on cars, client entertaining, or when the trader is not VAT-registered) cannot be claimed back, so it becomes **part of the cost** of the expense or asset.
    - Use the VAT rate the question gives (or the standard rate). This chapter uses **15%**, the standard rate in the workbook's worked answers.

## 4.3 Formulas and layouts

> **FORMULA** Accounting equation: Assets = Capital + Liabilities (it stays true after every correctly recorded transaction)

> **FORMULA** Double entry: total debits = total credits, for every transaction and for the whole ledger

> **FORMULA** Closing balance of an account = total of the bigger side − total of the smaller side

> **FORMULA** Net price after trade discount = List price × (100% − trade discount %)

> **FORMULA** Early settlement discount = Invoice amount after trade discount × discount %

> **FORMULA** Amount recorded when the discount is expected to be taken = Net invoice × (100% − discount %)

> **FORMULA** VAT = Net amount × VAT rate; Gross amount = Net amount × (100% + VAT rate)

> **FORMULA** VAT in a gross amount = Gross amount × rate ÷ (100% + rate); at 15% this is Gross × 15 ÷ 115 (which is Gross × 3 ÷ 23)

> **FORMULA** VAT payable = Output VAT (credits) − Input VAT (debits), after the opening balance and any payments to the tax authority

> **FORMULA** Cash received from credit customers = Opening receivables + Credit sales (incl. VAT) − Credit notes (incl. VAT) − Closing receivables

> **FORMULA** Cash paid to credit suppliers = Opening payables + Credit purchases (incl. VAT) − Returns (incl. VAT) − Closing payables

**The T-account layout** (this is the layout used throughout the exam):

| Name of account: debit side (left) | £ | Credit side (right) | £ |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Date and name of the other account | amount | Date and name of the other account | amount |
| | **total** | | **total** (same figure) |

**Quick table of common entries.** Learn this table; most exam questions are one line from it.

| Transaction | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Owner pays money in | Cash at bank | Capital |
| Owner takes money out | Drawings | Cash at bank |
| Cash sale | Cash at bank | Sales |
| Credit sale (no VAT) | Trade receivables | Sales |
| Customer pays | Cash at bank | Trade receivables |
| Cash purchase of goods | Purchases | Cash at bank |
| Credit purchase of goods (no VAT) | Purchases | Trade payables |

| Expense paid (rent, wages) | The expense | Cash at bank |
| Expense bill received, not paid | The expense | Trade payables |
| Non-current asset bought | The asset | Cash at bank (or Trade payables if on credit) |
| Bank loan received | Cash at bank | Loan |
| Loan interest paid | Loan interest (expense) | Cash at bank |
| Goods returned by a customer (credit note issued) | Sales (or Sales returns) | Trade receivables |
| Goods returned to a supplier (credit note received) | Trade payables | Purchases |
| Credit sale with VAT | Trade receivables (gross) | Sales (net) **and** VAT (output) |
| Credit purchase with VAT | Purchases (net) **and** VAT (input) | Trade payables (gross) |
| Pay VAT to the tax authority | VAT | Cash at bank |

**The journal layout:**

| Date | Account | Debit £ | Credit £ |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| 3 March | Account to be debited | amount | |
| | Account to be credited | | same amount |
| | *Narrative: why the entry is made* | | |

**The trial balance layout:**

| Account | Debit £ | Credit £ |
|---|---:|---:|
| Assets and expenses (debit balances) | listed here | |
| Liabilities, capital and income (credit balances) | | listed here |
| **Totals** | **must be equal** | **must be equal** |

**The VAT account layout:**

| VAT account: debit side | £ | Credit side | £ |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Input VAT on purchases and expenses | amount | Output VAT on sales | amount |
| Output VAT cancelled by credit notes issued | amount | Input VAT cancelled by credit notes received | amount |
| Cash paid to the tax authority | amount | Opening balance if owed to the authority | amount |

> **REMEMBER** In the trade receivables account, credit sales and the opening balance are on the **debit** side; cash received and credit notes are on the **credit** side. In the trade payables account it is the **opposite**: purchases and the opening balance are on the **credit** side; payments and returns are on the **debit** side.

## 4.4 Worked examples

### Example 1: Debit or credit? Eight transactions of a tea stall

Sumon opens his tea stall on 1 March 20X1. For each transaction, find the two accounts and decide which is debited.

| # | Transaction | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sumon pays £3,000 of his own money into a business bank account | Cash at bank (asset up) | Capital (capital up) |
| 2 | Buys a stove and kettle for £400, paid from the bank | Equipment (asset up) | Cash at bank (asset down) |
| 3 | Buys tea leaves and sugar for £250 on credit from Rahat Traders | Purchases (expense up) | Trade payables (liability up) |
| 4 | Sells tea for £180 cash, banked | Cash at bank (asset up) | Sales (income up) |
| 5 | Sells tea on credit to the office next door, £120 | Trade receivables (asset up) | Sales (income up) |
| 6 | Pays Rahat Traders £100 from the bank | Trade payables (liability down) | Cash at bank (asset down) |
| 7 | The office pays its £120 into the bank | Cash at bank (asset up) | Trade receivables (asset down) |
| 8 | Takes £50 from the bank for his own use | Drawings (up) | Cash at bank (asset down) |

The journal for transaction 2 is:

| Date | Account | Debit £ | Credit £ |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| 3 March 20X1 | Equipment | 400 | |
| | Cash at bank | | 400 |
| | *Narrative: purchase of stove and kettle, paid from the bank* | | |

*Easy way to see it:* a pocket rule is "**debit the account that receives value, credit the account that gives value**". In 2, the equipment receives value (debit) and the bank gives it (credit). In 6, the supplier receives cash (debit the supplier's account, trade payables).

### Example 3: Trade and early settlement discounts

Bright Stationers sells 200 notebooks at a list price of **£10** each to Karim Stores. It gives a **10% trade discount**, and a **2% early settlement discount** if Karim pays within 7 days. Bright **expects** Karim to take the discount.

1. List price: 200 × 10 = £2,000.
2. After trade discount: 2,000 × 90% = **£1,800**.
3. Early settlement discount: 1,800 × 2% = **£36**.
4. Amount recorded when the discount is expected: 1,800 − 36 = **£1,764**.

At the invoice date: **Dr Trade receivables £1,764, Cr Revenue £1,764.**

| What happens | Entry |
|---|---|
| Karim pays £1,764 within 7 days (as expected) | Dr Cash at bank 1,764; Cr Trade receivables 1,764 |
| Karim pays **£1,800** after 7 days (discount lost) | Dr Cash at bank 1,800; Cr Trade receivables 1,764; **Cr Revenue 36** |
| If Bright had **not expected** the discount: invoice recorded at £1,800. Karim then pays £1,764 early | Dr Cash at bank 1,764; **Dr Revenue 36**; Cr Trade receivables 1,800 |

Karim's books mirror this. If Karim expects to take the discount, it records **Dr Purchases 1,764, Cr Trade payables 1,764**. If Karim then pays late it pays £1,800: **Dr Trade payables 1,764, Dr Purchases 36, Cr Cash at bank 1,800.**

*Easy way to see it:* the invoice is always recorded at the amount you **expect** to receive or pay. If real life turns out differently, the difference is corrected in revenue (or purchases) when the cash moves.

### Example 4: VAT (rate 15%)

**(a) A VAT-registered shop buys and sells.** It buys goods on credit for £1,200 + VAT, and sells goods on credit for £2,000 + VAT.

| | Net £ | VAT £ | Gross £ |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Credit purchase (input VAT) | 1,200 | 180 | 1,380 |
| Credit sale (output VAT) | 2,000 | 300 | 2,300 |

- Purchase: Dr Purchases 1,200; Dr VAT 180; Cr Trade payables 1,380.
- Sale: Dr Trade receivables 2,300; Cr Sales 2,000; Cr VAT 300.

| VAT account: debit side | £ | Credit side | £ |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Input VAT on purchase | 180 | Output VAT on sale | 300 |
| **Balance c/d** | **120** | | |
| | **300** | | **300** |
| | | **Balance b/d** | **120** |

The shop owes the tax authority **£120**, shown in **other payables**. Paying it: Dr VAT 120, Cr Cash at bank 120. Notice that only **net** figures (purchases £1,200, sales £2,000) reach the statement of profit or loss. The VAT never touches profit.

**(b) Finding the VAT in a gross figure.** An invoice total is **£5,750 including VAT at 15%**. VAT = 5,750 × 15 ÷ 115 = **£750**. Net = 5,750 − 750 = **£5,000**. (Check: 5,000 × 15% = 750.)

**(c) Irrecoverable VAT.** A VAT-registered business buys a car for £20,000 + VAT £3,000. VAT on cars cannot be reclaimed, so the VAT is part of the cost: **Dr Motor vehicles £23,000, Cr Cash at bank £23,000.** No debit goes to the VAT account.

### Example 5: Trade receivables and payables control accounts with a missing figure

Gulshan Traders has these figures for the year. All VAT is at 15% and the figures below are **including VAT**.

- Trade receivables at the start: £18,400; at the end: £21,500. Credit sales: £74,750. Credit notes issued to customers: £1,150. **Cash received from customers: not known.**
- Trade payables at the start: £12,600; at the end: £14,950. Credit purchases: £46,000. Goods returned to suppliers: £575. **Payments to suppliers: not known.**

| Trade receivables: debit side | £ | Credit side | £ |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Balance b/d | 18,400 | Credit notes issued | 1,150 |
| Credit sales | 74,750 | **Cash received (balancing figure)** | **70,500** |
| | | Balance c/d | 21,500 |
| | **93,150** | | **93,150** |

| Trade payables: debit side | £ | Credit side | £ |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Goods returned to suppliers | 575 | Balance b/d | 12,600 |
| **Payments to suppliers (balancing figure)** | **43,075** | Credit purchases | 46,000 |
| Balance c/d | 14,950 | | |
| | **58,600** | | **58,600** |

Check receivables: 18,400 + 74,750 = 93,150, and 93,150 − 1,150 − 21,500 = **70,500**. Check payables: 12,600 + 46,000 = 58,600, and 58,600 − 575 − 14,950 = **43,075**.

The receivables ledger holds Customer A £9,800, Customer B £7,450 and Customer C £4,250. The total is 9,800 + 7,450 + 4,250 = **£21,500**, the same as the control account.

| Extract: statement of financial position at the year-end | £ |
|---|---:|
| Current assets: trade receivables | 21,500 |
| Current liabilities: trade payables | 14,950 |

*Easy way to see it:* a control account is a tank with a tap in and a tap out. What was in at the start, plus what flowed in, minus what flowed out, equals what is left. When one flow is unknown, it is the balancing figure.

## 4.5 Exam traps

1. **"Debit means increase."** Not always. A debit **decreases** a liability, capital or income. A debit to Trade payables means we have paid or returned goods, so we owe **less**.
2. **Drawings.** Money the owner takes for personal use is **debit Drawings, credit Cash at bank**. It is **not** an expense and does not reduce profit. It reduces the owner's capital. Do not confuse it with capital **introduced** (debit Cash at bank, credit Capital).
3. **Cash or credit?** A credit sale or purchase **never touches Cash at bank**. The bank is used later when the customer or the business pays. When the customer pays, do **not** credit Sales again: credit Trade receivables.
4. **Gross or net?** A VAT-registered business records Sales and Purchases **net of VAT** and puts the VAT in the VAT account. Trade receivables and payables are **gross**. Sales of £1,000 + VAT of £150 means the customer owes **£1,150**.
5. **VAT account direction and timing.** Output VAT is a **credit** and input VAT is a **debit**. A **credit** balance means we **owe** the tax authority. VAT is recorded at the **invoice date**, not when the customer pays. Irrecoverable VAT is **not** debited to the VAT account: it is added to the cost.
6. **Discounts.** Deduct the **trade** discount **first**, then work out the early settlement discount on the reduced figure. A customer who was **expected** to take the discount but pays late pays the **full** amount: the extra goes to **Revenue**, not to a discount account. Do not mix up "list price" with "invoice price".
7. **Who owes whom.** In **our** ledger, a **debit** balance on a **customer's** account means **they owe us**. A **credit** balance on a **supplier's** account means **we owe them**. Read the account's owner carefully.
8. **"The trial balance balances, so everything is right."** False. It only proves debits equal credits. Missing transactions, wrong accounts and cancelling errors do not show up. Also remember that the receivables and payables ledgers are **memorandum** records, so a sale is **not** double-entered again in the customer's personal account.

### Q10. Scenario: control accounts, VAT and the statement of financial position (14 marks)

**Mizan Wholesale** is a sole trader with a year-end of **31 December 20X4**. VAT is charged at **15%** on all sales and purchases. The following information is for the year.

*Receivables.* Trade receivables at 1 January 20X4: **£42,300**. Credit sales (excluding VAT): **£310,000**. Credit notes issued to credit customers (excluding VAT): **£6,000**. Cash received from credit customers: **£344,100**. Cash sales (excluding VAT, banked): **£40,000**.

*Payables.* Trade payables at 1 January 20X4: **£28,400**. Credit purchases (excluding VAT): **£205,000**. Goods returned to suppliers, credit notes received (excluding VAT): **£4,000**. Trade payables at 31 December 20X4 per the list of supplier balances (this agrees with the payables control account): **£31,200**. The payments made to suppliers are not known.

*VAT.* The VAT account had a **credit** balance of **£9,600** at 1 January 20X4 (owed to the tax authority). Expenses paid in cash were **£48,000 excluding VAT**, with VAT charged of £7,200. The £48,000 includes **£2,000** of client entertainment, and the **£300** VAT on it is **irrecoverable**. All the other VAT on expenses is recoverable. VAT paid to the tax authority during the year: **£22,000**.

At 31 December 20X4 the total of the individual balances in the receivables ledger is **£47,300**. Investigation shows that a sales invoice to customer K for **£500 (including VAT)** was entered in the sales day book (so it is in the control account totals) but was **never posted to K's personal account**.

**Required**

1. Prepare the trade receivables control account (using figures including VAT) and find the closing balance. (3 marks)
2. Reconcile the total of the receivables ledger (£47,300) to the control account, and state the trade receivables figure for the statement of financial position. (2 marks)
3. Prepare the trade payables control account (using figures including VAT) and calculate the payments made to suppliers in the year. (3 marks)
4. Prepare the VAT account for the year. State the closing balance and where it is shown in the statement of financial position. State where the £300 irrecoverable VAT is shown. (4 marks)
5. Show the extracts from the statement of financial position at 31 December 20X4 for trade receivables, trade payables and VAT. (2 marks)

## 4.8 Answers and explanations

### Answers: extra practice questions

**Q1. Answer: C (Debit Drawings £600; credit Cash at bank £600)**

- **Why:** Money the owner takes for personal use is **drawings**. Drawings go up (debit) and the bank balance goes down (credit).
- **Easy example:** Sumon takes £50 from the till for his own lunch. The stall's cash falls and his "taken out" record rises. It is not a cost of making tea.
- **Why not the others:** A is the opposite: it would increase the bank. B treats personal spending as a business expense, which would wrongly reduce profit. D records capital paid **in**, not taken out.

**Q2. Answer: B and D**

- **Why:** A credit to Cash at bank means cash is paid out. Paying a supplier (B) and paying rent (D) both reduce the bank balance.
- **Easy example:** Whenever money leaves your account, the bank account is credited.
- **Why not the others:** A (owner pays money in) and C (customer pays) bring cash **in**, so Cash at bank is **debited**.

**Q3. Answer: A (£768)**

- **Why:** The trade discount comes first. Naz **expects** to take the early settlement discount, so the payable is recorded net of it.
- **Formula:** List 40 × 25 = 1,000. After trade discount: 1,000 × 80% = 800. Early settlement discount: 800 × 4% = 32. Payable = 800 − 32 = **£768**.
- **Easy example:** A shop quotes £1,000, you get 20% off, and then you also pay quickly for another 4% off the new price.
- **Why not the others:** B (£760) takes 24% (20% + 4%) off the original £1,000, but the 4% must be taken off the reduced price. C (£800) ignores the early settlement discount although it is expected. D (£960) takes only the 4% off the list price and ignores the trade discount.

**Q4. Answer: D**

- **Why:** The receivable is £1,940, but Mina has paid £2,000 because the discount was lost. Cash goes up by £2,000. Receivables are cleared by £1,940. The extra £60 is more revenue.
- **Formula:** Debit Cash at bank 2,000 = Credit Trade receivables 1,940 + Credit Revenue 60. The discount was 2,000 × 3% = £60.
- **Easy example:** You priced a book at £97 for a quick payer, but the customer pays late and gives the full £100. The extra £3 is earned income.
- **Why not the others:** A shows £1,940 received, but £2,000 was paid, so the bank would not agree. B credits receivables with £2,000 although only £1,940 was recorded, so the account is left with a £60 credit balance and revenue stays £60 too low. C debits Revenue, which is the entry for a discount **taken**, and credits receivables with £2,060, more than was invoiced.

**Q5. Answer: B (£4,000)**

- **Why:** The £4,600 includes VAT. The business can reclaim the VAT, so it is **not** part of the asset's cost. Only the net price is debited to the asset. The VAT goes to the VAT account.
- **Formula:** VAT = 4,600 × 15 ÷ 115 = £600. Net = 4,600 − 600 = **£4,000**. (Check: 4,000 × 1.15 = 4,600.) Entry: Dr Computer equipment 4,000; Dr VAT 600; Cr Cash at bank 4,600.
- **Easy example:** You collect VAT for the government. VAT you pay on your own purchases is "lent" and comes back, so it is not part of your cost.
- **Why not the others:** A (£3,910) takes 15% of the **gross** figure (4,600 × 15% = 690) instead of the 15/115 share. C (£4,600) debits the gross amount and ignores the VAT. D (£5,290) adds 15% on top of a figure that already includes VAT.

**Q6. Answer: A and C**

- **Why:** A is the rule for output VAT: credit the VAT account at the invoice date. C: a credit balance means the business owes the tax authority, which is a liability in other payables.
- **Easy example:** A VAT-registered shop is a tax collector. The tax it has collected but not yet handed over is money it owes.
- **Why not the others:** B is wrong because input VAT is debited to the **VAT account** (it is recoverable), not to Purchases. D is wrong because VAT is not the trader's income, so revenue is shown **net of VAT**.

**Q7. Answer: C (£175,200)**

- **Why:** Cash sales never go through trade receivables, so only credit sales are included. Build the receivables account and the cash received is the balancing figure.
- **Formula:** Credit sales = 210,000 − 26,000 = 184,000. Cash received = 32,600 + 184,000 − 2,500 − 38,900 = **£175,200**.

| Trade receivables: debit side | £ | Credit side | £ |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Balance b/d | 32,600 | Credit notes | 2,500 |
| Credit sales | 184,000 | Cash received (balancing figure) | 175,200 |
| | | Balance c/d | 38,900 |
| | **216,600** | | **216,600** |

- **Easy example:** Customers owed you £32,600, you added £184,000 of new sales, you cancelled £2,500, and £38,900 is still owed. The rest, £175,200, must have been paid.
- **Why not the others:** A (£177,700) ignores the credit notes. B (£180,200) adds the credit notes instead of subtracting them. D (£187,800) swaps the opening and closing balances.

**Q8. Answer: D (Hosne owes Sarker £1,500)**

- **Why:** Sarker is Hosne's **supplier**, so a **credit** balance means Hosne owes Sarker. Purchases increase what Hosne owes (credit). Payments and goods returned reduce it (debit).
- **Formula:** 5,000 − 3,200 − 300 = **£1,500** credit balance.
- **Easy example:** You owe a shop £5,000. You pay £3,200 and return goods worth £300. You still owe £1,500.
- **Why not the others:** A (£1,800) ignores the credit note. B has the wrong direction: this is a supplier account, so it is Hosne that owes. C (£2,100) adds the credit note instead of deducting it.

| Goods returned (incl. VAT) | 4,600 | Balance b/d | 28,400 |

**Requirement 4: VAT account**

Output VAT on credit sales: 310,000 × 15% = 46,500. On cash sales: 40,000 × 15% = 6,000. Credit notes issued: 6,000 × 15% = 900. Input VAT on credit purchases: 205,000 × 15% = 30,750. Returns to suppliers: 4,000 × 15% = 600. Input VAT on expenses: 7,200 less irrecoverable 300 = **6,900**.

| Input VAT: expenses (7,200 − 300 irrecoverable) | 6,900 | Output VAT: credit sales | 46,500 |

The closing balance is a **credit of £2,150**. It is owed to the tax authority, so it is a **liability** in **other payables**. The **£300** irrecoverable VAT is **part of the cost** of the entertainment expense, so the expense in the statement of profit or loss is £2,000 + £300 = **£2,300**. It does not go to the VAT account.

| | £ |
|---|---:|

*Marking tips:* the traps are (i) the question gives **net** figures but control accounts use **gross** figures, (ii) **cash sales** do not belong in the receivables account, (iii) credit notes cancel both the receivable **and** the VAT, (iv) the irrecoverable VAT is **not** debited to the VAT account, and (v) the reconciliation: the personal account list is corrected, not the control account.

*Checks:* receivables rose by 47,800 − 42,300 = 5,500, which equals credit sales less credit notes less cash received: 356,500 − 6,900 − 344,100 = 5,500. Payables rose by 31,200 − 28,400 = 2,800, which equals purchases less returns less payments: 235,750 − 4,600 − 228,350 = 2,800. The cash paid to suppliers of £228,350 is less than the net purchases of £231,150, so £2,800 more is owed at the year-end.

### Answers: true or false

| # | Answer | Reason and easy example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **False** | A debit increases assets and expenses, but it **decreases** liabilities, capital and income. Paying a supplier is a debit to Trade payables, and the amount owed goes down. |
| 2 | **False** | The trial balance only shows that debits equal credits. A forgotten transaction or a debit posted to the wrong expense account still leaves it balanced. |
| 3 | **True** | A credit balance means more money has been paid out than has come in. The bank is owed money, so it is a liability (overdraft). |
| 4 | **False** | The receivables ledger is a **memorandum** record. The double entry uses the single trade receivables account in the nominal ledger, which equals the total of the personal accounts. |
| 5 | **True** | Take off the trade discount first, then work out the early settlement discount on the reduced price. Example: £1,000 less 10% = £900, and 2% of £900 = £18. |
| 6 | **False** | The VAT belongs to the tax authority. The business collects it. Revenue is shown **net of VAT**, and the VAT goes to the VAT account. |
| 7 | **True** | VAT is recorded when the invoice is raised or received. It is credited to the VAT account at that point, even if the customer pays a month later. |
| 8 | **True** | Drawings are a debit (they reduce capital) and Cash at bank is credited because cash leaves the business. It is not an expense. |
