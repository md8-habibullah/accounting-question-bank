# Chapter 3: Recording financial transactions

## 3.1 Start here: the idea in plain English

Think of Rahim, who runs a small shop. Every time something happens in the shop he gets a piece of proof. The wholesaler sends a **bill**. A customer who returns a damaged shirt gets a **note** saying Rahim owes them money back. The bank sends a **message** when a customer pays by bKash or bank transfer. If Rahim threw all this away, he could never remember who owes him, whom he owes, or how much cash he really has.

Accountants call this proof a **source document**: the document a business uses as the **starting point for recording a transaction**. The accounting records are only as good as these documents. A wrong document in means a wrong report out.

Almost every business today records its transactions in **accounting software** (a *computerised accounting system*), often kept on the internet (*cloud accounting*). Like any system it has **inputs** (source documents and standing data), **processes** (the ledgers and journals are updated) and **outputs** (reports, the trial balance and the financial statements). This chapter is about the inputs: which papers and records feed the system, and how the software and its helpers (such as artificial intelligence) deal with them. It ends with the **payroll**, the record that feeds wages and salaries into the system.

| Word | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Source document** | The document that starts the recording of a transaction (for example a sales invoice) |
| **Invoice** | A bill sent by the seller asking the buyer to pay |
| **Credit note** | A "negative invoice": the seller reduces what the customer owes (returns, overcharge) |
| **Standing data** | Reference data that rarely changes (business name, payment terms, VAT rate, wage rates) |
| **Real-time processing** | Each transaction updates the system the moment it happens |
| **Batch processing** | Transactions are collected and processed together, for example at the end of the day |
| **Cloud accounting** | Using accounting software and data stored on a provider's servers, reached through the internet |
| **Transaction report** | The bank's list of receipts and payments; the source document for the cash at bank account |
| **Exception report** | A list of items the software could not match; the accountant must investigate them |
| **Payroll** | The record of employees' pay, deductions and the employer's extra costs |
| **Gross pay / net pay** | Gross = pay before deductions. Net = the cash the employee actually receives |

> **REMEMBER** For credit sales and credit purchases, only the **invoice** and the **credit note** are source documents. Orders, delivery notes and goods received notes support them, but they are **not** recorded in the accounts.

> **KEY FACT** Gross pay is **not** what the employee receives. The employer first deducts withholding tax and the employee's pension contribution, and pays those amounts to other people. The employee gets the **net pay**.

## 3.2 Key facts to remember

- **Accounting system:** the system that lets a business **record, process and store** financial information, so it can keep records and give relevant, reliable information to stakeholders.
    - Old way: a manual system of books written by hand (this is why we still say "bookkeeper" and "books of the company").
    - Now: nearly all are computerised, from simple off-the-shelf packages for small firms to bespoke (made-to-order) systems for big ones.
    - The software is made of **modules** (for example payroll, balances owed by credit customers, non-current assets, and the main accounting record). Entering a credit sale updates the sales module **and** the customer-balances module automatically.
- **Standing data** is reference data that does not change often (name and address, payment terms, VAT registration number and rates, wage rates). Access to it must be tightly controlled, because many transactions rely on it.
- **Account codes:** every account in a module has a **unique code**, which makes input and processing easier.
- **Real-time processing:** each transaction is entered and the accounts are updated **at the point it happens**. **Batch processing:** a group of transactions is processed together, usually at the end of each day or week. The workbook assumes real-time processing.
- **Controls:** trained and qualified staff, access limited to authorised users, and approval and review of what is input.
- **Cloud accounting** means doing accounting tasks with cloud software, often delivered as **software as a service (SaaS)**. Software and data sit on the provider's servers.
    - Benefits: use from any computer, tablet or phone with internet; no need to buy expensive IT equipment; the provider maintains the software (lower IT support cost); accountants and auditors can see the live data together.
    - Drawbacks: needs a reliable internet connection; the provider's servers may be down; data security risks (hacking, loss); the business relies on the provider for back-up.
    - Cloud accounting does **not** remove the need to input source documents. It only changes where the software runs.
- **Artificial intelligence (AI)** helps with: automatic data entry from digital documents; coding (categorising) expenses, for example always coding the mobile phone company's invoices to "Phone expenses"; matching bank transactions (reconciliations); spotting unusual patterns that may be fraud or error; producing routine reports; chatbots for customer questions.
    - Disadvantages: high set-up cost; poor with unusual transactions that need judgement; only as good as the data it learns from; security risk because it handles confidential data.
    - **Manual checks are still needed.** AI is unlikely to replace bookkeepers completely.
- **Source documents for credit transactions:**
    - **Sales:** sales order (not source), delivery note (not source), **sales invoice** (source), **credit note** (source).
    - **Purchases:** purchase order (not source), goods received note, GRN (not source), **purchase invoice** (source), **credit note received** (source).
    - A purchase invoice is the **same document** as the seller's sales invoice, seen from the buyer's side.
- **Checking a purchase invoice:** before it is recorded, agree it to the **purchase order** (was it ordered, at what price?) and to the **goods received note** (did the goods arrive, in what quantity?). A sales invoice should agree to the delivery note.
- **Invoice contents:** invoice number (numbered in sequence), seller and buyer names and addresses, date, description of goods or services, quantity and unit price, trade discount (if any), any settlement (cash) discount offered, VAT, total amount due, payment date and bank details, sometimes a **remittance advice** slip.
- **Remittance advice:** a slip the customer sends back with the payment, so the seller knows which invoices are being paid. It helps to match payments but is not one of the main source documents.
- **Credit note:** issued by the **seller** to the customer for returned goods or when the customer was overcharged. It is a **source document**.
- **Debit note:** issued by the **buyer** to the supplier as a formal request for a credit note. It is **not** a source document.
- **VAT (value added tax):** a sales tax added to most invoices in Bangladesh. The standard rate used in ICAB Accounting examples is **15%** (use the rate given in the question). VAT charged on **sales** is **output tax** (owed to the VAT Authority). VAT paid on **purchases** is **input tax** (recoverable from the VAT Authority). The business pays the **difference**. Chapter 4 gives the full double entry.
- **Trade discount** is a reduction in the **price** (for example for buying in bulk). It is shown on the invoice and sales or purchases are recorded **net** of it. **Early settlement discount** (also called prompt payment discount or cash discount) is a reduction offered only if the customer **pays early**. Trade discount is deducted **first**. Chapter 4 gives the double entry for settlement discounts.
- **Recording a credit sale:** Debit Trade receivables (gross, including VAT), Credit Revenue (net), Credit VAT (output tax). A **credit note** reverses part of this. Revenue **excludes** VAT, trade receivables **include** it.
- **Recording a credit purchase:** Debit Purchases (net), Debit VAT (input tax), Credit Trade payables (gross). A credit note received reverses part of this.
- **Bank transactions:** the **transaction report** downloaded from electronic banking is the source document for the cash at bank account. Some software connects directly to the bank. Either way, **AI matches** known transactions (a regular supplier paid the exact invoice amount; net wages that agree to the payroll). Items it cannot match go on an **exception report** and are held in a temporary **suspense account** until investigated.
- **Likely to be unmatched:** cash paid into the bank, one-off purchases (for example a new laptop), payments that differ from the invoice (for example a prompt-payment discount was taken), part payments, bank charges.
- **Payment methods** recorded through the cash at bank account: payment card, digital wallet (such as bKash), direct bank transfer, standing order, direct debit, cheque. Bank interest and charges are also recorded there.
- **Cheque:** a written order from an account holder to their bank to pay a sum to a person or organisation. A cheque received is recorded straight away, but it takes several days to **clear** into the bank account.
- **Petty cash:** a small amount of cash kept on the premises for odd, small expenses, recorded separately in a **petty cash book**. The workbook does not study it further, but the ICAEW question bank asks about the **imprest system**: a fixed **float** is topped up, at intervals, by the **exact amount spent**. Each payment is supported by a **voucher**.
- **Payroll:** the source document for wages and salaries. It shows gross pay, **withholding tax (WHT)** (income tax), the employee's pension contribution, net pay, and the **employer's pension contribution**.
    - Net pay = gross pay − WHT − employee's pension contribution.
    - Wages and salaries **cost** (expense) = gross pay + employer's contribution. It is **more** than gross pay.
    - WHT is paid to the **Commissioner of Taxes**. Pension contributions (employee's and employer's) are paid to the **pension plan**.
    - Some questions use UK words (PAYE for income tax, "employee's NI" and "employer's NI" for social-security contributions). Treat them the same way: the employee's amounts are deducted from gross pay, the employer's amount is an **extra cost on top**.
- **Effect on the statements:** a credit sale raises revenue and trade receivables (no cash yet). Payroll cost is an expense that reduces profit. Only the net pay leaves the bank at once. WHT and pension amounts stay as **current liabilities** until paid.

## 3.3 Formulas and layouts

> **FORMULA** Net invoice amount = (Quantity × list price) − trade discount

> **FORMULA** VAT = Net amount × VAT rate

> **FORMULA** Invoice total (gross) = Net amount + VAT. With 15% VAT, gross = net × 1.15

> **FORMULA** Net = Gross ÷ 1.15 (when the VAT rate is 15%)

> **FORMULA** VAT inside a gross figure = Gross × 15 ÷ 115 (when the VAT rate is 15%)

> **FORMULA** Settlement discount = Invoice amount after trade discount × settlement discount %

> **FORMULA** VAT payable to the VAT Authority = Output VAT (sales) − Input VAT (purchases). A negative answer means VAT is **recoverable**

> **FORMULA** Customer balance after a return = Invoice total (including VAT) − Credit note total (including VAT)

> **FORMULA** Net pay = Gross pay − Withholding tax − Employee's pension contribution

> **FORMULA** Gross pay (working back) = Net pay + Withholding tax + Employee's pension contribution

> **FORMULA** Wages and salaries expense (total payroll cost) = Gross pay + Employer's pension contribution

> **FORMULA** Paid to Commissioner of Taxes = Withholding tax deducted from employees

> **FORMULA** Paid to pension plan = Employee's contributions + Employer's contributions

> **FORMULA** Check: Net pay + WHT + Pension plan amounts = Total payroll cost

> **FORMULA** Closing cash at bank = Opening balance + Receipts − Payments

> **FORMULA** Imprest top-up = Vouchers paid out − Cash refunds received back = Float − Cash left in the box

## 3.4 Worked examples

### Example 1: Which documents? (a credit sale and a return)

Farida's Stationery sells **300 notebooks at £2 each** on credit to Greenfield School. It sends a **delivery note** with the goods and later a **sales invoice** for £600. The school finds **40 notebooks torn** and returns them. Farida issues a **credit note**.

1. Sales invoice: 300 × £2 = **£600**. (Source document.)
2. Credit note: 40 × £2 = **£80**. (Source document.)
3. The school now owes £600 − £80 = **£520** (ignoring VAT).
4. The delivery note and the school's earlier order are **not** source documents. They do not create entries.

On the school's side, the same invoice is a **purchase invoice** and the credit note is a **credit note received**. Before recording the invoice, the school's clerk agrees it to the **purchase order** (was it ordered, at what price?) and to the **goods received note** (how many notebooks arrived?).

*Easy way to see it:* the invoice and the credit note are the papers that change how much money is owed. Delivery notes and orders only say "something is on its way".

### Example 2: Invoice arithmetic with trade discount, settlement discount and VAT

Hossain Electricals sells **25 lamps at list price £40** to Rafi Stores. It gives a **12% trade discount**. VAT is **15%** on the net amount.

1. List price: 25 × 40 = 1,000.
2. Trade discount: 12% × 1,000 = 120. Net amount = 1,000 − 120 = **£880**.
3. VAT: 15% × 880 = **£132**.
4. Invoice total: 880 + 132 = **£1,012**.

Hossain bought the lamps from a wholesaler for £500 plus VAT of £75 (input tax).

| VAT | £ |
|---|---:|
| Output VAT on the sale | 132 |
| Input VAT on the purchase | (75) |
| **VAT payable to the VAT Authority** | **57** |

*Settlement discount, separate example (no VAT):* an invoice for £2,400 (already after trade discount) offers **5% for payment within 14 days**. The discount is 5% × 2,400 = £120. A customer who pays on time pays 2,400 − 120 = **£2,280**.

*Easy way to see it:* the trade discount is a "shop price" cut that happens before the invoice is written. The settlement discount is a "thank you for paying early" that happens only if the customer actually pays early.

### Example 3: A payroll, in statement layout

Three employees work for Sultana Foods. WHT and pension contributions for the month are as follows. Employees pay 5% of gross into the pension plan. The employer adds 8% of gross.

| Employee | Gross pay £ | WHT £ | Employee pension £ | **Net pay £** | Employer pension £ | **Total cost £** |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Nusrat | 4,000 | 520 | 200 | 3,280 | 320 | 4,320 |
| Imran | 3,000 | 300 | 150 | 2,550 | 240 | 3,240 |
| Sabina | 2,500 | 225 | 125 | 2,150 | 200 | 2,700 |
| **Total** | **9,500** | **1,045** | **475** | **7,980** | **760** | **10,260** |

1. Net pay for Nusrat = 4,000 − 520 − 200 = 3,280.
2. Total payroll cost = 9,500 + 760 = **£10,260** (this is the wages and salaries expense).
3. Where the money goes: employees 7,980 + Commissioner of Taxes 1,045 + pension plan (475 + 760 = 1,235) = **£10,260**.

| Extract: statement of profit or loss for the month | £ |
|---|---:|
| Wages and salaries expense | 10,260 |

| Extract: statement of financial position, if WHT and pension are unpaid at the month-end | £ |
|---|---:|
| Trade and other payables: withholding tax payable | 1,045 |
| Trade and other payables: pension plan payable | 1,235 |

*Easy way to see it:* the employer is a "collector" for the tax office and the pension plan. It keeps their money for a short time and then hands it over.

### Example 4: The bank transaction report

Moon Bakery uploads its bank transaction report. The software tries to match each line.

| Line on the report | £ | In the system | Result |
|---|---:|---|---|
| Deposit | 1,150 | Sales invoice 118 to Lotus Cafe for £1,150 | Matched |
| Withdrawal | (760) | Purchase invoice from Flour Mill for £760 | Matched |
| Withdrawal | (1,470) | Purchase invoice from Sugar Co for £1,500 | **Not matched** |
| Deposit | 500 | Nothing | **Not matched** |
| Withdrawal | (2,400) | Nothing | **Not matched** |

1. The £1,470 payment is £30 less than the invoice. The accountant finds Sugar Co allowed a 2% prompt-payment discount (2% × 1,500 = £30).
2. The £500 deposit turns out to be cash banked from the day's sales.
3. The £2,400 payment is a one-off purchase of a new oven.
4. Until these are identified, the three items sit in a **suspense account** and appear on the **exception report**.

Net effect on the bank: 1,150 + 500 − 760 − 1,470 − 2,400 = **(2,980)**. All five lines changed the bank balance. The unmatched lines only need **investigation** about where the other side of the entry belongs.

### Example 5: Petty cash with an imprest float

Aziz's office has an **imprest float of £300**. During the month the petty cashier pays out vouchers for stationery £64, refreshments £48, taxi fares £35 and postage £27. A shop also gave a **£14 cash refund** for an item that was returned, and the cash went back into the box.

| Imprest working | £ |
|---|---:|
| Vouchers: 64 + 48 + 35 + 27 | 174 |
| Less cash refund received back | (14) |
| **Net spending = amount to reimburse** | **160** |
| Cash that should be in the box now: 300 − 160 | 140 |

The cashier draws **£160** from the bank to put the float back to **£300**.

*Easy way to see it:* the float is like a mobile top-up plan with a fixed limit. Each month you top it up by exactly what you used.

## 3.5 Exam traps

1. **"Is it a source document?"** Orders, delivery notes and goods received notes are **not** source documents. A **debit note** is **not**. Invoices, credit notes, the bank transaction report, cheques and the payroll **are**. If a question asks "which is NOT a source document", pick from the first list.
2. **Credit note and debit note directions.** The **seller** issues the credit note. The **buyer** may issue a debit note to ask for it. If you return goods, you will **receive** a credit note.
3. **A credit note carries VAT too.** It is a negative invoice. If you credit only the net amount, you leave VAT wrong. Customer balance after a return = invoice gross − credit note gross.
4. **Order of discounts and VAT.** Deduct the **trade discount first**. Charge VAT on the **net** amount (after trade discount). Only the price after trade discount goes into revenue or purchases. The early settlement discount is calculated on the amount after trade discount.
5. **Net pay, gross pay and the expense.** The wages expense is **gross pay plus employer's contribution**. It is **not** the net pay. When working back from net pay, add back **WHT and the employee's pension**. Do **not** add the employer's contribution to find gross pay.
6. **Who gets what.** WHT goes to the **Commissioner of Taxes**. Pension (employee's and employer's) goes to the **pension plan**. Net pay goes to the **employees**. Total payroll cost = all three added together.
7. **Bank matching.** Regular, exact payments and receipts match automatically. **Cash deposits, one-off purchases and amounts that differ from the invoice** do not. An unmatched item is not "wrong money". It is money that moved and still needs its correct account, so it is held in suspense.
8. **Imprest and system words.** The petty cash top-up is the **amount spent** (net of refunds), not the whole float. **Real-time** means "when it happens", **batch** means "grouped later". **Cloud** or **AI** does not remove source documents or manual checks.

## 3.8 Answers and explanations

### Answers: extra practice questions

**Q1. Answer: B and D**

- **Why:** A sales invoice (sent to a customer) and a credit note (received from a supplier) are source documents. They change what is owed and so are entered in the system.
- **Easy example:** The invoice and credit note are the papers that change the money owed. The others only help to check them.
- **Why not the others:** A (goods received note) only confirms the goods arrived. C (purchase order) only asks for goods. E (debit note) is a request from the buyer for a credit note; it is not recorded as a source document.

**Q2. Answer: B (£1,380)**

- **Why:** Deduct the trade discount first. VAT is charged on the net amount after the discount.
- **Formula:** List 60 × 25 = 1,500. Trade discount 20% × 1,500 = 300. Net = 1,200. VAT 15% × 1,200 = 180. Total = 1,200 + 180 = **£1,380**.
- **Easy example:** A shop first marks the price down, then adds the tax to the reduced price.
- **Why not the others:** A (£1,200) leaves out the VAT. C (£1,425) charges VAT on the full list price (£225) but takes the discount off the price. D (£1,725) forgets the trade discount.

**Q3. Answer: D (£39,400)**

- **Why:** Net pay is gross pay after the **employee's** deductions: WHT and the employee's pension. The employer's contribution is an extra cost for the employer. It is not taken from the employee.
- **Formula:** 48,000 − 6,200 − 2,400 = **£39,400**.
- **Easy example:** Your salary slip shows tax and pension coming off your pay. Your employer's own top-up to the pension is not taken from you.
- **Why not the others:** A (£41,800) deducts only the WHT and forgets the employee's pension. B (£35,800) also deducts the employer's contribution. C (£51,600) is gross pay plus the employer's contribution, which is the total payroll cost.

**Q4. Answer: C (£32,700)**

- **Why:** First work back to gross pay by adding the employee's deductions to net pay. Then add the employer's pension contribution. The expense is gross pay plus the employer's contribution.
- **Formula:** Gross pay = 25,000 + 4,100 + 1,400 = 30,500. Expense = 30,500 + 2,200 = **£32,700**.
- **Easy example:** The employer's cost is everything that leaves the business for these employees: net pay, tax and pension (including its own top-up).
- **Why not the others:** A (£27,200) is net pay plus the employer's pension, with no deductions added back. B (£30,500) is gross pay only and leaves out the employer's contribution. D (£31,300) forgets to add back the employee's pension (25,000 + 4,100 + 2,200).

**Q5. Answer: C and E**

- **Why:** The software easily matches regular, exact amounts that agree to documents it already holds. Cash deposits and payments that differ from the invoice cannot be matched.
- **Easy example:** A friend pays you the exact amount you invoiced, and that is easy to tick off. An unexplained cash deposit makes you ask, "What was this for?"
- **Why not the others:** A is a regular supplier paid the exact invoice, so it matches. B is net wages equal to the payroll, so it matches. D is a customer paying exactly the invoice, so it matches.

**Q6. Answer: A (£135)**

- **Why:** Under the imprest system the cash drawn equals the net amount spent. A refund put back into the box reduces the amount you need.
- **Formula:** 146 − 11 = **£135**. Cash left in the box = 250 − 135 = 115.
- **Easy example:** You started with a full £250. You spent £146, but £11 came back, so you only lost £135.
- **Why not the others:** B (£146) ignores the refund. C (£115) is the cash that should still be in the box. D (£157) adds the refund instead of taking it off.

**Q7. Answer: B**

- **Why:** One benefit of cloud accounting is access from any computer, tablet or phone that has internet.
- **Easy example:** Like opening your email on any phone, you can open the books from the office, home or the accountant's desk.
- **Why not the others:** A describes real-time processing, not batch. C is false: manual checks are still needed whenever AI is used. D is false: standing data does not change often, it is entered once and tightly controlled.

**Q8. Answer: A (£4,025)**

- **Why:** A credit note is a negative invoice, so it must include VAT too. The amount owed is the invoice total less the credit note total.
- **Formula:** Invoice: 4,000 + 15% (600) = 4,600. Credit note: 500 + 15% (75) = 575. Balance = 4,600 − 575 = **£4,025**.
- **Easy example:** If you return goods that included tax, the shop must give back the tax as well.
- **Why not the others:** B (£3,500) ignores VAT completely. C (£4,100) takes only the net £500 off the gross invoice. D (£5,175) adds the credit note instead of deducting it.

### Answers: true or false

| # | Answer | Reason and easy example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **False** | A delivery note is supporting paperwork only. The **invoice** is recorded. It is like a parcel slip that comes with the goods. |
| 2 | **True** | A credit note reduces what a customer owes, as an invoice in reverse, and it is a source document. |
| 3 | **True** | Real-time processing updates the system at the point of the transaction. Batch processing groups transactions and does them later. |
| 4 | **False** | Cloud accounting changes where the software and data are kept. The source documents still have to be input, by a person or automatically. |
| 5 | **True** | AI is powerful but can misread unusual items and depends on its data. A person must verify the results. |
| 6 | **False** | The expense is gross pay plus the employer's contribution. Net pay is only what the employee receives after deductions. |
| 7 | **True** | Unknown items go on an exception report, held in suspense, until the accountant identifies them. |
| 8 | **False** | Under the imprest system the top-up is the **exact amount spent**, so it changes each time. Only the float (the total after top-up) stays fixed. |
